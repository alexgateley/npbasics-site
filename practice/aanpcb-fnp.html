Question 1 · Assess

A 9-year-old with asthma takes a low-dose inhaled corticosteroid daily and albuterol as needed. Over the past 4 weeks his parent reports daytime cough or wheeze 3 days a week, one night he woke up coughing, albuterol for symptoms 3 times a week and a dose before every soccer practice. He plays soccer without having to stop. How should the NP classify his asthma symptom control?

  1. Well controlled
  2. Severe asthma needing high-dose treatment
  3. Partly controlled
  4. Uncontrolled

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Well-controlled asthma has none of the four features. He has daytime symptoms and reliever use above twice a week and a night waking.
  • B. Severity in GINA is judged by the treatment needed to achieve control, after technique and adherence are checked. Poor control on a low dose does not make asthma severe.
  • C. Partly controlled means one or two features. This is tempting if the single night waking or the pre-exercise doses are discounted, but any night waking counts, giving him three features.
  • D. Correct. He has three of the four GINA features: daytime symptoms more than twice a week, a night waking and reliever use for symptoms more than twice a week.

GINA assesses symptom control over the past 4 weeks with four questions: daytime symptoms more than twice a week, any night waking due to asthma, reliever needed for symptoms more than twice a week (doses taken before exercise are not counted) and any activity limitation. None means well controlled, one or two partly controlled and three or four uncontrolled. This child has three, so his asthma is uncontrolled even though he plays sports fully. Before stepping up treatment, the NP checks inhaler technique and spacer use, adherence, exposures such as smoke and allergens, and comorbid rhinitis, because these explain most poor control. Risk factors for exacerbations are assessed separately from symptom control.

Sources: GINA Asthma Strategy Report (2026)

Question 2 · Assess

At a 7-month well-child visit, the NP examines a girl who was born at term from a breech presentation. Her mother says the left leg seems harder to move apart during diaper changes. The left thigh has an extra skin fold, abduction of the flexed left hip stops at about 45 degrees compared with 75 degrees on the right, and the left knee sits lower than the right when both knees are flexed with the feet flat on the table. Ortolani and Barlow maneuvers are negative. Which evaluation is MOST appropriate?

  1. Hip ultrasonography and pediatric orthopedic referral
  2. Repeat hip examination at the 9-month visit
  3. Reassurance because the Ortolani and Barlow tests are negative
  4. AP pelvis radiograph and pediatric orthopedic referral

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Ultrasound is the imaging study of choice in young infants, before the femoral head ossifies; by 7 months the ossific nucleus limits ultrasound, so a radiograph is preferred.
  • B. Waiting delays treatment of a likely dislocation, and the success of closed treatment falls as the child gets older, so the hip should be imaged now.
  • C. After about 3 months, soft-tissue tightening makes a dislocated hip irreducible, so Ortolani and Barlow tests turn negative even when the hip is out; they do not exclude dysplasia at this age.
  • D. Correct. Limited abduction and a positive Galeazzi sign suggest a dislocated hip, and after about 4 months the ossifying femoral head makes a radiograph the preferred image.

Developmental dysplasia of the hip is screened for at every well visit through walking age. In the newborn, the Ortolani test reduces a dislocated hip and the Barlow test dislocates an unstable one, but after about 3 months these tests become negative as the soft tissues tighten. The most reliable signs in an older infant are limited hip abduction, a positive Galeazzi sign with the knee lower on the affected side, and asymmetric thigh or gluteal folds. Female sex, breech position and a family history are the main risk factors. Ultrasound is used in young infants, while an AP pelvis radiograph is preferred after about 4 months, and a suspected dislocation needs prompt pediatric orthopedic referral for bracing or closed reduction.

Sources: AAP Clinical Report: Evaluation and Referral for Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (2016); AAP Bright Futures Guidelines (4th ed.) and Periodicity Schedule

Question 3 · Assess

A 77-year-old woman is brought by her son, who reports 18 months of gradually worsening forgetfulness: she repeats questions, has missed bills and recently got lost driving to a familiar store. She has type 2 diabetes treated with metformin for 12 years and hypertension. She denies low mood. Her Montreal Cognitive Assessment score is 19/30, and the neurologic examination shows reduced vibration sense at both great toes but is otherwise normal. Which test is MOST appropriate to include in her initial evaluation?

  1. Amyloid PET scan of the brain
  2. Routine electroencephalogram
  3. Serum vitamin B12 level
  4. APOE genotyping to estimate her Alzheimer risk

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Amyloid PET can show Alzheimer pathology, but appropriate-use criteria reserve it for specialist evaluation of selected patients, such as candidates for anti-amyloid therapy, after reversible causes are excluded; it is not part of the initial primary care workup.
  • B. An EEG is useful when seizures, rapidly progressive dementia or a prion disease is suspected; her slow 18-month decline without episodes or myoclonus does not call for one.
  • C. Correct. B12 deficiency is a treatable contributor to cognitive impairment that guidelines include in the routine dementia workup, and her long-term metformin use and reduced vibration sense raise its likelihood.
  • D. APOE e4 raises risk but neither confirms nor excludes Alzheimer disease in a person with symptoms, so it is not recommended as a diagnostic test and does not change the initial evaluation.

The initial evaluation of suspected dementia combines history from an informant, a validated cognitive test, a depression screen, a neurologic examination, structural brain imaging and laboratory tests for reversible contributors, chiefly serum B12 and TSH, with other tests such as a metabolic panel and CBC by context. The Alzheimer's Association 2025 guideline places these first-tier tests in primary care and leaves biomarker testing such as amyloid PET to later tiers, usually with a specialist. Metformin reduces B12 absorption, and the ADA recommends periodic B12 measurement in long-term users, especially those with neuropathy. Correcting a deficiency may not reverse an established Alzheimer process, but it removes a contributor and treats her neuropathy.

Sources: Alzheimer's Association DETeCD-ADRD Guideline: Primary Care Recommendations (2025); AAN Practice Parameter: Diagnosis of Dementia (2001); ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2026); Lancet Commission on Dementia (2024)

Question 4 · Assess

A 40-year-old woman, G2P1, presents for her first prenatal visit at 9 weeks of gestation. She had gestational diabetes in her first pregnancy 3 years ago. BMI is 31. She is in a monogamous relationship and has no history of sexually transmitted infection or thyroid disease. The routine first-visit panel has been ordered, including CBC, blood type and antibody screen, rubella immunity, HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B, hepatitis C and urine culture. Which additional test is MOST appropriate at this visit?

  1. Nucleic acid amplification testing for chlamydia and gonorrhea
  2. Rectovaginal group B streptococcus culture
  3. Hemoglobin A1C or fasting plasma glucose
  4. Thyroid-stimulating hormone level

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. CDC recommends routine chlamydia and gonorrhea screening in pregnancy for those younger than 25 and older patients at increased risk. At 40 in a monogamous relationship without risk factors, she is not in the routine group.
  • B. Group B streptococcus is screened by culture at 36 0/7 to 37 6/7 weeks, so that the result reflects colonization near delivery. A culture at 9 weeks does not predict status at birth.
  • C. Correct. Prior gestational diabetes and obesity are risk factors, so she should be tested for undiagnosed diabetes at the first visit, before 15 weeks, with standard nonpregnant criteria.
  • D. TSH testing in pregnancy is targeted to women with symptoms, a personal history of thyroid disease or other specific risks. She has none, so it is not routine.

The ADA 2026 Standards recommend testing pregnant patients with risk factors for undiagnosed diabetes at the first prenatal visit, ideally before 15 weeks, using standard criteria such as A1C, because preexisting diabetes raises the risk of congenital anomalies and needs early treatment. Prior gestational diabetes carries a high recurrence risk, and her obesity adds to it. If early testing is normal, she still needs screening at 24 to 28 weeks. Other first-visit tasks for her include offering aneuploidy screening, which is offered to every patient regardless of age, and assessing preeclampsia risk for low-dose aspirin. Group B streptococcus culture belongs at 36 to 37 weeks.

Sources: ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2026); CDC STI Treatment Guidelines (2021)

Question 5 · Assess

A 72-year-old woman is sent from an assisted-living facility with a note reading "bilateral leg cellulitis." For 3 weeks both lower legs have been red and warm from the ankles to mid-calf. She has varicose veins and long-standing ankle swelling. Data are: temperature 37.0 C, heart rate 78/min, pitting edema 2+ to mid-shin, dry, scaly skin over both medial ankles. Which finding MOST strongly argues against cellulitis?

  1. Rust-brown discoloration of the lower shins
  2. Matching involvement of both legs
  3. The skin is warm to the touch
  4. Normal temperature and heart rate

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Rust-brown hemosiderin staining shows chronic venous insufficiency, which coexists with and predisposes to cellulitis, so it does not argue against infection.
  • B. Correct. Cellulitis is almost always unilateral; symmetric bilateral redness points to a mimic such as stasis dermatitis, lipodermatosclerosis or contact dermatitis.
  • C. Warmth occurs in both cellulitis and inflamed stasis dermatitis, so it does not separate them.
  • D. Many patients with uncomplicated cellulitis are afebrile with normal vital signs, so normal vital signs do not exclude it.

Pseudocellulitis is common, and studies of patients admitted for lower-extremity cellulitis find that roughly a third have another diagnosis, most often stasis dermatitis. Bacterial cellulitis is usually unilateral, acute, tender and spreading, sometimes with a portal of entry, lymphangitis or fever. Stasis dermatitis is bilateral, chronic, often itchy rather than painful, and comes with varicosities, edema, scaling and hemosiderin deposits. Recognizing the mimic avoids unneeded antibiotics; treatment is compression, leg elevation, emollients and a short course of topical corticosteroid, with reassessment if one leg becomes painful, hot and rapidly spreading.

Sources: IDSA Skin and Soft Tissue Infections (2014); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 6 · Diagnose

A 67-year-old woman, postmenopausal and not using vaginal estrogen, has had 10 days of yellow-green vaginal discharge with itching and burning on urination. She began a new sexual relationship 2 months ago. Speculum examination shows a frothy discharge and mildly pale, thin vaginal mucosa. Vaginal pH is 6.0, the whiff test is negative, and saline wet mount shows many white blood cells without clue cells, yeast or motile trichomonads. Which is the MOST appropriate next diagnostic step?

  1. A trial of vaginal estrogen for genitourinary syndrome of menopause
  2. Nucleic acid amplification testing for Trichomonas vaginalis
  3. Vaginal culture for Candida species
  4. Gram stain of vaginal fluid scored by Nugent criteria

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Atrophy raises pH and causes burning and white cells, which makes this tempting, but a frothy yellow-green discharge after a new partner points to an STI that should be excluded before attributing symptoms to atrophy.
  • B. Correct. Wet mount sensitivity for trichomonas is only about 44% to 68%, so a negative mount with a typical picture calls for NAAT, the preferred test.
  • C. Yeast culture is used for recurrent or microscopy-negative suspected candidiasis, which typically has a normal pH of 4.5 or less and a thick white discharge.
  • D. Nugent scoring diagnoses bacterial vaginosis, but a negative whiff test and absent clue cells make BV unlikely.

Trichomoniasis is the most common nonviral STI, and the CDC 2021 guidelines note its prevalence does not fall with age the way chlamydia and gonorrhea do, so older women with new partners remain at risk. Wet mount microscopy is inexpensive but misses roughly a third to over half of infections, especially if the slide is not read promptly. NAAT on a vaginal swab or urine has sensitivity and specificity above 95% and is the recommended test. If positive, she is treated with metronidazole 500 mg orally twice daily for 7 days, partners are treated, and she is retested within 3 months. Testing for other STIs and HIV should be offered.

Sources: CDC STI Treatment Guidelines (2021)

Question 7 · Diagnose

A 67-year-old man with a 45 pack-year smoking history has chronic morning cough and dyspnea when hurrying uphill. Seven months ago he was hospitalized for 3 days with a flare of cough, purulent sputum and dyspnea treated with prednisone and antibiotics. Data are: post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC 0.61, post-bronchodilator FEV1 58% of predicted, mMRC dyspnea grade 1, COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score 8. Which GOLD classification is MOST accurate?

  1. GOLD 2, group A
  2. GOLD 2, group E
  3. GOLD 2, group B because of daily cough
  4. GOLD 3, group E

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tempting because his mMRC grade and CAT score are both low, but group A requires no hospitalized exacerbation and at most one moderate exacerbation; his hospitalization moves him to group E.
  • B. Correct. An FEV1 of 50% to 79% of predicted is GOLD 2, and one exacerbation leading to hospitalization in the past year places him in group E regardless of symptom scores.
  • C. Group B is defined by more symptoms (mMRC 2 or more, or CAT 10 or more) without high exacerbation risk; chronic cough alone does not define a group, and his hospitalization outranks symptoms.
  • D. The group is right, but GOLD 3 means a post-bronchodilator FEV1 of 30% to 49% of predicted; 58% falls in the GOLD 2 range.

COPD is confirmed by a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.70 in a patient with symptoms and exposure. GOLD then grades airflow limitation by FEV1 percent predicted (GOLD 1 at least 80%, GOLD 2 50% to 79%, GOLD 3 30% to 49%, GOLD 4 below 30%) and assigns a group from exacerbation history and symptoms. Group E includes anyone with two or more moderate exacerbations or at least one exacerbation leading to hospitalization in the past year; only without that are patients split into group A (mMRC 0 to 1 and CAT below 10) or group B. The group, not the spirometric grade, guides initial inhaled therapy, so his hospitalization matters more than his low CAT score.

Sources: GOLD COPD Report (2026); ATS/ERS Spirometry Standardization (2019)

Question 8 · Diagnose

A 31-year-old woman asks to start HIV preexposure prophylaxis. Her male partner has HIV, and she does not know his viral load; they have had condomless sex several times in the past month. Two weeks ago she had 4 days of fever, sore throat and muscle aches that resolved. Her laboratory HIV-1/2 antigen/antibody combination immunoassay is reactive, and the HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation immunoassay is negative. Which is the MOST appropriate next step?

  1. HIV-1 RNA nucleic acid test
  2. Start PrEP and repeat the antigen/antibody test in 3 months
  3. CD4 count to judge whether infection is present
  4. HIV-1 Western blot to confirm the screening result

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A reactive antigen/antibody screen with a negative differentiation assay fits acute HIV infection before antibodies form, or a false-positive screen; an HIV-1 NAT tells them apart.
  • B. Starting a two-drug PrEP regimen during undiagnosed acute HIV infection can select for drug resistance; the discordant result must be resolved first.
  • C. A CD4 count stages immune function in known HIV infection and can be normal early on; it does not diagnose HIV.
  • D. The Western blot is no longer part of the recommended algorithm and, like the differentiation assay, detects antibodies that are not yet present in early infection.

The CDC laboratory algorithm begins with a fourth-generation antigen/antibody immunoassay, which detects p24 antigen before antibodies appear. A reactive screen is followed by an HIV-1/HIV-2 antibody differentiation assay; when that is negative or indeterminate, an HIV-1 nucleic acid test is done. A reactive NAT with a negative differentiation assay means acute HIV-1 infection, and a negative NAT means a false-positive screen. Her recent flu-like illness and high-risk exposure make acute infection likely, so PrEP is held until the NAT is back, and she is referred for treatment if it is positive.

Sources: CDC 2018 Quick Reference Guide: Recommended Laboratory HIV Testing Algorithm; CDC HIV PrEP Clinical Practice Guideline (2021)

Question 9 · Diagnose

A 49-year-old woman reports 6 months of hot flashes and night sweats. Her cycles, once every 28 days, now come every 35 to 70 days, and she has gone more than 60 days without a period twice this year. Her last period began 7 weeks ago, and a urine hCG is negative. She asks for a blood test to confirm that she is in menopause before discussing treatment. Which is the MOST appropriate approach to the diagnosis?

  1. Diagnose the late menopausal transition from her history
  2. Order a serum FSH; a level above 30 mIU/mL confirms menopause
  3. Order cycle-day-3 FSH and estradiol levels to stage the transition
  4. Order an anti-Mullerian hormone level to predict her final period

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. In a woman over 45, cycle changes with vasomotor symptoms establish the menopausal transition clinically; 60 days or more of amenorrhea marks the late stage.
  • B. FSH fluctuates widely during the transition and can be high one month and normal the next, so a single value neither confirms nor excludes menopause and does not change management.
  • C. Timed hormone levels are used in fertility evaluation, not to diagnose or stage menopause in a symptomatic woman of this age.
  • D. AMH falls with ovarian aging, but it is not recommended for diagnosing menopause and does not reliably predict the timing of the final period in an individual.

STRAW+10 stages reproductive aging by bleeding pattern: a persistent change of 7 days or more in cycle length marks the early transition, and an interval of 60 days or more without bleeding marks the late transition. Menopause itself is diagnosed retrospectively after 12 months of amenorrhea. ACOG notes that in women over 45 with typical symptoms, laboratory testing is not needed, because FSH and estradiol vary widely from cycle to cycle. Testing has a role in younger women with amenorrhea, where premature ovarian insufficiency, pregnancy, thyroid disease or hyperprolactinemia must be considered. She can proceed to a discussion of vasomotor treatment and still needs contraception until menopause is established.

Sources: ACOG PB 141: Management of Menopausal Symptoms (2014); STRAW+10: Staging Reproductive Aging (2012)

Question 10 · Plan

A 62-year-old man with type 2 diabetes takes metformin 1,000 mg twice daily and empagliflozin 10 mg daily. His eGFR has declined over 2 years and is now 38 mL/min/1.73 m2, confirmed on repeat testing 3 months apart. Hemoglobin A1c is 7.1%. He has no heart failure, liver disease or heavy alcohol use. Which change to his metformin is MOST appropriate?

  1. Stop metformin and start glyburide 5 mg daily to keep glucose control
  2. Reduce metformin to 500 mg twice daily
  3. Continue 1,000 mg twice daily until eGFR falls below 30
  4. Stop metformin now because the eGFR has fallen below 45

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Metformin need not stop until eGFR falls below 30, and glyburide is a long-acting sulfonylurea with active renal metabolites that raise the risk of prolonged hypoglycemia in CKD.
  • B. Correct. At an eGFR of 30 to 44, metformin is continued at a maximum of 1,000 mg per day with kidney function checked every 3 to 6 months.
  • C. Below an eGFR of 45, the total daily dose is reduced to limit drug accumulation and lactic acidosis risk; full dosing continues only at 45 or higher.
  • D. Starting metformin is not recommended at an eGFR of 30 to 45, but a patient already taking it may continue at a reduced dose; stopping is required below 30.

The metformin label contraindicates use at an eGFR below 30, advises against starting it at 30 to 45, and asks prescribers to reassess benefit and risk when eGFR falls below 45 in a patient already taking it. KDIGO and the ADA translate this into a dose of no more than 1,000 mg per day at an eGFR of 30 to 44, with kidney function checked every 3 to 6 months, vitamin B12 checked periodically, and the drug held around iodinated contrast, surgery or acute illness. His SGLT2 inhibitor is kidney-protective and continues; its glucose-lowering effect fades at low eGFR, so a GLP-1 receptor agonist is the next add-on if A1c rises.

Sources: KDIGO Clinical Practice Guideline for Diabetes Management in CKD (2022); ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2026); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 11 · Plan

A 69-year-old man with a 45 pack-year smoking history, who quit last year, was hospitalized 4 months ago for a COPD exacerbation. He uses albuterol only as needed. Post-bronchodilator spirometry now shows FEV1/FVC 0.58 and FEV1 54% of predicted. His COPD Assessment Test (CAT) score is 19, and the blood eosinophil count is 380 cells/microL. Which maintenance regimen is MOST appropriate to start?

  1. LABA plus inhaled corticosteroid combination
  2. LABA plus LAMA in a single inhaler
  3. Long-acting muscarinic antagonist alone
  4. LABA plus LAMA plus inhaled corticosteroid

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. GOLD does not encourage LABA plus ICS without a LAMA in COPD; when an ICS is indicated, it is combined with both long-acting bronchodilators.
  • B. Dual bronchodilation is the usual starting choice for group E and is tempting, but GOLD advises adding the inhaled corticosteroid at the start when eosinophils are 300 cells/microL or higher, as here.
  • C. A single long-acting bronchodilator fits group A or some group B patients; a patient with a hospitalized exacerbation and high eosinophils needs more than monotherapy.
  • D. Correct. One hospitalized exacerbation places him in GOLD group E, and with blood eosinophils of 300 cells/microL or more, GOLD supports starting triple therapy, ideally in a single inhaler.

GOLD assessment combines confirmed airflow obstruction (post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.70), the spirometric grade (FEV1 54% is GOLD 2) and the ABE group. Group E is defined by two or more moderate exacerbations or one leading to hospitalization in the past year, regardless of symptom score. Initial therapy for group E is LABA plus LAMA, with LABA plus LAMA plus ICS considered when blood eosinophils are at least 300 cells/microL, because higher eosinophils predict a reduction in exacerbations with ICS. He also needs vaccinations, pulmonary rehabilitation referral after the hospitalization, and inhaler technique review.

Sources: GOLD COPD Report (2026)

Question 12 · Plan

A 67-year-old man had a myocardial infarction treated with a drug-eluting stent 4 years ago and takes aspirin 81 mg daily, atorvastatin and metoprolol. For 3 months he has also taken over-the-counter naproxen 440 mg twice daily for back pain. Upper endoscopy for epigastric pain found a clean-based 8-mm gastric ulcer; biopsies were negative for Helicobacter pylori. Hemoglobin is 13.4 g/dL. Which plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Stop naproxen, continue aspirin, and start a proton pump inhibitor
  2. Hold aspirin for 8 weeks while famotidine 20 mg twice daily heals the ulcer
  3. Replace aspirin with clopidogrel 75 mg daily and stop naproxen
  4. Replace naproxen with celecoxib and add a proton pump inhibitor

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The naproxen is the removable cause; low-dose aspirin for secondary prevention after a stent is continued, and a proton pump inhibitor heals the ulcer and is kept long term while he stays on aspirin.
  • B. Stopping aspirin in a patient with coronary stents raises the risk of thrombotic events, and a standard-dose H2 blocker heals and protects against NSAID ulcers less well than a proton pump inhibitor.
  • C. Clopidogrel feels safer for the stomach, but trials in patients with prior aspirin-related ulcers found more recurrent ulcer bleeding on clopidogrel alone than on aspirin plus a proton pump inhibitor.
  • D. A COX-2 inhibitor loses much of its gastric advantage when taken with aspirin, and with a recent ulcer plus high cardiovascular risk the ACG advice is to avoid NSAIDs, including COX-2 inhibitors, altogether.

In an H. pylori-negative ulcer the NSAID is the cause, so the first step is to stop it and treat with a proton pump inhibitor for about 8 weeks. Low-dose aspirin for secondary prevention after myocardial infarction and stenting should not be stopped for an uncomplicated ulcer; the ACG NSAID guideline pairs ongoing aspirin with long-term proton pump inhibitor co-therapy. For patients with both high gastrointestinal and high cardiovascular risk, it advises avoiding NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors entirely. His back pain should be treated with acetaminophen, activity and physical therapy instead. If testing for H. pylori had been positive, eradication with confirmation of cure would also be required.

Sources: ACG Guideline: Prevention of NSAID-Related Ulcer Complications (2009); ACG Helicobacter pylori Guideline (2024); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 13 · Plan

A 67-year-old woman comes for a preventive visit. She smoked 1 pack per day from age 32 to 54 and has not smoked since. She has no cough, hemoptysis or weight loss, and she would accept surgery if a cancer were found. Which recommendation about lung cancer screening is MOST appropriate?

  1. One baseline low-dose CT, repeated if a nodule is seen
  2. Annual low-dose CT
  3. Annual chest radiograph with sputum cytology
  4. Screening is not advised because her history is under 30 pack-years

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A single baseline scan does not match the screening evidence; the USPSTF recommends yearly low-dose CT until she has been smoke-free for 15 years or develops a limiting health problem.
  • B. Correct. She is 50 to 80 years old, has a 22 pack-year history and quit 13 years ago, within the 15-year window, so annual low-dose CT is recommended after shared decision-making.
  • C. Chest radiography and sputum cytology did not reduce lung cancer mortality in trials and are not recommended screening tests.
  • D. The 30 pack-year threshold came from the 2013 USPSTF statement; the 2021 update lowered it to 20 pack-years, which she meets with 22.

The 2021 USPSTF statement recommends annual low-dose CT for adults aged 50 to 80 with at least a 20 pack-year history who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years (grade B). Pack-years equal packs per day times years smoked, here 1 x 22 = 22. Screening stops once a person has not smoked for 15 years, when a health problem limits life expectancy, or when the person would not accept curative treatment. She will age out of screening in about 2 years by years since quitting, and Medicare requires a shared decision-making visit before the first scan.

Sources: USPSTF Lung Cancer Screening (2021)

Question 14 · Evaluate

A 24-year-old woman was seen in urgent care yesterday for 8 hours of vague periumbilical pain with nausea. Her exam, CBC, urinalysis and urine pregnancy test were normal, and the nurse practitioner arranged a recheck in 18 hours. Today the pain is constant in the right lower quadrant and worse with walking. Data are: temperature 38.3 C (100.9 F), pulse 102/min, WBC 14,600/mm3. She has right lower quadrant guarding and a positive Rovsing sign. Pelvic exam shows no cervical motion tenderness or discharge. Which is the MOST appropriate next step?

  1. Start oral amoxicillin-clavulanate and recheck her tomorrow
  2. Repeat the CBC and abdominal exam in 24 hours
  3. Send her to the emergency department for surgical evaluation and imaging
  4. Treat for pelvic inflammatory disease with ceftriaxone and doxycycline

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Antibiotic-first management is an option only for imaging-confirmed uncomplicated appendicitis under surgical supervision; empiric outpatient antibiotics can mask perforation.
  • B. Serial exams were the right plan yesterday, but the picture has now evolved, so another delay raises the risk of perforation.
  • C. Correct. Migration of pain to the right lower quadrant with fever, leukocytosis and peritoneal signs makes appendicitis likely and needs imaging and surgical assessment the same day.
  • D. PID is a key mimic in young women, but treatment rests on cervical motion, uterine or adnexal tenderness, which she lacks.

Watchful waiting with a planned recheck is reasonable when early abdominal pain is nonspecific, but the purpose of the recheck is to catch evolution. The classic progression from periumbilical pain to localized right lower quadrant pain, then fever, tachycardia and leukocytosis, raises the Alvarado score into the high-risk range. In a woman of reproductive age, the differential includes ectopic pregnancy, ovarian torsion, ruptured cyst and PID, which is why the negative pregnancy test and benign pelvic exam matter. Imaging, usually CT or ultrasound in young women, and surgical review belong in the emergency department.

Sources: WSES Jerusalem Guidelines: Acute Appendicitis (2020 update); CDC STI Treatment Guidelines (2021)

Question 15 · Evaluate

A 70-year-old woman with chronic kidney disease stage G4 (eGFR 22 mL/min/1.73 m2) and chronic constipation started taking magnesium hydroxide 30 mL twice daily on her own 3 weeks ago. Her bowels now move daily, but her daughter reports increasing drowsiness, nausea and weakness. Data are: BP 102/64 mm Hg, pulse 54/min, respirations 14/min. She is drowsy but oriented, and patellar reflexes are diminished bilaterally. TSH was normal 2 months ago. Which is the MOST appropriate next step?

  1. Continue the laxative at half the dose and reassess in 2 weeks
  2. Switch to a sodium phosphate enema as needed
  3. Stop the magnesium hydroxide and check a serum magnesium and ECG today
  4. Repeat the TSH and free T4

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Lowering the dose still adds magnesium the kidneys cannot clear, and waiting 2 weeks ignores neurologic and cardiovascular signs that need same-day evaluation.
  • B. Phosphate enemas can cause dangerous hyperphosphatemia and hypocalcemia in advanced CKD and do not address the suspected electrolyte toxicity.
  • C. Correct. Magnesium is cleared by the kidneys, and daily magnesium laxatives in stage G4 CKD can cause hypermagnesemia, which produces drowsiness, hyporeflexia, hypotension and bradycardia.
  • D. Hypothyroidism can cause constipation, fatigue, bradycardia and slow reflexes, but a recent normal TSH and the timing after starting a magnesium product make magnesium toxicity far more likely and more urgent.

Magnesium-containing laxatives carry label warnings for people with kidney disease because reduced glomerular filtration lets magnesium accumulate. Early hypermagnesemia causes nausea, flushing and lethargy; higher levels bring loss of deep tendon reflexes, hypotension, bradycardia and eventually respiratory depression and heart block. Evaluating a new symptom after any medication change starts by asking whether the drug caused it. Stop the source, measure serum magnesium with a basic metabolic panel and obtain an ECG, and send her to the emergency department if the level is high or she worsens. For ongoing constipation in CKD, polyethylene glycol or a stimulant such as senna or bisacodyl is safer.

Sources: AGA-ACG Guideline: Pharmacological Management of Chronic Idiopathic Constipation (2023); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); KDIGO CKD Guideline (2024)
