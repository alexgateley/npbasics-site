Question 1 · Assessment

A 69-year-old man who has smoked for 45 years comes to urgent care with 3 hours of severe left flank pain radiating to the groin. He has never had a kidney stone. Data are: BP 106/64 mm Hg (usually about 145/85 mm Hg), heart rate 98/min, temperature 37.0 °C. A urine dipstick shows a trace of blood. He has never had abdominal imaging. Before attributing his pain to a kidney stone, which examination is MOST important for the NP to perform?

  1. Examination of the testes and spermatic cords for tenderness
  2. Digital rectal examination for prostate tenderness
  3. Abdominal palpation for a pulsatile, expansile mass
  4. Percussion of the left costovertebral angle

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Testicular torsion or epididymitis can refer pain to the flank and groin, but at his age, with relative hypotension and smoking history, a vascular cause is the greater threat.
  • B. A rectal examination assesses prostatitis, which causes fever and voiding symptoms with perineal pain rather than acute flank pain radiating to the groin.
  • C. Correct. An older male smoker with a first episode of apparent renal colic and a blood pressure well below his usual may have a leaking abdominal aortic aneurysm, which can cause flank pain and hematuria.
  • D. Costovertebral angle tenderness is expected with an obstructing stone, so finding it would not exclude a more dangerous cause; it adds little to his decision.

Abdominal aortic aneurysm is the most dangerous mimic of renal colic, and it is most common in men older than 65 who have smoked. A rupture or leak can track into the retroperitoneum, causing flank pain radiating to the groin and microscopic hematuria from ureteral irritation. A first episode of apparent renal colic after age 60, relative hypotension or tachycardia should prompt examination for a pulsatile abdominal mass and urgent imaging, with emergency transfer if he is unstable. A normal examination does not exclude an aneurysm, especially in obesity. The USPSTF recommends one-time ultrasound screening for men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smoked; he has never been screened.

Sources: EAU Guidelines on Urolithiasis; USPSTF Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm Screening (2019); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 2 · Assessment

Parents bring their 5-week-old son because of a cough for 6 days. His mother did not receive Tdap during pregnancy, and a 13-year-old sibling has had a cough for 3 weeks. Which history finding would MOST strongly suggest pertussis in this infant?

  1. Staccato cough and sticky eye discharge since age 3 weeks
  2. Coughing spells ending in apnea and cyanosis, with a well appearance between spells
  3. Wheezing and chest retractions after 3 days of rhinorrhea
  4. Fever to 39.2 C (102.6 F) with a barking cough

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. An afebrile infant with a staccato cough and a history of conjunctivitis suggests Chlamydia trachomatis pneumonia, a close mimic in the first months of life.
  • B. Correct. Young infants often do not whoop; instead they gag, gasp, turn cyanotic or have apnea after cough paroxysms, and look surprisingly well between episodes, usually without fever.
  • C. Rhinorrhea followed by wheeze, crackles and increased work of breathing is the typical course of viral bronchiolitis, not pertussis.
  • D. A barking cough with fever suggests croup or another febrile infection; pertussis typically causes little or no fever.

Infants too young to be fully vaccinated have the highest pertussis hospitalization and death rates. CDC notes that infants may have minimal cough, no whoop, and present with apnea, cyanosis or posttussive emesis, while older children and adolescents (like this sibling) have a prolonged paroxysmal cough. Maternal Tdap at 27 to 36 weeks of each pregnancy protects newborns until their DTaP series starts at 2 months. Suspected infant pertussis warrants nasopharyngeal PCR, prompt macrolide (azithromycin) treatment, often admission, and prophylaxis for household contacts.

Sources: CDC, Clinical Overview of Pertussis; CDC Child and Adolescent Immunization Schedule Notes (2025)

Question 3 · Assessment

A 66-year-old woman asks whether she can stop cervical cancer screening. At age 54 she had a loop electrosurgical excision procedure for CIN 3. Since then her HPV-based tests at 6 months and then yearly for 3 years were negative, followed by negative cotests every 3 years; the most recent was 2 years ago. She still has her cervix and is not immunosuppressed. Which screening recommendation is MOST appropriate?

  1. Stop screening now, because she has had more than 3 negative results in a row
  2. Stop after one more negative cotest at her next visit
  3. Continue HPV-based testing every 3 years until 25 years after treatment
  4. Switch to yearly cytology alone until age 75

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Stopping at 65 requires adequate negative prior screening and no CIN 2 or worse in the past 25 years; her CIN 3 was 12 years ago, so the usual exit rule does not apply.
  • B. A single additional negative result does not shorten the 25-year surveillance period that follows treated high-grade disease.
  • C. Correct. After treatment of CIN 2, CIN 3 or adenocarcinoma in situ, surveillance continues at 3-year intervals for at least 25 years, even past age 65, so she continues until about age 79.
  • D. Cytology alone is less sensitive than HPV-based testing, and yearly testing is not needed once she has had three consecutive negative post-treatment HPV-based results.

The USPSTF and ACS recommend stopping cervical screening after 65 only in women with adequate prior negative screening who are not at high risk. Women treated for CIN 2 or worse keep an elevated risk of cervical cancer for decades. The 2019 ASCCP risk-based guidelines call for HPV testing or cotesting at 6 months, then yearly until three consecutive negative results, then every 3 years for at least 25 years after treatment, regardless of age, as long as her health and life expectancy allow. Women who have had a hysterectomy with removal of the cervix for benign reasons can stop.

Sources: ASCCP Risk-Based Management Consensus Guidelines (2019); USPSTF Recommendation Statements

Question 4 · Diagnosis

A 9-year-old boy has had recurrent episodes of cough and wheeze with colds and during soccer practice for the past year, with nighttime cough about twice a month. He has eczema. The nurse practitioner orders testing to confirm asthma before starting controller therapy. Which result BEST confirms the diagnosis?

  1. FEV1 rises by 15% of predicted after albuterol
  2. Average daily peak flow variability of 9% over 2 weeks
  3. Fractional exhaled nitric oxide of 24 ppb
  4. Pre-bronchodilator FEV1 of 84% of predicted

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. In children, an increase in FEV1 after a bronchodilator well above the GINA threshold (more than 12% of predicted, or more than 10% under the newer ERS/ATS definition) shows the excess variability in airflow that confirms asthma.
  • B. Tempting because peak flow variability can confirm asthma, but in children the GINA threshold is an average daily diurnal variability above 13%; 9% does not meet it.
  • C. FeNO reflects type 2 airway inflammation and can support the picture, but a modest value like this neither confirms nor excludes asthma.
  • D. An FEV1 in the normal range does not show variable airflow limitation; many children with asthma have normal baseline spirometry between episodes.

GINA confirms asthma by a history of variable respiratory symptoms plus documented variable expiratory airflow limitation. In children, the evidence includes a bronchodilator increase in FEV1 above the pediatric threshold, average daily diurnal peak flow variability above 13%, or a clear rise in lung function after 4 weeks of controller treatment; a reduced FEV1/FVC (normally above 0.90 in children) on at least one occasion adds confidence. Spirometry is feasible in most children 5 years and older. Testing before controller therapy matters because inhaled corticosteroids reduce variability and can make the diagnosis harder to confirm later.

Sources: GINA Asthma Strategy Report (2026); ATS/ERS Spirometry Standardization (2019)

Question 5 · Diagnosis

A 15-month-old girl has had fever to 39.5 °C for 2 days with no cough, rhinorrhea, diarrhea or rash. She is drinking, has wet diapers and appears well between fever doses; the ear, throat and lung exams are normal. A urinalysis from a urine bag applied in the office shows positive leukocyte esterase and 25 white cells per high-power field. Which is the MOST appropriate next step in establishing the diagnosis?

  1. Start oral antibiotics and order a renal and bladder ultrasound
  2. Send the bag specimen for culture
  3. Obtain urine by catheterization for urinalysis and culture before antibiotics
  4. Order a voiding cystourethrogram after the fever resolves

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Ultrasound is recommended after a confirmed first febrile UTI in this age group, but starting antibiotics without a proper culture leaves the diagnosis unconfirmed and sterilizes later specimens.
  • B. Bag cultures are contaminated so often that a positive result cannot establish UTI; only a negative bag culture is useful, so this sample cannot confirm the diagnosis.
  • C. Correct. A bag specimen can screen, but a positive result must be confirmed by culture of urine obtained by catheterization or suprapubic aspiration before the first antibiotic dose.
  • D. A VCUG is not routine after a first febrile UTI; it is reserved for abnormal ultrasound findings or recurrent febrile UTI, and the diagnosis has not been established yet.

The AAP guideline for febrile infants and children 2 to 24 months without a source allows two pathways: obtain a catheterized or suprapubic specimen for urinalysis and culture from the start, or, in a child who does not need immediate antibiotics, screen with a urinalysis from any convenient specimen and confirm a positive screen with a properly collected culture. Diagnosis of UTI requires both pyuria or bacteriuria on urinalysis and at least 50,000 CFU/mL of a single uropathogen from a catheterized or aspirated specimen. After a confirmed first febrile UTI, a renal and bladder ultrasound is recommended, and a VCUG is deferred unless the ultrasound is abnormal or febrile UTIs recur.

Sources: AAP Febrile UTI in Children 2-24 Months (2011, reaffirmed); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 6 · Planning

An 81-year-old man has had lightheadedness on standing and two falls since terazosin 5 mg at bedtime was added for benign prostatic hyperplasia 2 months ago. He also takes amlodipine 5 mg and hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg for hypertension. Blood pressure is 138/76 mm Hg supine and 108/64 mm Hg after 3 minutes standing, with reproduction of his symptoms; heart rate rises from 70 to 78/min. An ECG is unchanged from last year. Which is the MOST appropriate initial plan?

  1. Start midodrine 2.5 mg three times daily
  2. Stop terazosin
  3. Order a 30-day event monitor and tilt-table testing
  4. Start fludrocortisone 0.1 mg daily and liberalize salt

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Midodrine is used for symptomatic orthostatic hypotension that persists after offending drugs are removed; adding it while terazosin continues treats a drug side effect with another drug and risks supine hypertension.
  • B. Correct. The symptoms began with the alpha-blocker, and removing or reducing vasoactive drugs is the first step; his BPH can be managed with a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor or a more uroselective agent if needed.
  • C. Further testing suits unexplained syncope or suspected arrhythmia. Here the bedside orthostatic readings reproduce his symptoms and the ECG is unchanged, so the cause is already identified.
  • D. Volume expansion is a later option for persistent orthostatic hypotension, but in a man with hypertension it worsens supine pressure and causes hypokalemia, and the reversible cause has not been addressed.

Orthostatic hypotension is a fall in systolic pressure of at least 20 mm Hg or diastolic of at least 10 mm Hg within 3 minutes of standing. Here the drop is 30 mm Hg with only a small heart-rate rise, a pattern common in older adults taking vasodilators. The ESC syncope guideline puts withdrawal or reduction of vasoactive drugs first, along with education, adequate fluids and slow position changes. Nonselective alpha-1 blockers such as terazosin and doxazosin are among the commonest culprits because they dilate both arterioles and veins, so stopping the new drug treats the cause. His diuretic and amlodipine should also be reviewed. Midodrine or fludrocortisone is reserved for symptoms that persist.

Sources: ESC 2018 Syncope Guidelines; AAFP: Falls in Older Adults: Approach and Prevention (2024)

Question 7 · Planning

A 6-year-old boy has 4 days of honey-colored crusted lesions around his nostrils and on his upper lip, three lesions in all, the largest 1.5 cm across. He is afebrile and otherwise well, without lymphadenopathy. No one at home has similar lesions, there is no history of MRSA in the family, and he has no drug allergies. Which treatment is MOST appropriate?

  1. Oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for 7 days to cover MRSA
  2. Mupirocin ointment twice daily for 5 days
  3. Oral cephalexin four times daily for 7 days
  4. Bacitracin ointment three times daily until healed

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. MRSA coverage is used when MRSA is suspected or confirmed; he has limited disease and no MRSA risk, and this drug covers group A strep poorly.
  • B. Correct. For a few localized lesions of nonbullous impetigo, a topical antibiotic such as mupirocin twice daily for 5 days is first-line.
  • C. Oral therapy is for numerous lesions or outbreaks affecting several people; with three small lesions it adds side effects and resistance pressure without added benefit.
  • D. Over-the-counter bacitracin is tempting, but it is less effective against Staphylococcus aureus and group A strep and is not recommended for impetigo.

Impetigo is a superficial infection caused by Staphylococcus aureus, group A streptococcus, or both. The IDSA guideline recommends topical mupirocin or retapamulin twice daily for 5 days for limited lesions, and oral therapy (cephalexin or dicloxacillin, or an MRSA-active drug when MRSA is suspected) for numerous lesions or outbreaks. Teaching includes gently washing off crusts with soap and water, covering lesions, frequent handwashing, not sharing towels, and keeping his nails short. A Gram stain and culture are reserved for treatment failure or extensive disease.

Sources: IDSA Skin and Soft Tissue Infections (2014)

Question 8 · Planning

A 40-year-old woman, G3P2, comes for her first prenatal visit at 9 weeks of gestation. She had gestational diabetes in her last pregnancy, and her BMI is 33. She feels well, and her blood pressure is 118/74 mm Hg. Standard first-visit laboratory tests are being ordered. Which plan for glucose screening is MOST appropriate?

  1. A 50-g 1-hour glucose challenge test at 24 to 28 weeks
  2. Test for diabetes at this visit
  3. A 100-g 3-hour glucose tolerance test at 28 weeks
  4. Start metformin now to prevent recurrent gestational diabetes

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. This is the routine screen for people without risk factors. Waiting would leave preexisting diabetes, which raises the risk of fetal anomalies in the first trimester, undetected for months.
  • B. Correct. Prior gestational diabetes and obesity call for early testing for undiagnosed type 2 diabetes, with standard nonpregnant criteria, at the first prenatal visit.
  • C. The 3-hour test is a diagnostic step after an abnormal challenge test in the two-step approach, not a substitute for early screening in a high-risk patient.
  • D. Metformin is not given to prevent gestational diabetes in a patient with unknown glucose status; testing comes first, and lifestyle and insulin remain the usual first steps if diabetes is found.

The ADA Standards of Care 2026 recommend testing for undiagnosed diabetes early in pregnancy, before 15 weeks, in people with risk factors, using standard diagnostic criteria (an A1C, fasting glucose or 75-g OGTT). Prior gestational diabetes and a BMI of 30 or more both raise her risk, and her age adds more. Diabetes found this early by standard criteria is treated as preexisting diabetes, which carries risks of congenital anomalies and needs early glucose control. If early testing is normal, she still needs the standard screen at 24 to 28 weeks, because gestational diabetes usually develops as insulin resistance rises in the second and third trimesters.

Sources: ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2026); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 9 · Implementation

A 66-year-old woman of Han Chinese ancestry has 2 months of seconds-long, electric-shock pains in the right cheek and jaw triggered by chewing and brushing her teeth. Neurologic examination is normal and an MRI of the brain has been ordered. The nurse practitioner plans to start carbamazepine. Which test should be done BEFORE the first dose?

  1. Baseline 12-lead ECG to measure the corrected QT interval
  2. Thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) activity level
  3. HLA-B*15:02 genotyping
  4. HLA-B*58:01 genotyping for severe skin reactions

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. An ECG is not a required pretreatment test for carbamazepine, which does not meaningfully prolong the QT interval; the label's pretreatment tests are HLA-B*15:02 in at-risk ancestry plus baseline blood counts and liver tests.
  • B. TPMT activity guides dosing of azathioprine and mercaptopurine, which are not used for trigeminal neuralgia.
  • C. Correct. The carbamazepine boxed warning calls for HLA-B*15:02 screening before treatment in patients with ancestry from populations where the allele is common, including Han Chinese, because carriers have a much higher risk of Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis.
  • D. HLA-B*58:01 predicts severe cutaneous reactions to allopurinol, not to carbamazepine.

Carbamazepine (or oxcarbazepine) is first-line drug therapy for classic trigeminal neuralgia, and MRI is advised for every patient to look for a compressing vessel or a secondary cause such as multiple sclerosis or a tumor. The carbamazepine label carries a boxed warning for serious dermatologic reactions strongly linked to HLA-B*15:02, which is common in people of Han Chinese, Thai, Malaysian, Filipino, Indonesian and South Asian ancestry; carriers should not receive carbamazepine unless the benefit clearly outweighs the risk. A second boxed warning covers aplastic anemia and agranulocytosis, so a baseline blood count is obtained. Hyponatremia is common in older adults, so sodium is monitored.

Sources: DailyMed: Tegretol (carbamazepine) prescribing information; European Academy of Neurology Guideline on Trigeminal Neuralgia (2019)

Question 10 · Implementation

A nurse practitioner refers a 72-year-old man with a changing pigmented lesion on his back to dermatology. Several referrals from the clinic were lost in the past year, and one patient's melanoma was diagnosed late. Which process change will MOST reliably close the referral loop?

  1. Mark the referral complete once the order and records are faxed
  2. Ask the patient at his next annual visit whether he saw the specialist
  3. Track each referral until the consult report is back and reviewed
  4. Give the patient the dermatology office's number so he can schedule himself

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Sending the order is only the first step; closing at that point is how referrals are lost when appointments never occur or reports never return.
  • B. Checking a year later is far too late for a possible melanoma and depends on the patient's memory.
  • C. Correct. A tracking system that keeps each referral open until the consultant's findings return and are reviewed catches missed appointments and lost reports.
  • D. Patient engagement helps, but this shifts the burden to the patient and gives the clinic no way to know whether the visit happened.

Referral breakdowns are a common source of diagnostic delay in ambulatory care. Closed-loop referral management assigns responsibility, tracks each referral from order to scheduled appointment to returned consult note, flags overdue items for follow-up and confirms the referring clinician has reviewed and acted on the result. Clear referral questions, sending relevant records and urgency labels for suspected cancer also help. Reliable systems prevent the cost and harm of late diagnosis better than reminders that rely on individual memory.

Sources: AHRQ PSNet: Handoffs and Signouts (2024); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 11 · Implementation

A 16-year-old girl comes to the clinic alone and, under a state law that lets minors consent to contraceptive care, receives her first depot medroxyprogesterone injection. The state law does not require or permit telling parents about this care without the minor's agreement. Two days later her mother, whose employer plan covers the patient, calls the nurse practitioner and asks what the visit was for. Which response by the NP is MOST appropriate?

  1. Explain the visit, because a parent is the personal representative of a minor under HIPAA
  2. Confirm the visit date and the clinician seen, but withhold the reason for the visit
  3. Share the reason for the visit, because it was billed to the mother's insurance plan
  4. Decline to discuss the visit without the patient's permission

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A parent usually is the personal representative of a minor, which is why this is tempting, but the Privacy Rule makes an exception when the minor may lawfully consent to the care without a parent.
  • B. This feels like a careful middle path, but the fact that she was seen, and when, is itself protected health information, so confirming it is a disclosure she did not agree to.
  • C. Being the policyholder does not make the mother the patient's personal representative for care the minor lawfully consented to; billing creates a confidentiality risk to plan for, not a right of access.
  • D. Correct. Because the minor lawfully consented to this care on her own, she, not her mother, controls the health information about it, and state law gives no basis for telling the parent.

Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule (45 CFR 164.502(g)(3)), a parent is normally the personal representative of a minor child, but not for care the minor may lawfully obtain on her own consent under state law. For that care the adolescent controls her protected health information, and what may be shared with a parent follows state law. Acknowledging that a visit occurred is a disclosure. The AAFP supports confidential adolescent care, and the NP should talk with adolescents in advance about how insurance statements may reveal services and whether to request confidential communications or another payment route.

Sources: HIPAA Privacy and Breach Rules, 45 CFR 164; AAFP Policy: Adolescent Health Care, Confidentiality (2025)

Question 12 · Implementation

A 69-year-old man with bipolar I disorder has been stable on lithium carbonate 300 mg twice daily for 10 years, with a serum lithium level of 0.7 mEq/L last month. He has type 2 diabetes, blood pressure of 144/88 mm Hg on repeated visits, a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 160 mg/g and an eGFR of 68 mL/min/1.73 m2. His psychiatrist agrees he needs an antihypertensive. Which plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Start losartan, which avoids the lithium interaction of ACE inhibitors
  2. Start lisinopril and check lithium level and kidney function in 5 to 7 days
  3. Start hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg daily
  4. Start lisinopril and check the lithium level at his 3-month visit

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Losartan suits his albuminuria, but angiotensin receptor blockers also reduce lithium clearance and can raise levels, so the interaction is not avoided.
  • B. Correct. Albuminuria calls for an ACE inhibitor or ARB, and because these can raise lithium levels, a level and creatinine are checked within about a week and the lithium dose adjusted as needed.
  • C. Thiazides reduce lithium clearance by about 25 to 40 percent and are a classic cause of lithium toxicity; they also do not provide the kidney protection his albuminuria needs.
  • D. Lisinopril is appropriate, but waiting 3 months leaves the rise in lithium unmonitored; levels can reach toxic range within days to weeks, especially in older adults.

With diabetes, hypertension and moderately increased albuminuria, ADA and KDIGO recommend an ACE inhibitor or ARB titrated to the highest tolerated dose. Lithium has a narrow therapeutic range and is cleared almost entirely by the kidneys, and ACE inhibitors, ARBs, thiazide diuretics and NSAIDs all reduce its clearance. The lithium label advises close monitoring of levels when these drugs are started. A practical plan is a lithium level (drawn 12 hours after a dose), creatinine and electrolytes about 5 to 7 days after starting, again after dose changes, and patient teaching on toxicity signs: worsening tremor, diarrhea, vomiting, unsteady gait and confusion. Older age and reduced eGFR increase his risk.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2026); KDIGO CKD Guideline (2024)

Question 13 · Implementation

A 48-year-old man in a long-term monogamous relationship has 2 days of fever, perineal pain, dysuria and frequency. Temperature is 38.6 C (101.5 F), heart rate 98/min and blood pressure 128/80 mm Hg. He is not toxic-appearing, tolerates fluids, and has a tender, warm, boggy prostate. Urinalysis shows pyuria and positive nitrites; a culture is sent. Local fluoroquinolone resistance in E. coli is low. Which plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once plus doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 10 days
  2. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 2 weeks, extended if symptoms persist
  3. Prostatic massage to collect secretions for culture before choosing an antibiotic
  4. Nitrofurantoin 100 mg twice daily for 5 days, then repeat urinalysis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. This regimen targets gonorrhea and chlamydia and suits younger or sexually at-risk men with prostatitis or epididymitis. His age, monogamy and nitrite-positive urine point to enteric gram-negative organisms.
  • B. Correct. A fluoroquinolone or TMP-SMX penetrates the prostate and covers E. coli; mild outpatient cases are treated for about 2 weeks, with a longer course when symptoms persist.
  • C. Massage of an acutely inflamed prostate is painful and can release bacteria into the bloodstream; the urine culture is sufficient.
  • D. Nitrofurantoin reaches therapeutic levels only in the bladder; it does not penetrate prostate tissue and is not used for prostatitis or any upper or systemic infection.

Acute bacterial prostatitis is usually caused by E. coli and other enteric gram-negative bacteria, with sexually transmitted organisms more likely in younger, sexually active men. A patient who is not septic, can take oral drugs and has reliable follow-up can be treated as an outpatient with ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, adjusted to the culture. AFP advises 10 to 14 days for mild illness with extension if symptoms continue, and European guidance uses 2 to 4 weeks, because a short course risks chronic bacterial prostatitis. Prostatic massage is avoided. Admission is needed for sepsis, urinary retention, inability to take oral therapy, or failure to improve; a prostatic abscess is suspected if fever persists after 36 to 48 hours.

Sources: AFP: Acute Bacterial Prostatitis, Diagnosis and Management (2016); CDC STI Treatment Guidelines (2021)

Question 14 · Evaluation

A 47-year-old woman found a lump in her right breast 5 weeks ago. The NP feels a 2-cm hard, irregular, fixed mass in the upper outer quadrant that has not changed since her first visit, when she was midway through her menstrual cycle. Diagnostic mammography and targeted ultrasound of the area were both read as negative (BI-RADS 1). She has no family history of breast cancer. Which step is MOST appropriate NEXT?

  1. Reexamine the breast after her next menstrual period
  2. Repeat the diagnostic ultrasound in 6 months
  3. Refer to a breast surgeon for tissue diagnosis
  4. Reassure her and resume yearly screening mammography

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Reexamining after menses helps sort cyclic nodularity in a premenopausal woman, but this mass is discrete, hard, fixed and unchanged across her cycle.
  • B. Short-interval imaging is the plan for a probably benign finding (BI-RADS 3) or a low-suspicion exam, not for a hard, fixed, irregular mass.
  • C. Correct. A clinically suspicious mass needs tissue diagnosis even when mammography and ultrasound are negative, because imaging misses a small share of palpable cancers.
  • D. Negative imaging lowers the chance of cancer but does not exclude it; returning to routine screening leaves a clinically suspicious mass without a diagnosis.

Evaluation of a palpable breast mass rests on the clinical exam, imaging and, when needed, tissue: the so-called triple test. A hard, irregular, fixed mass is suspicious on exam alone, and when the clinical and imaging findings do not agree, the clinical finding governs; mammography and ultrasound together miss a small percentage of palpable cancers. Negative imaging is reassuring only when the exam is also low suspicion, in which case clinical follow-up in a few months is reasonable. The USPSTF screening schedule (biennial mammography from age 40 to 74) applies to women without symptoms, not to the workup of a mass.

Sources: ACR Appropriateness Criteria: Palpable Breast Masses (2022 update); USPSTF: Breast Cancer Screening (2024); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 15 · Evaluation

A 29-year-old woman at 30 weeks' gestation was diagnosed with gestational diabetes 2 weeks ago and has followed medical nutrition therapy and daily walking since. Her log shows fasting glucose 98 to 106 mg/dL on 11 of 14 mornings, and 1-hour postprandial values of 118 to 136 mg/dL. Her weight gain is on track, and fetal growth on last week's ultrasound was normal. Which is the MOST appropriate adjustment?

  1. Start bedtime NPH insulin and continue nutrition therapy
  2. Start metformin 500 mg twice daily
  3. Start glyburide 2.5 mg each morning
  4. Continue nutrition therapy and recheck the log in 4 weeks

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Insulin is preferred when targets are not met with lifestyle therapy, and bedtime intermediate-acting insulin targets fasting hyperglycemia while her postprandial values are at goal.
  • B. Metformin is used when insulin cannot be, but it crosses the placenta and has uncertain long-term effects on the child, so it is not the preferred agent.
  • C. Glyburide crosses the placenta and is linked to more neonatal hypoglycemia and macrosomia; it is not a first-line agent for gestational diabetes.
  • D. Two weeks of lifestyle therapy with most fasting values above target is enough to show it is not working, and waiting a month further into the third trimester risks fetal overgrowth.

ADA targets in gestational diabetes are fasting glucose below 95 mg/dL and either 1-hour below 140 or 2-hour below 120 mg/dL. When lifestyle therapy fails to reach them, usually judged over 1 to 2 weeks, insulin is the preferred drug because it does not cross the placenta to a meaningful degree. Bedtime NPH addresses fasting hyperglycemia; mealtime rapid-acting insulin is added only if postprandial values rise. Postpartum follow-up includes a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test at 4 to 12 weeks and lifelong diabetes screening every 1 to 3 years.

Sources: ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2026); American Family Physician (AAFP)
