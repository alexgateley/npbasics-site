Otitis externa

  • Pain with tragal or pinna movement, canal edema; swimming is the top risk. Mostly Pseudomonas and S. aureus.
  • Topical antimicrobial drops, with or without a steroid, for 7-10 days; a wick for a swollen canal; scheduled acetaminophen or NSAID.
  • TM perforation or tubes: a quinolone drop only. No aminoglycoside, alcohol or acetic acid drops.
  • Oral antibiotics only for spread beyond the canal, immunocompromise, uncontrolled diabetes, or drops that cannot be placed.
Safety Diabetic or immunocompromised patient with severe pain, granulation tissue or a cranial nerve palsy: malignant otitis externa. Emergent ENT.

Try one

HEENT Primary Care practice question

A 20-month-old boy has had a cough and runny nose for 4 days and, since last night, fever to 38.6 C (101.5 F) and fussiness with tugging at his left ear. He cries throughout the otoscopic exam. Which finding on otoscopy of the left ear BEST establishes the diagnosis of acute otitis media?

  1. Diffuse redness of the tympanic membrane
  2. Moderate bulging of the tympanic membrane
  3. Reduced movement of the membrane with pneumatic insufflation
  4. An air-fluid level behind a translucent, neutral-position membrane

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Erythema alone is not diagnostic; crying, fever and attempts to remove cerumen all redden the membrane. Intense erythema counts only with mild bulging and recent ear pain.
  • B. Correct. Moderate or severe bulging of the tympanic membrane, or new otorrhea not caused by otitis externa, establishes acute otitis media under the AAP criteria.
  • C. Reduced mobility shows middle ear effusion, which is present in both acute otitis media and otitis media with effusion, so it cannot separate the two.
  • D. Fluid without bulging or signs of acute inflammation is otitis media with effusion, which is common after a cold and does not need antibiotics.

The AAP 2013 guideline requires signs of acute middle ear inflammation with effusion: moderate to severe bulging of the tympanic membrane, new otorrhea not due to otitis externa, or mild bulging with ear pain for under 48 hours or intense erythema. Bulging is the single most predictive finding. Pneumatic otoscopy confirms effusion but not acuity. Strict criteria prevent overdiagnosis of otitis media with effusion, a common source of unnecessary antibiotic use in toddlers with colds.

Sources: AAP Acute Otitis Media Guideline (2013); AFP: Otitis Media Rapid Evidence Review (2019)

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Acute otitis media in detail

The AAP definition is strict because most red ears are not AOM. A crying child's eardrum flushes, and fluid behind the drum without inflammation is otitis media with effusion, which does not need antibiotics. AOM needs evidence of middle ear effusion plus signs of acute inflammation: moderate or severe bulging of the tympanic membrane, new otorrhea that is not from otitis externa, or mild bulging with ear pain of less than 48 hours or intense erythema of the drum. Pneumatic otoscopy or tympanometry confirms the effusion.

Observation is an option, not a default, and it needs a plan: the family must be reachable in 48 to 72 hours and able to start antibiotics if the child is not better or is worse. It suits a child 6 months or older without otorrhea or severe symptoms whose AOM is unilateral, or bilateral from age 2. Children under 2 with bilateral disease, and any child with otorrhea, severe symptoms (toxic appearance, otalgia longer than 48 hours, temperature of 39 °C or more in the past 48 hours) or uncertain follow-up, get antibiotics. Treat pain as needed, whichever path is chosen.

High-dose amoxicillin (80 to 90 mg/kg/day in two doses) is first line. Amoxicillin-clavulanate covers beta-lactamase-producing H. influenzae and M. catarrhalis, so it is chosen when the child had amoxicillin in the past 30 days, has purulent conjunctivitis as well, or has failed amoxicillin. Treatment failure means no improvement or worsening within 48 to 72 hours, or AOM diagnosed again within 30 days of starting treatment. For a penicillin allergy that is not type I, the CDC lists cefdinir, cefuroxime, cefpodoxime or ceftriaxone (AFP 2019 names cefdinir or azithromycin for penicillin allergy); AFP 2019 notes a single IM dose of ceftriaxone is not first line, because options are limited if it fails.

Duration follows age and severity: 10 days for a child under 2 or with severe symptoms, and 5 to 7 days for a child 2 years or older without severe symptoms (AFP 2019). Antibiotic prophylaxis is not recommended for recurrent AOM. Consider referral to ENT after three episodes in 6 months or four in a year with one in the past 6 months; tubes are not placed for recurrent AOM if no middle ear effusion is found at the ENT evaluation. A child under 2 is rechecked at the next well visit or about 3 months after treatment to make sure the effusion has cleared.

Otitis externa in detail

About 10% of people have acute otitis externa at some point. Swimming is the commonest risk; others are eczema, psoriasis and seborrhea, earplugs and hearing aids, cerumen removal, humidity and sweating. Most cases are Pseudomonas aeruginosa or Staphylococcus aureus. Symptoms start fast, usually within 48 hours: ear pain, itching, fullness and muffled hearing. Pain on moving the tragus or pinna separates it from otitis media; pneumatic otoscopy, when the canal allows, checks the drum.

Topical drops are the treatment; no one antimicrobial drop has proved better than another. Give them for 7 to 10 days, clean debris so the drops reach the skin, and place a wick when the canal is too swollen to admit drops. Most patients improve within 24 to 72 hours; reassess if they have not, and follow up if symptoms last beyond 2 weeks. If the drum is perforated, or might be, or the patient has tubes, use only a quinolone drop (ofloxacin, or ciprofloxacin with dexamethasone), the agents approved for the middle ear, and avoid potentially ototoxic aminoglycoside and alcohol preparations. Oral antibiotics are reserved for cellulitis beyond the canal, immunocompromise, uncontrolled diabetes, or a canal that cannot be treated topically; AFP 2023 notes that 20% to 40% of patients still receive them.

Itching with thick debris and little swelling suggests otomycosis, treated with careful cleaning and acetic acid or a topical antifungal. Malignant (necrotizing) otitis externa occurs mostly in people with diabetes or immunocompromise: severe pain, granulation or necrotic tissue in the canal, a raised ESR and sometimes cranial nerve palsies. It needs emergent ENT, admission, IV antibiotics and debridement. Prevention is keeping the canal dry (well-fitted earplugs for swimming, a hair dryer on low) and not irritating it, though none of this has been tested in trials.

The red eye

Most red eyes in primary care are conjunctivitis, but a 2015 review cited in AFP 2024 found a serious noninfectious cause in about 27% of patients presenting with red eye. The screening questions are vision, pain and photophobia. Conjunctivitis leaves vision and the pupil normal and causes irritation, not deep pain. Two bedside clues help (AFP 2016): photophobia on the penlight test points to uveitis or keratitis, and a negative test makes both unlikely (negative predictive value about 90%); unequal pupils with eye pain can signal anterior uveitis, while anisocoria under 1 mm is found in up to 20% of healthy people. Check acuity in every red eye, then the pupils, then fluorescein.

Acute angle-closure glaucoma blocks aqueous outflow and raises intraocular pressure fast: severe pain, blurred vision with halos, headache, nausea and vomiting, a hazy cornea, diffuse redness, a firm, tender globe and a fixed, mid-dilated pupil of about 4 to 6 mm that reacts poorly to light. Pressures above 40 to 50 mm Hg fit the diagnosis. Pressure-lowering drops (a topical beta blocker or alpha-2 agonist) and a systemic carbonic anhydrase inhibitor may be started, with pressure rechecked every 30 to 60 minutes, but the key step is emergent ophthalmology.

Anterior uveitis (iritis) gives an aching eye, photophobia, blurred vision, a small pupil and redness concentrated at the limbus (ciliary flush). About 60% of cases are idiopathic; the rest go with HLA-B27 spondyloarthropathies, sarcoidosis, syphilis and other systemic disease, so a recurrent or bilateral case gets an RPR, chest radiograph, ESR and HLA-B27. Treatment is topical steroid given by or with ophthalmology.

Keratitis is corneal infection or inflammation: pain, photophobia, reduced vision and a staining defect. Contact lens wear is the main risk, and Pseudomonas is the organism to cover; stop the lenses, start fluoroquinolone (or aminoglycoside) drops and refer. Acanthamoeba keratitis follows poor lens hygiene or swimming in lenses; bacterial cultures are negative and it is often misdiagnosed. HSV keratitis shows a branching dendrite on fluorescein; treat with topical ganciclovir or trifluridine, refer, and never give a topical steroid. Vesicles on the tip of the nose (herpes zoster ophthalmicus) warn of eye involvement; AFP 2016 gives oral acyclovir 800 mg five times daily or valacyclovir 1,000 mg three times daily with referral.

A corneal abrasion causes sudden foreign-body sensation, tearing and blepharospasm. A topical anesthetic allows the exam but is never sent home, because it is toxic to the epithelium, delays healing and masks symptoms. Fluorescein shows a linear or geographic stain after trauma, or punctate spots that merge into a round central defect with contact lenses (a branching stain is herpes), and the upper lid is always everted to find a retained foreign body. Treat pain with oral analgesics, or topical NSAID drops for 1 to 2 days. Topical antibiotics are of unproven benefit in an uncomplicated abrasion but are commonly given; a contact lens wearer needs Pseudomonas cover (a fluoroquinolone or aminoglycoside). Patching does not help and may harm, and antibiotic-steroid combinations are contraindicated. Most abrasions heal in 24 to 48 hours; refer if symptoms worsen or have not resolved at 48 hours, or for an ulcer or infiltrate, vision worse than 20/40 or a penetrating injury.

Scleritis is severe, boring pain that radiates around the orbit and worsens with eye movement; about half of patients have rheumatologic disease (rheumatoid arthritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis), and it can threaten vision, so it is referred. Episcleritis is not painful in the same way and is milder. A subconjunctival hemorrhage is a painless, sharply bordered red patch with normal vision; it clears in a few weeks, and the INR is checked in a patient on warfarin.

Conjunctivitis in detail

No single sign separates bacterial from viral conjunctivitis. Viral and allergic forms are more common in adults; bacterial is more common in children. Mucopurulent discharge with both eyes glued shut on waking is the best pointer to bacteria. Adenovirus causes most viral cases: a watery, gritty eye, spreading to the other eye in a day or two and lasting 1 to 2 weeks. It is highly contagious by hand-to-eye contact. AFP 2010 keeps food handlers and health care workers off work until the discharge stops, and refers if symptoms have not resolved after 7 to 10 days.

Acute bacterial conjunctivitis is usually S. aureus in adults and S. pneumoniae or nontypeable H. influenzae in children, and it often resolves without treatment. Topical antibiotics modestly speed cure (a 2023 Cochrane review, cited in AFP 2024, estimated a 26% improvement), and no agent has proved superior, so choose by cost and local resistance. Delayed prescribing gives similar symptom control and halves antibiotic use. Contact lens wearers remove the lenses, get a topical antibiotic with Pseudomonas cover (a fluoroquinolone) and are seen promptly by ophthalmology (CDC, AFP 2010); refer any case that has not improved after a week of treatment.

Hyperacute conjunctivitis with copious pus is gonococcal until proved otherwise and can perforate the cornea: CDC gives ceftriaxone 1 g IM once and suggests one-time saline lavage of the eye (with infectious disease consultation considered), and the patient needs urgent ophthalmology. Chlamydial conjunctivitis is chronic and follicular in a sexually active patient who does not respond to standard drops; treat as chlamydial infection with the current CDC regimen (doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days, or azithromycin 1 g once or levofloxacin 500 mg daily for 7 days as alternatives) and evaluate and treat partners. Any conjunctivitis in a newborn is referred; CDC treats neonatal chlamydial conjunctivitis with oral erythromycin 50 mg/kg/day in 4 divided doses for 14 days, because topical antibiotics alone are inadequate.

Allergic conjunctivitis is bilateral, itchy and watery with a stringy discharge, often with allergic rhinitis. Topical antihistamines with mast cell-stabilizing activity are the standard of care; mast cell stabilizers alone take longer to work. A patient who seems to need a topical steroid needs ophthalmology first.

Pharyngitis and group A strep

Viruses cause most sore throats. Group A strep (GAS) causes about 20% to 30% in children and 5% to 15% in adults, peaks at 5 to 15 years and is rare under 3. Classic GAS is a sudden sore throat with fever and painful swallowing, tender anterior cervical nodes, tonsillar swelling or exudate and palatal petechiae; children may have headache, abdominal pain or vomiting, and a scarlatiniform rash is scarlet fever. Cough, rhinorrhea, hoarseness, oral ulcers or conjunctivitis point to a virus, and those patients need no test.

Clinical scores sort probability but do not diagnose: IDSA notes that even patients meeting every criterion are confirmed GAS only about 35% to 50% of the time. The modified Centor (McIsaac) score gives a point each for fever, absent cough, tonsillar exudate or swelling and tender anterior nodes, adds one for age 3 to 14 and subtracts one over 45. CDC tests adults and children who have two or more criteria; AFP 2016 lists empiric antibiotics as an option at a score of 4, but IDSA and CDC treat only a positive RADT or culture, and boards follow that rule. RADTs are about 95% specific and 70% to 90% sensitive, so a negative RADT in a child or adolescent is backed up with a throat culture (rheumatic fever risk), while adults need no back-up culture. Do not test asymptomatic contacts, do not retest after treatment, and do not use antistreptococcal antibody titers for acute pharyngitis.

Penicillin and amoxicillin remain first line because no penicillin resistance has been reported in GAS. Doses (CDC, IDSA): amoxicillin 50 mg/kg once daily (maximum 1,000 mg) or 25 mg/kg twice daily (maximum 500 mg) for 10 days; penicillin V 250 mg two or three times daily in children, or 250 mg four times daily or 500 mg twice daily in adolescents and adults, for 10 days; or one IM dose of benzathine penicillin G (600,000 U under 27 kg, 1,200,000 U at 27 kg or more) when adherence is doubtful. For penicillin allergy, cephalexin or cefadroxil for 10 days unless the reaction was immediate-type; otherwise clindamycin for 10 days, azithromycin for 5 days (12 mg/kg day 1, then 6 mg/kg) or clarithromycin for 10 days, knowing macrolide and clindamycin resistance varies by region. Acetaminophen or an NSAID treats pain; steroids are not recommended routinely, and aspirin is avoided in children.

Treatment shortens symptoms, limits spread and prevents complications: suppurative ones (peritonsillar and retropharyngeal abscess, cervical adenitis, mastoiditis) and nonsuppurative ones (acute rheumatic fever, poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis). Children return to school once afebrile and at least 12 hours into antibiotics (CDC allows 12 to 24 hours). A chronic carrier usually needs no treatment, and tonsillectomy is not recommended only to reduce the number of strep infections.

Acute rhinosinusitis in detail

Most acute sinusitis follows a viral upper respiratory infection and needs no antibiotic. IDSA 2012 identifies bacterial sinusitis by one of three patterns: symptoms lasting 10 days or more without improvement; a severe start, with fever of 39 °C (102 °F) or higher and purulent nasal discharge or facial pain for 3 to 4 consecutive days at onset; or double sickening, in which a cold that was improving turns worse with new fever, headache or more discharge. Imaging is not used for uncomplicated sinusitis (IDSA, CDC).

The groups differ on what to do next. IDSA starts antibiotics once the criteria are met and prefers amoxicillin-clavulanate in children and adults, with high-dose amoxicillin-clavulanate (2 g twice daily in adults, 90 mg/kg/day in children) where resistant pneumococcus is common or for age under 2 or over 65, daycare, recent antibiotics or hospitalization, immunocompromise or severe infection. The CDC adult page encourages watchful waiting in uncomplicated cases with reliable follow-up and lists amoxicillin or amoxicillin-clavulanate as first line; the CDC pediatric page allows up to 3 days of observation for persistent but not severe or worsening symptoms. Adults with penicillin allergy take doxycycline or a respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin). Macrolides and TMP-SMX are not used for empiric therapy because of high resistance rates, and an oral second- or third-generation cephalosporin alone is not recommended. Treat adults for 5 to 7 days and children for 10 to 14 days.

Saline irrigation can help adults, and an intranasal steroid helps mainly those with allergic rhinitis; IDSA advises against oral or topical decongestants and antihistamines as adjuncts. If symptoms worsen at 48 to 72 hours or fail to improve after 3 to 5 days, change the antibiotic and look for resistant organisms or another cause. Eyelid swelling with pain on eye movement or restricted eye movement suggests orbital cellulitis (AFP 2016: admission, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and ophthalmology consultation), and IDSA prefers contrast CT over MRI when a suppurative complication is suspected. A seriously ill or immunocompromised patient, or one who keeps worsening on antibiotics, is referred.

Allergic rhinitis in detail

Allergic rhinitis is a clinical diagnosis: congestion, rhinorrhea, itching or sneezing with triggers, pale swollen turbinates, clear discharge and allergic shiners. It is reasonable to start treatment without testing. Test when the diagnosis is unclear, treatment fails, or immunotherapy is planned; skin testing is more sensitive (80% to 90%) than serum specific IgE, which is more specific (80% to 100%), and serum testing is used when skin testing is contraindicated or the patient prefers it. Drugs (beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, psychotropics, rebound from topical decongestants) suggest another diagnosis, and unilateral symptoms point to a structural or other cause.

Intranasal corticosteroids are first line and work used daily or as needed; chronic use has not been shown to slow children's growth. Intranasal antihistamines (azelastine, from age 5 for seasonal and 6 for perennial use) and combination steroid-antihistamine sprays are the next options. Oral second-generation antihistamines are less effective than nasal steroids for nasal symptoms. Montelukast carries an FDA boxed warning (2020) for serious neuropsychiatric events, and FDA says to use it for allergic rhinitis only after an inadequate response to, or intolerance of, other treatments. HEPA filters and mite-proof covers have not helped in trials. Oral pseudoephedrine is avoided in the first trimester; a nasal steroid such as beclomethasone is considered low risk in pregnancy. Topical oxymetazoline is limited to 3 days, because frequent or prolonged use makes congestion return or worsen (Drug Facts label). Refer to an allergist when the diagnosis is in doubt, first- and second-line treatment fails, or the patient wants immunotherapy.

Hearing loss

Conductive loss comes from the canal or middle ear: cerumen, effusion, perforation, cholesteatoma or otosclerosis (gradual bilateral loss, often at 30 to 50 years). Sensorineural loss comes from the cochlea or nerve: presbycusis (the most common type, bilateral and high frequency, with trouble following speech in noise), noise, ototoxic drugs, Meniere disease (episodic vertigo, fluctuating loss and tinnitus) and acoustic neuroma (unilateral, slowly progressive, with tinnitus).

With a tuning fork on the forehead (Weber), sound goes to the ear with conductive loss and away from the ear with sensorineural loss. With the fork on the mastoid and then by the ear (Rinne), air conduction normally outlasts bone; bone louder than air in an ear means conductive loss there. AFP 2019 points out that both tests have variable accuracy and that audiometry is the real test, but boards still ask the patterns. The whispered voice test (positive if the patient cannot repeat at least three of six letter-number combinations) detects loss of about 30 dB with 95% sensitivity and 82% specificity.

Sudden sensorineural hearing loss, 30 dB or more over three frequencies within 72 hours with no obvious cause, is an urgent referral (ideally within a week); systemic or intratympanic steroids should start within 2 weeks of onset. Rule out cerumen and effusion first. Asymmetric loss (a difference of at least 15 dB at 3,000 Hz) needs imaging, usually MRI, for a vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma). Adults with sensorineural loss go to audiology for hearing aids; FDA allows over-the-counter aids for mild to moderate loss, though ASHA advises them only for mild loss. USPSTF gives an I statement for screening adults 50 and older without symptoms; anyone who reports hearing trouble is evaluated.

Mononucleosis

Epstein-Barr mono peaks at 15 to 24 years: fever, sore throat (exudate in about half) and lymphadenopathy, with posterior cervical nodes the most useful single sign, plus fatigue, palatal petechiae and splenomegaly. Atypical lymphocytes over 10% and lymphocytes over 40% to 50% support it, and transaminases are raised in 58% to 100% of patients. Order a CBC with differential and a heterophile antibody test; the heterophile test misses up to 25% of adults in the first week and many children under 5, when EBV viral capsid antigen IgM is the better test.

Care is supportive: rest and fluids, avoiding excess alcohol, acetaminophen and other liver-toxic drugs, and no aspirin in children and teens. Corticosteroids and antivirals are not recommended for uncomplicated mono; steroids are considered for airway obstruction or severe disease. Antibiotics are only for coexisting strep, and amoxicillin is avoided: the label warns that a high percentage of patients with mono develop a rash, and AFP 2023 puts it at 90% to 100% after amoxicillin in the 10 days before symptoms (under 5% without that exposure). Splenic injury is rare (0.1% to 0.2%) and usually nontraumatic; about 74% happen in the first 21 days and 90% by day 31, so all athletic activity stops for 3 weeks from symptom onset, followed by a graded return to noncontact activity once the patient is well and afebrile; return to contact sport is a shared decision. Some patients report fatigue for 6 months or longer.

Peritonsillar abscess and epistaxis

Peritonsillar abscess is the most common deep neck infection, usually in adults 20 to 40. Worsening one-sided throat pain with ear pain, fever, drooling, trismus and a muffled hot-potato voice, with the tonsil pushed down and in and the uvula deviated to the other side, is the picture. Needle aspiration, incision and drainage, and immediate tonsillectomy (kept for strong indications such as recurrent tonsillitis) work about equally well; imaging (contrast CT, or intraoral ultrasound to separate abscess from cellulitis) is for uncertain cases. The infection is polymicrobial (GAS and the Strep milleri group are the main aerobes, Fusobacterium necrophorum the main anaerobe), so cover streptococci and oral anaerobes for 10 to 14 days: oral options include amoxicillin-clavulanate, clindamycin, or penicillin V plus metronidazole, and clindamycin is the IV choice for penicillin allergy. Avoid macrolides (Fusobacterium resistance). Mono coexists in 1.5% to 6% (avoid amoxicillin if it is confirmed). Admit for airway concern, dehydration or inability to take fluids, treatment failure, or sepsis.

About 90% of nosebleeds are anterior, mostly from Kiesselbach plexus on the septum. Check vital signs, mental status and airway, ask about medications, then have the patient sit up, lean forward and hold firm pressure on both nostrils below the nasal bones for 10 to 15 minutes without letting go. Cotton soaked in oxymetazoline (or epinephrine 1:1,000) can slow the bleeding. In children, nose picking is the most common cause. Once bleeding slows, cauterize with silver nitrate around the site and then the site itself, never blindly; packing stays 48 hours. Bleeding down the throat despite good anterior pressure suggests a posterior source, which is more likely to need hospital care and ENT. Ask about prior bleeding and systemic causes (anticoagulants, liver disease, thrombocytopenia, leukemia), and think of cocaine use, intranasal steroids and nasal oxygen.

Cataract, macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy

Cataract causes gradual blurring, glare and halos, especially at night and when driving. Smoking and ultraviolet light speed it. Surgery (phacoemulsification with a lens implant) is the only effective treatment and is done when vision limits function; routine preoperative testing does not improve outcomes.

Age-related macular degeneration takes central vision and spares the periphery. Dry AMD shows drusen and atrophy and progresses slowly; wet (neovascular) AMD bleeds and leaks, causing sudden distortion of straight lines. Patients check an Amsler grid and report new wavy or missing lines urgently, because wet AMD is treated with intravitreal anti-VEGF injections. AREDS-formula supplements slowed progression in moderate to severe AMD (26.7% vs 35.7% over 6.3 years, NNT 11) but do not prevent it. Beta-carotene raised lung cancer risk in smokers, so AREDS2 dropped it (lutein and zeaxanthin took its place), and current or former smokers should avoid the original formula. Smoking raises the risk of AMD vision loss, so counsel smokers to quit. Glaucoma, by contrast, is silent early; peripheral field loss and then central loss appear as it advances, and the loss is permanent.

ADA 2026 sets the diabetic eye exam: a dilated exam by an ophthalmologist or optometrist within 5 years of onset in type 1 and at diagnosis in type 2; every 1 to 2 years if one or more exams show no retinopathy and glycemia is at goal; at least yearly with any retinopathy. Retinal photography with remote reading or an FDA-approved AI system is an acceptable screen if it leads to timely referral. In pregnancy with preexisting diabetes, examine before conception and in the first trimester, then each trimester and up to a year postpartum as severity requires. Refer promptly for any macular edema, moderate or worse nonproliferative retinopathy, or any proliferative retinopathy; treatment is anti-VEGF injections or panretinal laser. Retinopathy is not a reason to withhold aspirin. Rapid A1C lowering, including with GLP-1 receptor agonists, can briefly worsen retinopathy, so check retinal status when therapy is intensified.

Vision and hearing screening in children

Every newborn has a hearing screen, ideally before discharge and no later than 1 month, a full audiology evaluation by 3 months after a failed screen, and early intervention by 6 months (the 1-3-6 benchmarks). A child at risk for late-onset or progressive loss is tested at least once by 2 to 2½ years. The Bright Futures schedule then calls for audiometry at 4, 5, 6, 8 and 10 years and once in each of the 11-14, 15-17 and 18-21 age bands, adding 6,000 and 8,000 Hz to catch noise-induced loss; other visits are risk assessments.

Amblyopia is the target of early vision screening, because it does not resolve on its own and treatment works less well with age. Its risk factors are strabismus, high refractive error or a difference between the eyes, and media opacity such as cataract; the red reflex checks for the last. USPSTF (2017, update in progress) gives a B to screening at least once between 3 and 5 years with acuity charts (Lea, HOTV), stereoacuity, cover-uncover and corneal light reflex tests or an instrument-based screener, and an I under age 3. Bright Futures checks acuity at 4 and 5 years and in cooperative 3-year-olds, then at 6, 8, 10, 12 and 15, and allows instrument-based screening at 12 and 24 months and at 3 to 5 years. Any positive screen goes to a full eye exam.
