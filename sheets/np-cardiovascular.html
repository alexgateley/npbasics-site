Blood pressure: measure, confirm, diagnose

  1. Prepare: no caffeine, exercise or smoking for 30 minutes, empty bladder, seated with back supported and feet flat, 5 minutes of quiet rest.
  2. Measure: validated automated device, right-sized cuff on a bare arm at heart level, no talking. Average two or more readings 1 minute apart (or automated office BP: three readings after a 5-minute wait).
  3. Confirm outside the office before treating: home or ambulatory monitoring.
  4. Diagnose on the average of readings on separate occasions, not one visit.
The out-of-office lines are ESC 2024's equivalents of an office 140/90, not of the US office 130/80.

Try one

Cardiovascular Primary Care practice question

A 62-year-old man has newly diagnosed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. His only known condition is hypertension, treated with amlodipine. Which additional history finding would MOST increase his CHA2DS2-VASc stroke-risk score?

  1. A transient ischemic attack last year
  2. Drinking 10 or more alcoholic drinks a week
  3. Type 2 diabetes treated with metformin
  4. An eGFR of 52 mL/min/1.73 m2

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Prior stroke, TIA or thromboembolism adds 2 points, raising his score from 1 to 3 and making oral anticoagulation clearly indicated.
  • B. Heavy alcohol use counts toward bleeding risk in the HAS-BLED score, not toward the stroke-risk score.
  • C. Diabetes is a scored item and adds 1 point, raising his score to 2, but a prior TIA adds 2 points and so raises it more.
  • D. Chronic kidney disease affects anticoagulant dosing and bleeding risk, but it is not scored in CHA2DS2-VASc.

Stroke risk in atrial fibrillation is scored the same whether the pattern is paroxysmal or persistent. CHA2DS2-VASc gives 1 point each for heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, vascular disease, age 65 to 74 and female sex, and 2 points each for age 75 or older and prior stroke, TIA or thromboembolism; ESC 2024 uses CHA2DS2-VA, which drops the sex point. With hypertension alone this man scores 1, where anticoagulation is a shared decision; at 2 or more it is recommended. A history of TIA must be asked about directly, because patients often do not volunteer brief resolved symptoms.

Sources: ESC Atrial Fibrillation Guideline (2024)

Resistant hypertension

  1. Definition: above goal on maximally tolerated thiazide-type diuretic, CCB and ACEi or ARB.
  2. Rule out pseudo-resistance first: adherence, technique and cuff size, white-coat effect (home or ambulatory readings).
  3. Remove raisers: NSAIDs, decongestants, licorice and other supplements, alcohol, stimulants.
  4. Look for secondary causes: primary aldosteronism (aldosterone-to-renin ratio), sleep apnea, kidney disease, renal artery stenosis.
  5. Fourth drug: spironolactone (amiloride if not tolerated). Check potassium and creatinine.
  6. Refer to a hypertension specialist if still uncontrolled.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Measuring and confirming blood pressure

Most office readings are taken badly, and a poor technique can move the number by more than the difference between two treatment thresholds. The preparation and technique on the one-page section follow the AFP summary of the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline and ESC 2024: no caffeine, exercise or smoking for at least 30 minutes, an empty bladder, the patient seated with the back supported, feet flat and legs uncrossed for more than 5 minutes, a validated oscillometric device, a correctly sized cuff on a bare, supported arm at heart level, no talking and no phone, and at least two readings 1 minute apart. ESC notes that a cuff that is too small reads falsely high and one that is too large reads falsely low. VA/DoD 2026 prefers fully automated office BP, programmed to wait 5 minutes and then average three readings at least 30 seconds apart.

USPSTF (2021, grade A) recommends screening all adults 18 and older and confirming the diagnosis with measurements outside the clinic before starting treatment, using ambulatory or home monitoring with a validated device. It suggests yearly screening from age 40 and for people at higher risk (high-normal readings, overweight or obesity, Black adults), and every 3 to 5 years for adults 18 to 39 at lower risk with a prior normal reading. For home monitoring, the AFP summary describes two readings in the morning before eating and two before bed; home readings are more reproducible than office readings and can guide titration.

Where the diagnostic line falls depends on the guideline. The usual US threshold is 130/80 (AHA/ACC 2025, through AFP; ADA 2026 for people with diabetes). VA/DoD 2026 makes the diagnosis when a follow-up office reading is above 130 systolic or above 90 diastolic. ESC 2024 calls 120–139/70–89 'elevated' and 140/90 or higher hypertension, with home or daytime ambulatory 135/85, 24-hour 130/80 and night-time 120/70 as the out-of-office equivalents. The differences matter less than the method: confirm out of office, and judge white-coat and masked hypertension by the home or ambulatory numbers. VA/DoD suggests home or ambulatory monitoring when office BP is normal but organ damage is present, which can be masked hypertension.

At the first visit look for target-organ damage (stroke, MI, PAD, left ventricular hypertrophy, heart failure, CKD, retinopathy) and the conditions that travel with hypertension (CKD, dyslipidemia, diabetes, obesity, sleep apnea, nicotine use), as VA/DoD lists them. Check orthostatic BP when a treated patient reports dizziness or light-headedness, and in older adults: ESC defines orthostatic hypotension as a fall of 20/10 or more at 1 and/or 3 minutes after standing from rest.

Treating hypertension

Lifestyle change comes first for everyone and continues with drugs: a DASH-style diet with less than 2,300 mg of sodium a day plus more than 120 minutes a week of physical activity (VA/DoD strong recommendation), weight loss in overweight or obesity (VA/DoD recommends GLP-1 receptor agonist-based drugs for weight loss in hypertension with overweight or obesity), less alcohol and no tobacco. KDIGO suggests sodium below 2 g a day and at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity in CKD. In VA/DoD's summary table, the DASH diet at under 2,300 mg sodium lowers BP by about 6/3, and a single drug by about 8–10 systolic.

When to start drugs. The AFP summary of the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline gives drug treatment for an average of 140/90 or higher in everyone, and from 130 systolic in people with cardiovascular risk factors or when lifestyle change has not worked. ADA starts and titrates drugs at a confirmed 130/80 in diabetes. ESC treats elevated BP when 10-year cardiovascular risk is 10% or more or the patient has CKD, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, organ damage or familial hypercholesterolemia.

Targets. The usual US target is below 130/80: AHA/ACC (with below 120/80 encouraged), ADA (below 130/80 if safely reached; below 120 systolic encouraged at high cardiovascular or kidney risk) and VA/DoD (systolic below 130, diastolic below 90 from age 30). AAFP's own 2022 guideline targets below 140/90 and calls 135/85 an option for MI risk; AAFP gave the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline an Affirmation of Value rather than endorsement, in part because its universal 130/80 target does not match. KDIGO suggests systolic below 120 in CKD when tolerated, measured with standardized office technique. ESC aims for 120–129 systolic if tolerated, with more lenient targets for symptomatic orthostatic hypotension, age 85 or older, moderate-to-severe frailty or limited life expectancy.

First drugs. Thiazide-type diuretics, long-acting dihydropyridine CCBs, and ACE inhibitors or ARBs are the first-line classes (VA/DoD strong recommendation; ESC). VA/DoD suggests a thiazide-type diuretic at 65 and older, with electrolytes checked 1–4 weeks after starting; chlorthalidone 12.5–25 mg and hydrochlorothiazide 12.5–50 mg are its usual ranges, and its table notes gout and low potassium. The AFP summary of AHA/ACC 2025 favors starting most patients on two drugs, an ACEi or ARB with a thiazide-type diuretic or a long-acting dihydropyridine CCB; VA/DoD finds insufficient evidence that starting with two improves outcomes but says to consider it when BP is more than 20/10 above goal. VA/DoD's drug table also notes ankle edema, flushing and headache with dihydropyridine CCBs, no ACEi after angioedema, and pausing an ACEi or ARB to look for a cause (such as renal artery stenosis) if kidney function falls by more than about 30%. VA/DoD notes that doubling one drug lowers systolic BP only about 1–2.6 more, while adding a second class lowers it about 9/5 more. Beta-blockers are not first-line for uncomplicated hypertension; ESC keeps them for angina, heart failure, post-MI and rate control.

Comorbidities. ADA makes an ACEi or ARB first line in diabetes with albuminuria or coronary disease and strongly recommends one at the highest tolerated dose for a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 300 mg/g or more or an eGFR below 60. KDIGO recommends an ACEi or ARB in CKD with severely increased albuminuria (A3) and suggests it at A2 without diabetes; with diabetes it recommends one at A2 and A3. Monitor potassium and creatinine with an ACEi, ARB or MRA. Never combine an ACEi, an ARB and a direct renin inhibitor (VA/DoD, KDIGO, ADA). ADA advises avoiding ACE inhibitors, ARBs, MRAs, direct renin inhibitors and neprilysin inhibitors in people who could become pregnant and are not using reliable contraception; they are contraindicated in pregnancy.

Race. Older US guidance advised a thiazide or CCB rather than ACEi or ARB monotherapy in Black patients. The 2026 VA/DoD guideline lists that recommendation as deleted, and ESC does not use race. Board items written to older standards may still expect a thiazide or CCB first in a Black patient without CKD or albuminuria; with CKD and albuminuria, an ACEi or ARB is right whatever the patient's race.

Very high readings. In the AFP summary of the AHA acute-care statement, a hypertensive emergency is systolic above 180 and diastolic above 110 with new or worsening target-organ damage (brain, arteries, retina, kidney, heart), and it is treated in the hospital by lowering BP quickly to an organ-specific goal. The same readings without organ damage are often due to measurement problems or temporary factors, frequently need no acute treatment, and are best handled by starting or adjusting oral drugs in primary care. The statement discourages the terms 'urgency' and 'crisis'.

Resistant hypertension

ESC defines resistant hypertension as BP that stays at or above 140/90 despite lifestyle measures and maximum or maximally tolerated doses of a thiazide or thiazide-like diuretic, a RAS blocker and a CCB, confirmed by out-of-office readings, once poor adherence and the white-coat effect are excluded; with eGFR below 30, a loop diuretic replaces the thiazide. VA/DoD uses the same three-drug definition against the patient's goal.

Before adding a fourth drug, VA/DoD asks the clinician to check adherence (refill history, home log, interview), look for interfering substances (supplements and teas such as licorice root, prescription and over-the-counter drugs, NSAIDs, alcohol, recreational drugs), address lifestyle factors, and re-examine the diagnosis, including secondary causes. Screen for primary aldosteronism: the AFP summary of AHA/ACC 2025 recommends it in resistant hypertension, and ESC names the aldosterone-to-renin ratio as the test (long-acting CCBs and alpha-blockers do not interfere with it). Sleep apnea is listed by VA/DoD as a co-occurring condition to address. In renal artery stenosis, VA/DoD recommends drugs over angioplasty or stenting.

The fourth drug is spironolactone: ESC calls it the most effective add-on in resistant hypertension, VA/DoD suggests spironolactone or amiloride, and ADA calls MRAs effective for resistant hypertension in type 2 diabetes. The Aldactone label gives 25–100 mg a day for hypertension and asks for potassium within 1 week of starting or titrating. Refer to a hypertension specialist when BP stays uncontrolled (VA/DoD).

Lipids: who gets a statin and how strong

Statin intensity is defined by expected LDL lowering: high intensity lowers LDL by 50% or more (atorvastatin 40–80 mg, rosuvastatin 20–40 mg) and moderate intensity by 30–49% (atorvastatin 10–20, rosuvastatin 5–10, simvastatin 20–40, pravastatin 40–80, lovastatin 40, fluvastatin XL 80, pitavastatin 1–4 mg), per ADA 2026 Table 10.1; the VA/DoD 2025 drug table lists the same high-intensity doses.

Secondary prevention. With clinical ASCVD, ADA recommends a high-intensity statin, a 50% or greater LDL reduction and an LDL goal below 55 mg/dL in diabetes, adding ezetimibe or a PCSK9 inhibitor if the goal is not met. VA/DoD suggests a high-intensity statin, or a moderate-intensity statin with ezetimibe or a PCSK9 inhibitor, and combination therapy for very high-risk patients: MI or ACS in the past 12 months, recurrent events, or ASCVD with LDL 70 or higher on therapy. VA/DoD finds insufficient evidence for a treat-to-target LDL strategy over fixed-dose high-intensity therapy, and suggests continuing treatment when LDL falls below 30. AFP 2018 gives a high-intensity statin to everyone under 75 with stable coronary disease, moderate intensity at 75 and older.

Primary prevention. USPSTF 2022 recommends a statin (grade B, moderate intensity) for adults 40–75 with at least one risk factor (dyslipidemia, diabetes, hypertension or smoking) and a 10-year risk of 10% or more, a selective offer (grade C) at 7.5% to below 10%, and finds insufficient evidence to start one at 76 or older; it uses the Pooled Cohort Equations and does not apply to LDL above 190 or familial hypercholesterolemia. VA/DoD 2025 suggests the PREVENT calculator instead, recommends at least a moderate-intensity statin for diabetes, LDL 190 or higher, or a 10-year risk of 10% or more, and suggests one at about 5% to below 10%. ADA recommends a moderate-intensity statin for everyone with diabetes aged 40–75, and a high-intensity statin with an LDL goal below 70 for those at higher risk.

Monitoring and intolerance. ADA checks lipids at diagnosis, 4–12 weeks after starting or changing a statin, and then yearly. VA/DoD suggests using a statin as indicated when baseline ALT or AST is below 3 times the upper limit of normal. For intolerance, VA/DoD suggests a washout and rechallenge with the same or a different statin or a lower dose, then nondaily dosing, then a nonstatin (bempedoic acid, ezetimibe, a fibrate or a PCSK9 antibody). Coronary artery calcium can refine an intermediate or high estimate when the result would change the decision, and lipoprotein(a) identifies extra risk (VA/DoD). Both ADA and VA/DoD advise against adding fibrates to statins for event reduction; ADA and VA/DoD support icosapent ethyl in ASCVD with triglycerides of 150 or more on a statin. With triglycerides of 500 or more, ADA asks for secondary causes and treatment to lower pancreatitis risk. Statins are generally stopped before conception (ADA).

Aspirin. USPSTF 2022 makes starting low-dose aspirin for primary prevention an individual decision at 40–59 with a 10-year risk of 10% or more (grade C) and recommends against starting it at 60 or older (grade D). For secondary prevention, aspirin 75–162 mg a day is standard, with clopidogrel 75 mg if aspirin cannot be taken (ADA; AFP 2018).

Chest pain and stable angina

The first office decision is whether the patient needs the emergency department now. FP Essentials 562 (2026) asks for immediate vital signs and a 12-lead ECG, ideally within 10 minutes, emergency treatment of instability and hypoxia, and EMS transport for anyone with clinical evidence of ACS or another life-threatening cause. AFP 2020 lists ST changes, new left bundle branch block, Q waves and new T-wave inversion as ECG findings that raise the likelihood of ACS. No single symptom rules ACS in or out, but combinations help: pressure-like or exertional pain, radiation to one or both arms or shoulders, nausea or sweating and pain worse than prior angina raise it; pleuritic, positional or palpation-reproduced pain lowers it. Dissection (an arm-to-arm pulse difference raises the likelihood) and PE (Wells and PERC) are the other causes that cannot wait.

For stable chest pain, AFP 2020 describes two primary-care rules. The Marburg Heart Score gives a point each for age (woman 65 or older, man 55 or older), known vascular disease, pain that increases with exercise, pain not reproduced by palpation and the patient's belief that it is cardiac; at a 10% pretest probability, scores of 0–1, 2–3 and 4–5 carried about 0.4%, 9.3% and 55.5% probability of coronary disease. Low scores identify patients who need no further testing. In US practice, a pretest probability below 5% needs no test, 5–70% gets noninvasive testing and above 70% invasive angiography (AFP 2020).

ESC 2024 describes typical angina as retrosternal discomfort brought on by exertion or emotional stress and relieved by rest or nitroglycerin, but notes that only 10–25% of patients with suspected chronic coronary syndrome have this classic picture and recommends describing symptoms rather than labeling them typical or atypical. Coronary CT angiography is preferred as the first test when the likelihood of obstructive disease is low to moderate, and functional imaging when ischemia or viability needs to be shown. Exercise ECG is mainly a prognostic test; ESC cites a sensitivity of 58% and specificity of 62% for obstructive disease. Recent-onset or changing symptoms belong on the acute pathway.

Treatment of stable angina (AFP 2018, Braun) has two jobs. To prevent events: daily low-dose aspirin (75–162 mg works as well as 325 mg with less bleeding; 81 mg for most), clopidogrel when aspirin cannot be used, a high-intensity statin under 75, and an ACE inhibitor with hypertension, diabetes, CKD, low EF or recent MI. To control symptoms: a beta-blocker first, a long-acting CCB (amlodipine or extended-release nifedipine, never short-acting nifedipine) when a beta-blocker fails or is not tolerated, and a long-acting nitrate or ranolazine as add-ons. Ranolazine does not lower BP or heart rate; it is contraindicated in liver impairment and with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors and needs care with QT-prolonging drugs. Lifestyle change, 30–60 minutes of moderate activity on 5–7 days a week, supervised exercise for high-risk patients and a yearly influenza vaccine complete the plan. ESC 2024 notes that most patients with stable disease and preserved LV function start with optimized medical therapy; revascularization is for refractory symptoms or high-risk anatomy.

Sublingual nitroglycerin, per the Nitrostat label: one tablet under the tongue at the first sign of an attack, sitting; another every 5 minutes if needed, no more than three in 15 minutes, and prompt medical attention if pain persists after three or is different from usual. It can be taken 5–10 minutes before an activity that brings on angina. Keep it in the original glass bottle, tightly capped. It is contraindicated with PDE-5 inhibitors and riociguat (severe hypotension), severe anemia, raised intracranial pressure and shock.

Heart failure with reduced and preserved EF

ESC 2021 classifies heart failure by EF: reduced at 40% or less, mildly reduced at 41–49%, and preserved at 50% or more, the last needing objective evidence of structural or functional abnormality consistent with diastolic dysfunction or raised filling pressure, including raised natriuretic peptides. Echocardiography gives the EF and the cause.

HFrEF has four disease-modifying classes: an angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (or an ACEi or ARB), an evidence-based beta-blocker, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist and an SGLT2 inhibitor (AFP 2025, Gauer). AFP recommends titrating them to target doses within 6–12 weeks of diagnosis and adjusting for hypotension, high potassium or worsening kidney function. Diuretics relieve congestion and reduce readmissions. Two large trials of SGLT2 inhibitors added to optimal therapy cut worsening heart failure or cardiovascular death by 25% and 26%, with or without diabetes. Second-line drugs for persistent symptoms include digoxin, hydralazine with isosorbide dinitrate, ivabradine and vericiguat. IV iron is reasonable with iron deficiency (ESC 2023 defines it as ferritin below 100 or transferrin saturation below 20%).

Sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto label): start 49/51 mg twice daily, doubling every 2–4 weeks to 97/103 mg twice daily; start at half that dose (24/26 mg) in patients not on an ACEi or ARB (or on low doses), with eGFR below 30, or with moderate liver impairment. It is contraindicated with an ACE inhibitor (36 hours apart when switching either way), with a history of angioedema on an ACEi or ARB, and with aliskiren in diabetes, and it carries a boxed warning for fetal toxicity. Monitor potassium, creatinine and BP.

Spironolactone (Aldactone label): in heart failure start 25 mg once daily if potassium is 5.0 or less and eGFR is above 50; consider 25 mg every other day with eGFR 30–50. Check potassium within 1 week of starting or changing the dose and regularly after; reduce or stop for hyperkalemia. It is contraindicated with hyperkalemia, Addison's disease and eplerenone. About 9% of men in RALES developed gynecomastia, usually reversible. Beta-blockers in HFrEF are carvedilol or metoprolol succinate (AFP 2018).

Drugs that harm. AFP 2018 advises against CCBs in abnormal LV function, and the VA/DoD 2026 drug table lists systolic heart failure and reduced LV function as contraindications to verapamil; diltiazem and verapamil are therefore avoided in HFrEF. The Actos label contraindicates starting pioglitazone in NYHA class III or IV heart failure. ADA allows metformin in stable heart failure while eGFR stays above 30. NSAIDs raise BP and are among the interfering substances VA/DoD lists.

HFpEF. The ESC 2023 focused update extended SGLT2 inhibitor recommendations to mildly reduced and preserved EF on the strength of EMPEROR-Preserved and DELIVER, and ADA 2026 recommends an SGLT2 inhibitor in type 2 diabetes with either HFrEF or HFpEF. In type 2 diabetes with obesity and symptomatic HFpEF, ADA recommends a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist or a GLP-1 receptor agonist. The rest is treating congestion with diuretics and treating the drivers: hypertension, AF, obesity, diabetes and coronary disease. ESC 2023 also recommends close follow-up in the first 6 weeks after a heart failure hospitalization.

Atrial fibrillation

Confirm AF on a 12-lead ECG or another recording. AFP 2024 (Holder) asks for a transthoracic echocardiogram, electrolytes, CBC, and thyroid, kidney and liver tests at the start. Hemodynamically unstable patients need urgent cardioversion; stable patients with new AF start with rate control and anticoagulation, and rhythm control is considered for younger patients, recent onset, reduced EF or symptoms despite rate control. Catheter ablation may be first-line rhythm control in younger patients with paroxysmal AF and few comorbidities. USPSTF (2022) finds insufficient evidence to screen asymptomatic adults 50 and older.

Rate control (AFP 2016): a beta-blocker or a nondihydropyridine CCB (diltiazem or verapamil); digoxin is no longer first line or used alone but can be added. Beta-blockers and CCBs are contraindicated with pre-excitation (Wolff-Parkinson-White). Lenient control to a resting rate below 110 is reasonable for most patients; stricter control below 80 is for persistent symptoms. Avoid diltiazem and verapamil when EF is reduced (see heart failure).

Stroke prevention. CHA2DS2-VASc scores CHF, hypertension, age 75 or older (2), diabetes, prior stroke, TIA or thromboembolism (2), vascular disease, age 65–74 and female sex (AFP 2024). AFP 2019 recommends anticoagulation at 2 or more in men and 3 or more in women, and consideration at 1 in men and 2 in women. ESC 2024 replaces it with CHA2DS2-VA, which drops sex because female sex modifies risk with age rather than adding it, and recommends anticoagulation at 2 or more and consideration at 1. Antiplatelet drugs are not recommended for stroke prevention in AF (ESC 2024).

Choice of drug. DOACs are preferred: less major bleeding than warfarin (AFP 2024) and about half the intracranial bleeding (ESC 2024). Warfarin remains the drug for a mechanical heart valve or moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis. With warfarin, consider switching to a DOAC if the INR is in the 2–3 range less than 70% of the time (AFP 2019). ESC 2024 warns that inappropriate DOAC dose reductions are common: give the full dose unless the label's criteria are met. Apixaban (Eliquis label) is 5 mg twice daily for AF, reduced to 2.5 mg twice daily only with at least two of age 80 or older, weight 60 kg or less, or creatinine 1.5 mg/dL or higher; it is not recommended with prosthetic valves or triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome, and its boxed warning notes higher thrombotic risk if it is stopped early. AFP 2019 asks for a HAS-BLED score at each visit, with modifiable risks (alcohol, anemia, aspirin or NSAID use) addressed; a score of 3 or more is high risk (AFP 2016).

Murmurs and aortic stenosis

Most adult murmurs are not serious, and the job in the office is to find the ones that are. AFP 2016 (Grimard) recommends transthoracic echocardiography for a loud unexplained systolic murmur, a single second heart sound, a history of bicuspid valve, or symptoms that could come from aortic stenosis, and again when the exam changes. Only a normally split S2 excludes severe aortic stenosis on examination. Diastolic murmurs come from aortic regurgitation or mitral stenosis (AFP 2001), so they point to valve disease and get an echo; AFP 2001 names echo as the study of choice for mitral stenosis.

Aortic stenosis is classically a harsh, late-peaking systolic murmur that radiates to the carotids; in older patients it can be softer and radiate to the apex, and a soft murmur does not exclude it as the LV fails. Its symptoms are dyspnea and other heart failure symptoms, angina and syncope, and in older adults sometimes only reduced exercise tolerance. Once symptoms appear, severe stenosis needs prompt valve replacement, the only treatment that improves survival; refer to cardiology. Replacement is also recommended for asymptomatic severe stenosis with an EF below 50%. Surveillance echo for asymptomatic disease: severe every 6–12 months, moderate every 1–2 years, mild every 3–5 years. Start vasodilators and diuretics cautiously and avoid peripheral alpha-blockers, which can cause syncope. Endocarditis prophylaxis is not recommended for aortic stenosis unless the patient has a prosthetic valve or prior endocarditis.

Maneuvers. Squatting increases venous return and makes most murmurs louder; standing and Valsalva do the opposite. A murmur that softens with squatting, or grows louder or longer on standing or with Valsalva, suggests hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or mitral valve prolapse (AFP 2007), and an athlete with that finding needs evaluation before playing. Aortic stenosis softens with Valsalva; aortic regurgitation grows louder with handgrip or squatting; in mitral valve prolapse, squatting moves the click toward S2 (AFP 2001).

Peripheral artery disease

Ask at-risk patients about exertional leg symptoms, rest pain and nonhealing wounds, and examine pulses and listen for femoral bruits (AFP 2019, Firnhaber). Classic claudication is the minority (about 10% of patients with PAD); about half have atypical leg symptoms and about 40% none. Do a resting ABI when the history or exam suggests PAD, and an exercise ABI when the resting value is normal but exertional symptoms persist. USPSTF (2018) finds insufficient evidence to screen asymptomatic adults with the ABI; ADA recommends ABI screening in asymptomatic people with diabetes who are 65 or older or have microvascular disease, foot complications or other end-organ damage, when a PAD diagnosis would change management.

ABI categories, per the AFP 2025 summary: 0.90 or less is abnormal, 0.91–0.99 borderline, 1.00–1.40 normal, and above 1.40 noncompressible, which calls for a toe-brachial index (0.70 or less is diagnostic). ABI and TBI can be normal or borderline in at least 25% of patients with chronic limb-threatening ischemia, so a normal number does not clear a patient with rest pain or a wound. Duplex, CT or MR angiography is for an inconclusive ABI or for planning revascularization.

Treatment lowers cardiovascular risk and improves walking. Stop smoking. Structured exercise at least three times a week for 12 weeks (AFP 2025) is first-line for claudication. Give a statin (high-intensity atorvastatin improves pain-free walking, AFP 2019), an ACEi or ARB, and a single antiplatelet: aspirin 75–325 mg or clopidogrel 75 mg (AFP 2019), with clopidogrel favored in the AFP summary of the 2024 guideline. Dual antiplatelet therapy adds bleeding without benefit in stable PAD. Rivaroxaban 2.5 mg twice daily with low-dose aspirin cut major cardiac and limb events in coronary disease with symptomatic PAD at the cost of about 1% more major bleeding (AFP 2019; ADA gives the same combination). Cilostazol improves claudication and is contraindicated in heart failure; pentoxifylline is not recommended.

Refer urgently for chronic limb-threatening ischemia: ischemic rest pain for more than 2 weeks (often at night, eased by hanging the foot over the bed), ischemic wounds or gangrene. Acute limb ischemia, a cold, painful, pale limb with weak or absent pulses, weakness and sensory loss, is an emergency: muscle tolerates ischemia for only about 4–6 hours (AFP 2019).

Deep vein thrombosis

The Wells DVT score (AFP 2019, Pyzocha) gives a point each for active cancer within 6 months, calf swelling 3 cm or more beyond the other leg, collateral superficial veins, paralysis, paresis or recent leg immobilization, pitting edema in the symptomatic leg, previous DVT, bedridden more than 3 days or major surgery within 12 weeks, swelling of the whole leg, and tenderness along the deep veins, and subtracts 2 if another diagnosis is at least as likely. At 1 or less, a negative D-dimer ends the workup; a positive D-dimer, or any score above 1, leads to compression ultrasound. If the first ultrasound is negative and concern remains, repeat it in 3–7 days and teach the patient the symptoms of PE and clot extension. The Dutch primary-care rule uses objective items and a D-dimer, and is an alternative.

DOACs are first-line for VTE without active cancer (AFP 2019, Wigle). Apixaban (Eliquis label) is 10 mg twice daily for 7 days, then 5 mg twice daily; for extended prevention after at least 6 months, 2.5 mg twice daily. AFP 2022 (Mount) notes that DOACs cause less major bleeding than warfarin (1.1 vs 1.7 events per 100 person-years). Warfarin, overlapped with heparin or LMWH for at least 5 days and until the INR has been in range 24 hours (AFP 2019, Wigle), is preferred in antiphospholipid syndrome (AFP 2022). With active cancer, LMWH remains the usual choice, though a DOAC may suit selected patients (AFP 2019, Wigle).

Duration (AFP 2022, citing CHEST 2016): a clot provoked by a transient risk factor is treated for 3 months; unprovoked VTE or VTE with a persistent risk factor is treated indefinitely unless bleeding risk is high. Recurrence in the first year after stopping was 3.3% after provoked and 10.3% after unprovoked VTE. If anticoagulation stops after 3 months, aspirin lowers recurrence (number needed to treat 33 a year); apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily or rivaroxaban 10 mg daily are reduced-dose extended options. Do age-appropriate cancer screening only after an unprovoked clot, and do not routinely test for thrombophilia, especially during the acute event.

Syncope

AFP 2023 (Bayard) asks for three things in every patient: a careful history, an examination that includes orthostatic BP, and an ECG. Measure BP and pulse after 5 minutes supine and again at 1 and 3 minutes of standing; a fall of 20 systolic or 10 diastolic defines orthostatic hypotension, with or without a rise in heart rate. Mortality after syncope was 1.4% at 30 days and 7% at one year in the studies AFP cites, mostly in the cardiac group.

High-risk features point to a cardiac cause and call for admission or observation: syncope during exertion or while sitting or lying, sudden palpitations, no prodrome, chest pain or dyspnea with the event, GI bleeding, known heart disease or a family history of sudden death at a young age; on exam, persistent heart rate below 40 without athletic training, a new systolic murmur, or systolic BP below 90; on ECG, ischemia, Mobitz II or third-degree block, persistent bradycardia, sinus pauses or a tachyarrhythmia. Complete left bundle branch block, first-degree block, multiple PVCs, a nonsinus rhythm and a short PR also raise 30-day risk.

Low-risk reflex or orthostatic syncope (after prolonged standing, a meal, head turning, cough, defecation or micturition; on rising; with a typical prodrome; in a younger patient without heart disease with similar past episodes and a normal exam) is managed without admission. Exertional syncope gets an exercise stress test. Head CT or MRI and carotid ultrasound are not ordered without neurologic signs or symptoms, and EEG is kept for suspected seizure. The Canadian Syncope Risk Score is the most accurate tool in a recent review but has not been shown to beat clinical judgment. Driving restrictions after syncope vary by state: check local rules.

Red flags and when to refer

  • Call EMS: suspected ACS, ECG changes with chest pain, suspected dissection or PE, unstable AF, acute limb ischemia, BP above 180/110 with organ damage, syncope with high-risk features.
  • Cardiology: symptomatic or severe aortic stenosis, new diastolic murmur, new HFrEF, AF needing rhythm control or ablation, angina that persists on medical therapy, suspected hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in an athlete.
  • Hypertension specialist: resistant hypertension after pseudo-resistance and secondary causes are addressed, or a positive aldosterone-to-renin ratio.
  • Vascular surgery: chronic limb-threatening ischemia (urgent), lifestyle-limiting claudication despite exercise and drugs.
  • Lipid specialist: LDL 190 or higher with suspected familial hypercholesterolemia, or statin intolerance with ASCVD.

The referral lines above combine the sources on this page: EMS and ED criteria from FP Essentials 562 and AFP 2020 (chest pain), AFP 2026 (BP), AFP 2024 (AF), AFP 2019 (limb ischemia) and AFP 2023 (syncope); cardiology referral for aortic stenosis from AFP 2016; specialist consultation for resistant hypertension from VA/DoD 2026; revascularization for refractory angina from ESC 2024; cardiology review before sport for an HCM-pattern murmur from AFP 2007. The new-HFrEF and lipid lines are practical suggestions, not guideline statements.
