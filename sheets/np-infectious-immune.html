Doxy-PEP (CDC 2024)

  • Who: men who have sex with men and transgender women with a bacterial STI (syphilis, chlamydia or gonorrhea) in the past 12 months. Counsel and offer by shared decision.
  • Dose: doxycycline 200 mg once, ideally within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours after sex; no more than 200 mg in 24 hours.
  • Monitor: STI testing at exposed sites every 3-6 months, HIV screening for those without HIV, and reassess need every 3-6 months.
  • No recommendation for cisgender women, cisgender heterosexual men or transgender men: the one trial in cisgender women showed no benefit.

Try one

Infectious Disease and Immunology Primary Care practice question

A 15-month-old boy who weighs 12 kg is brought in for his scheduled MMR and varicella vaccines. For the past 3 weeks he has taken prednisone 24 mg daily for newly diagnosed nephrotic syndrome. At 11 months he had hives around his mouth after eating scrambled egg. His mother is 20 weeks pregnant. Today he has clear rhinorrhea and a temperature of 37.9°C (100.2°F) but is playful and eating well. Which finding is a contraindication to giving the MMR vaccine today?

  1. Today's mild illness with fever
  2. His mother's pregnancy
  3. His corticosteroid course for the kidney disease
  4. His reaction to egg as an infant

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Mild acute illness, with or without low-grade fever, is not a contraindication or a reason to delay vaccination. Moderate or severe acute illness is a precaution.
  • B. A pregnant household contact is not a reason to withhold MMR or varicella from a child. MMR vaccine virus is not transmitted to contacts, varicella vaccine virus spreads only rarely from a recipient who develops a rash, and vaccinating the child protects the mother.
  • C. Correct. For a 12-kg child, 24 mg daily is 2 mg/kg/day; prednisone at 2 mg/kg/day or more (or 20 mg/day or more in a child over 10 kg) for 14 days or longer is immunosuppressive, so live vaccines are deferred until at least 1 month after the steroid stops.
  • D. Egg allergy, even severe, is not a contraindication to MMR. The measles and mumps components are grown in chick embryo fibroblast culture and contain negligible egg protein.

Screening for contraindications is part of every vaccine visit. Live vaccines such as MMR and varicella are contraindicated in severe immunosuppression, and CDC counts prednisone or its equivalent at 2 mg/kg/day or more (or 20 mg/day or more in a child over 10 kg) given for 14 days or longer as high-dose therapy. This child takes 24 mg daily, which is 2 mg/kg/day, for 3 weeks. Live vaccines should wait until at least 1 month after the steroid course ends, while inactivated vaccines can still be given today. Egg allergy, a pregnant household member and mild illness are common false contraindications that lead to missed doses.

Sources: CDC Child and Adolescent Immunization Schedule Notes (2025); CDC Pink Book, Chapter 6: Vaccine Administration (2024)

Anaphylaxis

  1. Recognize: sudden skin or mucosal signs plus breathing trouble, low blood pressure or severe GI symptoms; or sudden hypotension, bronchospasm or laryngeal involvement after a likely allergen, even with normal skin.
  2. Epinephrine IM in the anterolateral thigh: 0.01 mg/kg of 1 mg/mL, max 0.5 mg; repeat every 5-15 minutes as needed.
  3. Autoinjector 0.3 mg at 30 kg or more, 0.15 mg at 15-30 kg. Nasal spray (neffy) 2 mg at 30 kg or more, 1 mg at 15 to under 30 kg.
  4. Lie the patient flat (sit up if breathing is hard; left side if pregnant) and seek emergency care; device labels advise it after every dose.
  5. Antihistamines and steroids are second line and never replace or delay epinephrine.
  6. Observe for a biphasic reaction; prescribe two devices, an action plan and allergist referral.

Asplenia, immunosuppression and travel

  • Asplenia: Hib, pneumococcal, MenACWY and MenB vaccines, at least 14 days before elective splenectomy. Fever needs prompt care; discuss a standby antibiotic for travel.
  • High-dose steroids (prednisone 20 mg/day or more, or 2 mg/kg, for 14 days or more): no live vaccines; wait 1 month after stopping (3 months after chemotherapy).
  • Before planned immunosuppression: vaccinate at least 2 weeks ahead (live vaccines 4 weeks before high-dose steroids). Household contacts can have MMR, varicella and rotavirus.
  • Travel: update routine vaccines first, then hepatitis A, typhoid and others by itinerary. Fever after a malaria area is an emergency, prophylaxis or not.
  • Travelers' diarrhea: no routine antibiotic prophylaxis. Mild: loperamide or bismuth. Severe or bloody: azithromycin.

Antibiotic stewardship and allergy testing

  • No antibiotic for acute bronchitis, whatever the cough duration or sputum color, or for the common cold.
  • Bacterial sinusitis only if symptoms last over 10 days, are severe (39 °C and purulence for 3-4 days) or worsen after improving; then amoxicillin or amoxicillin-clavulanate, not a macrolide.
  • Sore throat: rapid strep test if 2 or more Centor criteria; do not treat a negative adult test.
  • About 10% of patients report penicillin allergy and fewer than 1% are allergic: evaluate and delabel.
  • Allergy tests show sensitization, not allergy: order from the history, never broad panels.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Chlamydia, gonorrhea and partners

CDC screens sexually active women under 25 for chlamydia every year, and older women with risk (a new partner, more than one partner, a partner with other partners or an STI). Pregnant patients under 25 or at risk are screened at the first visit and again in the third trimester. Evidence is insufficient for routine screening of young heterosexual men, so screening is considered in high-prevalence settings (adolescent and STI clinics, correctional facilities); men who have receptive anal sex get yearly rectal testing.

Chlamydia: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days; alternatives are azithromycin 1 g once or levofloxacin 500 mg daily for 7 days. Azithromycin is the pregnancy choice (amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for 7 days is the alternative), and doxycycline is avoided in the second and third trimesters. Pregnant patients need a test of cure about 4 weeks after treatment, preferably by NAAT, and a retest at 3 months.

Gonorrhea: ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once at any site, 1 g at 150 kg or more. Azithromycin is no longer added. If chlamydia has not been excluded, add doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days. With a cephalosporin allergy, gentamicin 240 mg IM plus azithromycin 2 g orally; if ceftriaxone cannot be given, cefixime 800 mg orally treats urogenital and rectal infection but not reliably the throat. No test of cure is needed for urogenital or rectal infection; pharyngeal infection is retested 7-14 days after treatment. Everyone is retested at 3 months, or at the next visit within 12 months.

Expedited partner therapy, where state law allows it: cefixime 800 mg once for a partner of a patient with gonorrhea, plus doxycycline for 7 days if chlamydia has not been ruled out.

Two oral drugs now carry FDA labeling for uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhea in patients 12 and older. Zoliflodacin is a single 3 g dose (35 kg or more); avoid it in pregnancy, get a pregnancy test first, and do not combine it with moderate or strong CYP3A4 inducers; men are advised to use contraception with partners for 3 months. Gepotidacin is 3,000 mg taken twice about 12 hours apart (45 kg or more) and is labeled for patients with limited or no other options. Both are labeled for urogenital infection only, and the CDC guideline has not yet been revised to place them.

Syphilis

Early latent syphilis is latent infection acquired within the past year (documented seroconversion, a sustained fourfold titer rise, prior primary or secondary symptoms, or a partner with early syphilis). Anything else, including unknown duration, is managed as late latent. Primary, secondary and early latent syphilis take one dose of benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM; more doses add nothing. Late latent takes 7.2 million units, as three weekly doses of 2.4 million units.

A missed weekly dose: 7-9 days between doses is preferred and 10-14 days may be acceptable outside pregnancy, but a pregnant patient whose dose is more than 9 days late restarts the series. Nonpregnant penicillin-allergic patients can take doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 14 days (early) or 28 days (late latent). Azithromycin is not used because of resistance.

Follow-up: repeat the nontreponemal titer (RPR or VDRL) at 6 and 12 months after primary or secondary syphilis (6, 12 and 24 months for latent syphilis) and compare with the titer on the day of treatment. A fourfold fall within 12 months is the expected response, though 10-20% of correctly treated patients do not reach it. A sustained fourfold rise, or lasting signs, means reinfection or failure.

Pregnancy: every pregnant patient is tested at the first visit. CDC 2021 repeats testing at 28 weeks and at delivery for patients at risk or in high-prevalence communities, and after any fetal death beyond 20 weeks. Only penicillin prevents congenital syphilis; doxycycline is not an option in pregnancy.

Shortage: CDC's March 10, 2026 letter reported very limited benzathine penicillin after a recall, asked jurisdictions to preserve it for pregnant patients and infants, and noted FDA-allowed temporary importation of Lentocilin. Staging carefully matters: early syphilis needs one dose, even in pregnancy.

Trichomoniasis, herpes and PID

Trichomoniasis: women take metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for 7 days (including women with HIV, because the single dose did worse in a trial); men take 2 g once; tinidazole 2 g once is the alternative. Treat current partners and avoid sex until everyone is treated. Women are retested at 3 months.

Genital herpes: every first episode gets 7-10 days of acyclovir 400 mg three times daily, valacyclovir 1 g twice daily or famciclovir 250 mg three times daily. For recurrences, start at the prodrome or within a day of lesions (for example valacyclovir 500 mg twice daily for 3 days or 1 g daily for 5 days). Daily suppression: valacyclovir 500 mg or 1 g daily, or acyclovir 400 mg twice daily; valacyclovir 500 mg may be less effective with 10 or more recurrences a year. Type-specific HSV-2 serology helps with atypical or recurrent symptoms and negative PCR, or when a partner has herpes; it is not a general screening test, and low positive index values can be false.

PID: start treatment in a sexually active woman with pelvic or lower abdominal pain and cervical motion, uterine or adnexal tenderness when nothing else explains it. Admit for a possible surgical emergency, tubo-ovarian abscess, pregnancy, severe illness or temperature above 38.5 °C, inability to take oral therapy, or no response to it. Outpatients get ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once plus 14 days of doxycycline and metronidazole and should improve within 72 hours. An IUD does not have to come out; consider removal if there is no improvement in 48-72 hours.

Doxy-PEP: evidence and cautions

In the DoxyPEP trial in MSM and transgender women, a 200 mg dose after sex lowered gonorrhea risk (RR about 0.43-0.45), chlamydia (RR 0.12-0.26) and early syphilis (RR 0.13-0.23). In the only trial in cisgender women (Kenya, 449 participants) bacterial STIs did not fall significantly (RR 0.88), and hair testing found the drug in only 29% of the doxycycline group, so poor adherence may explain the result.

Counsel to take doxycycline with food and a full glass of fluid and to stay upright for an hour; separate it by 2 hours from dairy, antacids and calcium, iron or magnesium supplements. Photosensitivity and GI upset are common. Tetracycline-resistant S. aureus carriage rose in the trial, and long-term effects on resistance and the microbiome are unknown. Doxy-PEP sits inside comprehensive sexual health care: condoms, vaccines, and PrEP or HIV care.

HIV testing and PrEP in practice

USPSTF (2019, update in progress) screens everyone 15-65, younger and older people at increased risk, and every pregnant patient, including those presenting in labor with unknown status. CDC advises at least yearly testing with ongoing risk and every 3 or 6 months for sexually active MSM depending on risk. A laboratory antigen/antibody test is preferred, with an HIV RNA test when acute infection or recent long-acting PrEP is in play; oral rapid tests are avoided for PrEP decisions.

USPSTF gives PrEP an A grade for people at increased risk (2023). CDC goes further: inform every sexually active adult and adolescent about PrEP and prescribe it to anyone who asks. Options: daily F/TDF for sexual or injection risk (CrCl 60 mL/min or more); daily F/TAF for sexual risk except receptive vaginal sex (CrCl 30 or more; check lipids); cabotegravir 600 mg IM, two doses a month apart and then every 2 months; and lenacapavir, 927 mg subcutaneously with 600 mg orally on days 1 and 2, then every 6 months (±2 weeks), for people 35 kg or more. Lenacapavir needs supplemental doses with moderate or strong CYP3A inducers and can raise other drug levels for months.

Baseline for oral PrEP: HIV test, hepatitis B screening (infection is not a contraindication but changes the plan), creatinine clearance and STI tests. Oral PrEP visits are every 3 months with an HIV test and up to a 90-day supply; creatinine clearance every 6 months for those 50 or older or with baseline CrCl under 90, otherwise yearly. 2-1-1 (event-driven) F/TDF is off-label and not recommended by CDC, but may be prescribed for adult MSM who have sex less than weekly and can dose at least 2 hours before sex; it is not for adolescents or people with hepatitis B.

Nonoccupational PEP (CDC 2025)

PEP is for an exposure within the past 72 hours that carries substantial risk: blood, genital secretions or other infectious fluid on mucosa or broken skin, from a source with HIV who is not durably suppressed or whose status is unknown (decided case by case). Sexual assault is an indication. There is no recommendation to start after 72 hours; test, counsel, offer PrEP and arrange follow-up instead. Give the first dose without waiting for results.

Preferred 28-day regimens: bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide one tablet daily, or dolutegravir plus TAF or TDF plus emtricitabine or lamivudine. Two-drug dolutegravir/lamivudine is not used. Pregnancy and breastfeeding are not contraindications. Baseline: HIV antigen/antibody (an RNA test too if long-acting PrEP was used in the past 6 months), creatinine, ALT and AST, hepatitis B, pregnancy test, and hepatitis C and STI tests as indicated. Test with antigen/antibody plus RNA at 4-6 weeks and 12 weeks; the 4-6 week test may be skipped by someone who started within 24 hours, took every dose and is not starting PrEP. Watch for a hepatitis B flare when HBV-active drugs stop. Anyone with ongoing risk can move straight from PEP to PrEP. Occupational exposures follow a separate guideline; the national PEPline (888-448-4911) advises on hard cases.

Lyme disease in more depth

Erythema migrans appears 3-30 days after the bite (about 7 on average), expands over days, can reach 30 cm or more, and occurs in roughly 70-80% of infections. A typical rash in an exposed patient is diagnosed and treated without testing. For an atypical lesion, test an acute serum sample and, if negative, a convalescent sample 2-3 weeks later. Serology is two-tier (EIA or IFA, then immunoblot) or modified two-tier (two EIAs).

Adult EM doses: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily (IDSA: 10 days; CDC: 10-14 days), amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for 14 days, or cefuroxime 500 mg twice daily for 14 days. Children: doxycycline 4.4 mg/kg/day in two doses (max 100 mg a dose), amoxicillin 50 mg/kg/day in three doses, or cefuroxime 30 mg/kg/day in two doses. Azithromycin is second line (5-10 days, 7 preferred in the US) and less effective.

Prophylaxis is only for a high-risk bite: an identified Ixodes tick, a highly endemic area and attachment of 36 hours or more. Otherwise watch and wait. The dose is a single doxycycline 200 mg (4.4 mg/kg in children, max 200 mg) within 72 hours of removal.

Carditis occurs in about 1% of reported cases and can cause AV block that changes quickly. Order an ECG only with cardiac symptoms. First-degree block with PR under 300 ms can be treated orally for 14-21 days; PR 300 ms or more, second- or third-degree block, or myopericarditis means admission, monitoring and IV ceftriaxone (2 g daily in adults), with a switch to oral once the block resolves. Temporary pacing, not a permanent pacemaker, covers unstable bradycardia. Lyme arthritis gets 28 days of oral therapy and IV ceftriaxone for 2-4 weeks if response is minimal; persistent arthritis goes to rheumatology. Fatigue, pain or cognitive complaints after adequate treatment, without objective infection, do not get more antibiotics.

Influenza and COVID-19 details

Higher risk for flu complications: adults 65 and older; children under 5, especially under 2; pregnancy and the 2 weeks after; BMI 40 or more; long-term care residents; people under 19 on long-term aspirin; and chronic lung, heart, kidney, liver, neurologic, blood, metabolic or endocrine disease, immunosuppression, past stroke, and disabilities that impair coughing or swallowing.

Oseltamivir renal dosing: CrCl 31-60, 30 mg twice daily; 11-30, 30 mg once daily; on hemodialysis, 30 mg after each session. Chemoprophylaxis is 75 mg once daily for 7 days after the last exposure, and in institutional outbreaks at least 2 weeks and until a week after the last case. Zanamivir prophylaxis is 10 mg daily (age 5 and up); baloxavir is one dose within 48 hours of contact. Amantadine and rimantadine are not used (resistance). Baloxavir is not for severely immunosuppressed patients alone or routinely for inpatients.

COVID-19: offer treatment to mild-to-moderate patients with a risk factor (age over 65, rising sharply over 75; not up to date on vaccination; chronic conditions; immunocompromise). Nirmatrelvir 300 mg with ritonavir 100 mg twice daily for 5 days; eGFR 30 to under 60: nirmatrelvir 150 mg with ritonavir twice daily; eGFR under 30 including dialysis: 300 mg on day 1, then 150 mg once daily (after dialysis), each with ritonavir 100 mg. Not recommended in Child-Pugh C.

Ritonavir is a strong CYP3A inhibitor (boxed warning). Contraindicated examples: amiodarone, flecainide, ranolazine, lovastatin and simvastatin, ergots, colchicine with kidney or liver impairment, sildenafil for pulmonary hypertension; and strong inducers that defeat it, such as rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin and St John's wort. Check every other drug with an interaction checker; some can be paused for the course. When Paxlovid will not work, remdesivir IV for 3 days within 7 days of onset; molnupiravir is the last option and is avoided in pregnancy.

Anaphylaxis in more depth

The WAO 2020 criteria make anaphylaxis highly likely with either pattern: acute skin or mucosal signs (hives, flushing, lip or tongue swelling) with respiratory compromise, low blood pressure or end-organ dysfunction, or severe GI symptoms; or acute hypotension, bronchospasm or laryngeal involvement after exposure to a known or highly probable allergen even without skin signs. The 2023 US practice parameter adds that infants and toddlers can show age-specific signs.

Epinephrine is the only first-line drug. Give 0.01 mg/kg of the 1 mg/mL solution IM into the vastus lateralis, up to 0.5 mg in adolescents and adults, and repeat every 5-15 minutes if symptoms persist. Autoinjectors are fixed doses: 0.3 mg for 30 kg or more and 0.15 mg for 15-30 kg, into the outer thigh, through clothing if needed, never into the buttock, hands or feet. Nasal epinephrine (neffy) is labeled for 15 kg and up: 2 mg at 30 kg or more and 1 mg at 15 to under 30 kg, with a second spray in the same nostril after 5 minutes if needed; nasal polyps, prior nasal surgery or injury may affect absorption, so another route may suit those patients.

H1 antihistamines ease skin symptoms only and do not treat airway or circulatory compromise; glucocorticoid benefit is unproven. About half of biphasic reactions occur within 6-12 hours, so observe longer after severe reactions or when more than one epinephrine dose was needed. Serum tryptase rises within about 15 minutes to 3 hours and peaks at 1-2 hours; a normal value does not exclude anaphylaxis, and a baseline is drawn at least 24 hours after recovery.

At discharge: epinephrine devices (labels advise carrying two), a written action plan, medical ID, and allergy referral to confirm the trigger. The 2023 parameter says immediate EMS activation may not be needed after a prompt, complete and lasting response to home epinephrine (the device labels still advise seeking care after each dose), and that stopping a needed beta-blocker or ACE inhibitor may carry more risk than continuing it.

Allergy testing and penicillin allergy

Skin prick testing (by an allergist) gives immediate results, is cost-effective for inhalants and tests many allergens, but antihistamines and dermatographism interfere. Serum specific IgE is unaffected by antihistamines or skin disease, carries no reaction risk, and is preferred when a patient is at high risk of severe anaphylaxis. Stop first-generation antihistamines 2-3 days and second-generation 3-10 days before skin testing; systemic steroids do not affect either test.

A positive test means sensitization, not allergy: many patients with a positive food test eat that food safely, while negative tests rule allergy out well. Order tests for the foods or allergens the history points to, not screening panels. The oral food challenge in an allergist's office confirms the diagnosis. Refer for anaphylaxis, systemic sting reactions (testing plus tryptase; a baseline above 11.5 ng/mL prompts evaluation for a mast cell disorder; venom immunotherapy prevents future severe reactions) and when a better diagnosis would change treatment.

About 10% of US patients report penicillin allergy; on evaluation fewer than 1% are allergic, and about 80% of those with true IgE allergy lose it after 10 years. Low-risk histories can go straight to a supervised amoxicillin oral challenge; others get skin testing, then a challenge. Severe delayed reactions (SJS, TEN, DRESS) are never challenged and go to a drug allergy specialist. Cross-reactivity with third-generation cephalosporins is under 1% and with first- and second-generation 1-8%.

The febrile infant in more depth

The AAP guideline covers well-appearing infants 8-60 days old, born at 37 weeks or later, with a temperature of 38.0 °C or more. It does not apply to the first week of life, ill-appearing infants, preterm infants, bronchiolitis, a focal bacterial infection such as cellulitis, a complicated neonatal course, known immune or congenital problems, or fever within 48 hours of vaccines.

8-21 days: urinalysis, blood culture and CSF, IV antibiotics and admission regardless of results; markers do not change the plan. Ask about maternal HSV risk, look for vesicles and consider HSV testing and acyclovir. 22-28 days: urinalysis, blood culture and inflammatory markers. Any elevated marker means LP, IV antibiotics and admission. A positive urinalysis with normal markers means IV antibiotics and admission, with LP optional. If everything is normal, the family can choose an LP (a normal CSF allows one ceftriaxone dose and discharge with follow-up) or admission off antibiotics without one.

29-60 days: urinalysis, blood culture and inflammatory markers. All normal: no LP, no antibiotics, close observation at home and a recheck within 24 hours. Positive urinalysis with normal markers: oral antibiotics at home, same follow-up. Elevated markers: an LP may be done; a positive CSF means IV antibiotics and admission, while a normal or missing CSF allows antibiotics with observation at home or in hospital. Admitted infants can go home at 24-36 hours if cultures are negative and they are improving.

Markers count as elevated at procalcitonin above 0.5 ng/mL, CRP 20 mg/L (2.0 mg/dL) or more, ANC above 4,000-5,200 per mm³, or temperature above 38.5 °C. Procalcitonin is used with the ANC when available; without it, ANC, CRP and peak temperature together. In one hospital's review the rules missed no invasive infections but labeled most uninfected infants high risk.

Rabies and tetanus in more depth

Rabies: wound washing comes first. An unvaccinated patient gets human rabies immune globulin 20 IU/kg, as much as possible infiltrated into and around the wound and the rest IM at a site away from the vaccine (separate syringe), plus vaccine on days 0, 3, 7 and 14 in the deltoid, never the gluteus; immunocompromised patients add day 28. A previously vaccinated patient gets vaccine on days 0 and 3 and no immune globulin. A healthy dog, cat or ferret that bit someone can be observed for 10 days. Any direct contact with a bat is a possible exposure, including a bat found in the room of someone who may not know contact occurred (a sleeping person, for example); PEP is not needed if the bat tests negative. Small rodents and rabbits are rarely infected and have not been known to transmit rabies; ask the health department.

Tetanus: dirty or major wounds are punctures, wounds with soil, feces or saliva (bites), and wounds with dead tissue (crush, burns, frostbite). Clean and debride every wound; antibiotics do not prevent tetanus. With a complete series and a dose within 5 years, no vaccine is needed. Clean minor wound: vaccine if the series is incomplete or unknown or the last dose was 10 or more years ago; TIG never. Dirty or major wound: vaccine if 5 or more years; TIG 250 units IM if the history is unknown or incomplete, or the patient has HIV or severe immunodeficiency.

Asplenia, immunosuppression and travel in more depth

Without a working spleen (surgical, sickle cell disease) patients can develop overwhelming, rapidly progressive sepsis from encapsulated organisms such as pneumococcus and meningococcus, even when vaccinated. They need Hib, pneumococcal, MenACWY and MenB vaccines, ideally at least 14 days before elective splenectomy or once stable afterward. Asplenia alone is not a contraindication to live vaccines, except live attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV), which is contraindicated. For travel, the Yellow Book suggests discussing a self-treatment antibiotic (penicillin VK, ciprofloxacin or azithromycin) to take at the first fever while seeking care; malaria is more severe, so prophylaxis and bite avoidance matter.

Significant immunosuppression rules out live vaccines. High-dose steroids mean prednisone 20 mg/day or 2 mg/kg/day (over 10 kg) for 14 days or more; wait at least a month after stopping. Withhold live vaccines at least 3 months after chemotherapy or radiation. Vaccinate before immunosuppression when possible: at least 2 weeks ahead for most therapies, and 4 weeks for a live vaccine before high-dose steroids; doses given in the 14 days before therapy are counted as not given. Close contacts should be fully vaccinated, including yearly flu vaccine; after a varicella vaccine rash, the contact avoids the patient until it clears, and hands are washed after rotavirus-vaccinated diapers. Yellow fever vaccine is contraindicated with CD4 under 200.

The pre-travel visit assesses the traveler and itinerary, updates routine vaccines and adds required ones (yellow fever proof for some countries, quadrivalent meningococcal vaccine for the Hajj) and recommended ones: hepatitis A, typhoid (especially South Asia), pre-exposure rabies when care would be hard to reach, Japanese encephalitis for long or repeated stays, and cholera where transmission is active. Travelers' diarrhea: mild cases need no antibiotic (loperamide or bismuth subsalicylate); moderate cases can use azithromycin, a fluoroquinolone or rifaximin; severe cases or dysentery get azithromycin (1 g once or 500 mg daily for 3 days), with loperamide only as an add-on. Bismuth is avoided with aspirin allergy, anticoagulants, kidney disease and in pregnancy. Suspected malaria after travel, especially falciparum, is a medical emergency.

Antibiotic stewardship

CDC's 2026 update of the outpatient Core Elements organizes stewardship into leadership commitment, accountability, expertise, action (such as decision support or audit and feedback), tracking, reporting and education, with a larger role for health systems. For the individual prescriber the habits are the same: make a diagnosis that needs an antibiotic, pick the narrowest effective drug for the shortest proven course, and explain the decision.

Acute bronchitis: no routine antibiotics regardless of cough length; the job is to exclude pneumonia, which is rare with normal vital signs and lung exam. Colored sputum is not a sign of bacterial infection. Acute bacterial sinusitis: severe (39 °C and purulent discharge or facial pain for 3-4 days), persistent (over 10 days) or double-worsening; watchful waiting suits uncomplicated cases with follow-up. First line is amoxicillin or amoxicillin-clavulanate; macrolides are avoided because about 40% of pneumococci resist them; doxycycline or a respiratory fluoroquinolone covers penicillin allergy.

Pharyngitis: group A strep causes only 5-10% of adult sore throats. Test with a rapid antigen test when 2 or more Centor criteria are present, do not treat a negative adult test, and use penicillin or amoxicillin for 10 days. Uncomplicated cystitis in a healthy nonpregnant woman: nitrofurantoin, TMP-SMX (only where resistance is under 20%) or fosfomycin, saving fluoroquinolones.
