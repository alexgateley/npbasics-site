Asthma: confirm it, then pick a track (GINA 2026)

  • Diagnosis needs variable symptoms plus variable airflow: in adults, FEV₁ rises by at least 12% and 200 mL after bronchodilator (or PEF by 20%).

Try one

Respiratory Primary Care practice question

A 31-year-old woman has had 2 weeks of painful red nodules on both shins, swollen painful ankles and a low-grade fever. She has a dry cough but no dyspnea. A chest radiograph shows bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy with clear lung fields, and spirometry is normal. Interferon-gamma release assay is negative. The pulmonologist agrees with a working diagnosis of Lofgren syndrome. Which plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Mediastinoscopy to biopsy the hilar lymph nodes
  2. NSAIDs, baseline organ screening and observation
  3. Methotrexate weekly to prevent pulmonary fibrosis
  4. Prednisone 40 mg daily, tapered over 6 to 12 months

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Tissue confirmation is usual for suspected sarcoidosis, but the ATS suggests not sampling nodes when the presentation is classic Lofgren syndrome, because the clinical diagnosis is highly specific.
  • B. Correct. Symptoms of Lofgren syndrome respond to NSAIDs, most cases resolve within 1 to 2 years, and the ATS advises baseline screening with an eye exam, ECG, serum calcium, creatinine and alkaline phosphatase.
  • C. Methotrexate is a steroid-sparing agent for chronic or steroid-requiring sarcoidosis; it is not used for self-limited Lofgren syndrome with normal lungs.
  • D. Systemic steroids are reserved for sarcoidosis with significant organ threat or impairment; Lofgren syndrome usually remits on its own, and long steroid courses add toxicity without a lasting benefit here.

Lofgren syndrome, the combination of erythema nodosum, bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy and migratory polyarthralgia or ankle periarthritis with fever, is an acute form of sarcoidosis with spontaneous remission in most patients. The ERS treatment guideline reserves glucocorticoids for disease that threatens organ function or quality of life, and NSAIDs treat the joint pain and nodules. The 2020 ATS guideline suggests no lymph node biopsy for classic Lofgren syndrome and recommends baseline screening in all patients with sarcoidosis: an ophthalmologic exam for silent uveitis, an ECG, serum calcium, creatinine and alkaline phosphatase. Follow-up radiographs and spirometry confirm resolution.

Sources: ATS: Diagnosis and Detection of Sarcoidosis (2020); ERS: Treatment of Sarcoidosis (2021)

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Asthma diagnosis in more detail

GINA asks for two things: a history of variable respiratory symptoms (wheeze, shortness of breath, chest tightness, cough that vary over time and in intensity, often worse at night or on waking, and set off by exercise, laughter, allergens or cold air) and evidence of variable expiratory airflow. In adults, a bronchodilator response of at least 12% and 200 mL in FEV₁ or FVC, a PEF rise of 20%, excess PEF variability over 2 weeks, a 12% and 200 mL improvement after 4 weeks of ICS, or the same change between visits all count. In children the bronchodilator threshold is 12% of predicted. Raised FeNO or blood eosinophils support type 2 asthma when spirometry is unavailable or normal, but low values do not exclude asthma.

Confirm the diagnosis before starting controller treatment when possible, because it is harder once the patient is on an ICS. In older adults asthma is often missed (poor perception of airflow limitation), and fixed airflow limitation raises the question of COPD or overlap. Look at the workplace: adult-onset asthma that improves away from work suggests occupational asthma, which GINA says should be confirmed objectively and referred without delay. In pregnancy, do not stop or reduce ICS-containing treatment.

Risk is assessed separately from symptom control. Risk factors for exacerbations include uncontrolled symptoms, SABA overuse (3 or more 200-dose canisters a year; 12 or more is linked with asthma death), inadequate ICS (none, poor adherence or poor technique), low FEV₁ (especially under 60% predicted), obesity, rhinosinusitis, GERD, pregnancy, allergen exposure, smoking or vaping, type 2 markers, and any severe exacerbation in the past year or prior intubation or ICU admission. Home nebulized SABA is also linked with asthma death.

Why GINA prefers Track 1

Using low-dose ICS-formoterol as the reliever means the patient gets an inhaled steroid each time symptoms flare, which is when exacerbation risk peaks. In trials of as-needed ICS-formoterol at Steps 1–2, severe exacerbations needing emergency care or admission fell by about two-thirds compared with SABA alone, and by over one-third compared with daily low-dose ICS plus SABA. At Steps 3–5 the same inhaler is used for maintenance and relief (MART). GINA’s example is budesonide-formoterol 160/4.5 mcg: 1 inhalation as needed at Steps 1–2, 1 inhalation twice daily plus as needed at Step 3, 2 inhalations twice daily plus as needed at Step 4, and no more than 12 inhalations in a day.

Track 2 keeps a separate reliever. GINA now places ICS-SABA (budesonide-albuterol, 2 inhalations of 80/90 mcg per dose, up to 6 doses a day) ahead of plain SABA as the Track 2 reliever. Before choosing a SABA reliever, check whether the patient will actually take the daily ICS; a patient who stops the ICS is back on SABA-only treatment. A patient whose ICS-LABA contains a non-formoterol LABA (such as salmeterol or vilanterol) must not use ICS-formoterol as the reliever.

Review 1–3 months after starting treatment, then every 3–12 months, every 4–6 weeks in pregnancy, and within 1 week after an exacerbation. Every patient needs a written (or digital or pictorial) action plan that names the usual medicines, how to increase them, and when to seek care. Short oral steroid courses can be life-saving but add side effects with each course, which is another reason GINA favors Track 1.

COPD: the assessment and why GOLD changed Group E

Consider COPD in anyone with dyspnea, chronic cough or sputum, recurrent lower respiratory infections, or exposure to smoke, dusts or fumes. Spirometry with post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC below 0.7 is required; a normal pre-bronchodilator ratio can rule COPD out. The GOLD grade (1–4) describes airflow obstruction only. Treatment is chosen by the ABE group, which combines symptoms (mMRC dyspnea grade, or the CAT, which GOLD 2026 calls the CAAT) with exacerbation history.

GOLD 2023 merged the old C and D groups into Group E. GOLD 2026 lowers the bar further: observational data show that even one moderate exacerbation before maintenance therapy raises the risk of the next, so one moderate or severe exacerbation in the past year now places a patient in Group E, and one moderate exacerbation is the threshold to consider escalating treatment. The aim is a low disease-activity state with no exacerbations. A moderate exacerbation is one treated with steroids and/or antibiotics.

GOLD 2026 separates INITIAL therapy (treatment-naive patients, by ABE group) from FOLLOW-UP therapy (patients already treated, by the dominant problem: dyspnea or exacerbations), irrespective of the group assigned at diagnosis. For persistent dyspnea on one long-acting bronchodilator, move to LABA + LAMA; if dyspnea persists on LABA + LAMA, consider a device or molecule switch, pulmonary rehabilitation, and the inhaled PDE3/PDE4 inhibitor ensifentrine where available. At every step look for other causes of dyspnea and check inhaler technique and adherence.

COPD drugs: what to watch

  • Beta₂-agonists: tremor (troublesome in some older adults at higher doses), tachycardia and, rarely, rhythm disturbance; hypokalemia, especially with thiazides.
  • Anticholinergics (LAMA, ipratropium): dry mouth is the main effect; nebulized solution through a face mask can reach the eye and precipitate acute glaucoma. Occasional urinary symptoms are reported, though GOLD finds no proven causal link.
  • Inhaled corticosteroids: oral candidiasis, hoarse voice, skin bruising and pneumonia. Rinse after use. The benefit tracks blood eosinophils: little below 100 cells/µL, most at 300 or more.
  • Do not withdraw the ICS from triple therapy unless it was started without a reason, did not help, or caused pneumonia or significant side effects; with eosinophils ≥300, withdrawal is more likely to bring exacerbations back.
  • A patient on LABA + ICS alone who still exacerbates moves to triple therapy if eosinophils are ≥100, or to LABA + LAMA if below 100.
  • Azithromycin for prevention is for people not currently smoking; weigh antibiotic resistance. Roflumilast needs FEV₁ <50%, chronic bronchitis and a prior exacerbation (severe or hospitalized).

Non-drug care matters as much: smoking cessation changes the course of the disease more than any drug; vaccinate against influenza, pneumococcus, RSV, COVID-19, Tdap (if not given in adolescence) and shingles per current schedules; refer for pulmonary rehabilitation, ideally starting during a hospital stay or within 4 weeks of discharge. LTOT (over 15 hours a day) improves survival only with severe resting hypoxemia: PaO₂ ≤55 mm Hg or SaO₂ ≤88%, or PaO₂ 55–60 mm Hg (or SaO₂ 88%) with pulmonary hypertension, edema suggesting heart failure, or hematocrit over 55%. Moderate or exercise-only desaturation is not an indication for routine LTOT.

COPD exacerbations in the office

An exacerbation is worse dyspnea, cough or sputum over up to 14 days, often with tachypnea or tachycardia. Infection and pollution are the usual triggers, but pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, acute heart failure and pneumothorax can look the same and must be considered. Start a short-acting beta₂-agonist with or without a short-acting anticholinergic. Systemic steroids shorten recovery and reduce relapse; GOLD recommends prednisone 40 mg a day for 5 days, oral being as effective as IV. Longer courses add pneumonia and mortality risk, and GOLD notes trial evidence that patients with low blood eosinophils in primary care may safely skip prednisone. Methylxanthines are not recommended.

Antibiotics help when there are signs of bacterial infection. GOLD 2026 gives them for 5 days when at least two of increased dyspnea, fever, sputum volume and sputum purulence are present and purulence is one of them, after a prior positive sputum culture, or when ventilation is needed. Choose by local resistance: usually amoxicillin-clavulanate, a macrolide or a tetracycline, with a quinolone in selected patients. Sputum purulence is sensitive (about 94%) but not specific for a high bacterial load; point-of-care CRP has cut antibiotic use without worse outcomes in primary-care trials. GOLD does not endorse procalcitonin protocols for this decision.

Start or optimize long-acting bronchodilators as soon as possible. After a moderate or severe exacerbation with raised eosinophils, consider adding an ICS to dual bronchodilator therapy. Recovery can take 4–6 weeks, and some patients never return to their baseline, so review prevention (inhaler technique, vaccines, rehabilitation, smoking) at follow-up.

Community-acquired pneumonia: the reasoning

For healthy adults, ATS/IDSA picked amoxicillin 1 g every 8 hours (strong recommendation) because high-dose amoxicillin covers pneumococcus well and has a long safety record, even though it misses atypical organisms; doxycycline 100 mg twice daily is the conditional alternative. A macrolide alone (azithromycin 500 mg on day 1 then 250 mg daily, or clarithromycin) is acceptable only where pneumococcal resistance to macrolides is below 25%. Comorbid patients need a beta-lactam (amoxicillin-clavulanate, cefpodoxime 200 mg twice daily or cefuroxime 500 mg twice daily) plus a macrolide or doxycycline, or a respiratory fluoroquinolone alone (levofloxacin 750 mg or moxifloxacin 400 mg daily). The outpatient regimens assume no resistance risk factors; the guideline’s risk factors are prior respiratory MRSA or Pseudomonas and hospitalization with IV antibiotics in the past 90 days, which the guideline answers with cultures and, in hospitalized patients, empiric MRSA or Pseudomonas coverage.

Treat for at least 5 days and until clinically stable; most patients stabilize within 48–72 hours, so 5 days is enough for most. The guideline recommends against routine outpatient sputum cultures, against using procalcitonin to decide whether to start antibiotics, and against routine steroids in nonsevere CAP. Adults with CAP who test positive for influenza should get an antiviral such as oseltamivir regardless of how long they have been ill. Follow-up imaging is not routinely needed when symptoms resolve within 5–7 days.

CURB-65 scores 1 point each for confusion, urea over 7 mmol/L, respiratory rate 30 or more, systolic BP under 90 or diastolic 60 or less, and age 65 or older. In Lim’s 2003 derivation, 30-day mortality rose from 0.7% at a score of 0 to 57% at 5. CRB-65 drops the urea so it can be scored in the office. ATS/IDSA prefers the Pneumonia Severity Index to CURB-65 for deciding on admission, and all of these scores sit alongside clinical judgment (hypoxemia, inability to take oral drugs, social factors). Outpatients can be treated on clinical grounds without imaging unless the diagnosis is in doubt.

Acute bronchitis: stewardship in practice

Acute bronchitis is a clinical diagnosis and is self-limiting. Over 90% of cases in healthy adults are viral, and the cough usually lasts 2–3 weeks (a median of about 18 days in one review). Antibiotics shorten the cough by roughly half a day while exposing patients to adverse effects, so they are not recommended regardless of cough duration, and colored sputum does not indicate bacterial infection. In a healthy adult under 70 with normal vital signs and a normal chest exam, pneumonia is unlikely; order a chest film for abnormal vital signs (heart rate ≥100, respiratory rate ≥24, temperature ≥38 °C), focal findings, dyspnea or high suspicion in an older patient.

Over-the-counter cough medicines, honey, antihistamines, NSAIDs and steroids have not been shown to help, and albuterol helps only with wheeze, asthma or COPD. What reduces antibiotic use without lowering satisfaction: calling it a chest cold, telling the patient the cough will last 2–3 weeks, and delayed prescribing when a prescription seems needed. Consider pertussis, influenza, COVID-19 and exacerbations of asthma, COPD or heart failure when the story fits.

Pertussis

The illness runs in three stages. The catarrhal stage looks like a cold (coryza, low fever, mild cough), and infants may have apnea. The paroxysmal stage brings bouts of rapid coughing, often ending in a whoop, with cyanosis, exhaustion and vomiting; paroxysms build over 1–2 weeks, plateau for 2–3 weeks, and are worse at night. In the convalescent stage the cough fades over 2–3 weeks but can return with later colds for months. Adolescents, adults and vaccinated people often have a milder illness without the whoop, which is why it is missed. Young infants may have little cough at all, only apnea; apnea and pneumonia are their most common complications, and about a third of infants under 12 months need hospital care.

Antibiotics reduce symptoms only if started early (the first 1–2 weeks), and CDC suggests treating within 3 weeks of cough onset (6 weeks for infants and pregnant people, especially near term). Treat on clinical grounds before results if the history is strong or the patient or a contact is high risk. Macrolides are preferred from 1 month of age; azithromycin is the choice for very young infants despite a reported link with infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, because severe pertussis is the greater risk. Infant azithromycin dosing is 10 mg/kg daily for 5 days under 6 months; from 6 months, 10 mg/kg on day 1 (max 500 mg) then 5 mg/kg (max 250 mg) on days 2–5. TMP-SMX (adult dose 160/800 mg twice daily for 14 days) is the alternative from 2 months; it is contraindicated earlier because of kernicterus.

Postexposure prophylaxis uses the same drugs. CDC supports it for household contacts within 21 days of the index patient’s cough onset, and for people at high risk of severe disease (infants, immunocompromised people, moderate to severe asthma) and those who are in contact with them (pregnant people in the third trimester, NICU, childcare and maternity staff) within 21 days of exposure. CDC recommends one dose of Tdap in every pregnancy, preferably early in weeks 27 through 36, so that maternal antibodies protect the newborn.

TB testing and latent TB treatment

USPSTF (B, 2023) recommends screening asymptomatic adults at increased risk: those born in or who lived in high-prevalence countries and those who have lived in congregate settings such as shelters or prisons. CDC now recommends a TB blood test (IGRA) for most testing, with the skin test acceptable when a blood test is unavailable or too costly; IGRA is preferred after BCG and for people unlikely to return for a reading. Current CDC guidance still favors the skin test under age 5, though some experts use IGRA. BCG can cause a false-positive skin test, and a vaccinated person is evaluated as if unvaccinated. People with a documented prior positive test or prior TB treatment are not retested.

Two-step skin testing is used for baseline testing of people who will be tested again, such as health care workers: if the first test is negative, repeat in 1–3 weeks; a positive second test is a boosted reaction and counts as prior infection. A negative test never excludes active TB, especially in HIV or severe illness. Every positive test needs a symptom review and chest X-ray, with sputum studies if disease is possible, before LTBI treatment begins. Active or suspected TB must be reported to the health department; LTBI is reportable in some states and localities.

CDC prefers the short rifamycin regimens. 3HP is isoniazid plus rifapentine once weekly for 12 doses (adult isoniazid 15 mg/kg, max 900 mg; rifapentine 900 mg at 50 kg or more), not for children under 2, pregnancy, or HIV regimens with significant interactions. 4R is rifampin 10 mg/kg (max 600 mg) daily for 4 months, useful with isoniazid intolerance or isoniazid-resistant exposure. 3HR is daily isoniazid 5 mg/kg (max 300 mg) plus rifampin for 3 months. Isoniazid alone for 6 or 9 months is the fallback when rifamycin interactions prevent a short course, with more toxicity and lower completion. Rifamycins alter the effects of many drugs, so review the medication list and add a barrier method to hormonal contraception.

Baseline liver tests are not routine; get them with liver disease, regular alcohol or injection drug use, HIV, pregnancy or the 3 months after delivery. See the patient at least monthly for adherence, TB symptoms and adverse effects, and stop the drug if transaminases exceed 3 times the upper limit with symptoms or 5 times without. 3HP is also avoided when the source case likely had isoniazid- or rifampin-resistant TB.

Lung cancer screening and pulmonary nodules

USPSTF (B, 2021) recommends yearly low-dose CT for adults 50 to 80 with at least 20 pack-years who smoke now or quit within the past 15 years, stopping once a person has been smoke-free 15 years or develops a health problem that limits life expectancy or the ability or willingness to have curative surgery. Medicare (2022) covers ages 50 to 77 with the same smoking history and requires one counseling and shared decision-making visit before the first scan, using a decision aid and covering adherence to yearly screening, comorbidities, willingness to be treated, and smoking cessation. Screening never replaces cessation counseling.

Incidental nodules follow the Fleischner 2017 rules by size and risk. Higher risk means older age, heavy smoking, larger size, irregular or spiculated edges, or upper-lobe location. Solid nodules under 6 mm need no follow-up in low-risk patients (optional CT at 12 months if high risk); 6–8 mm nodules get CT at 6–12 months and again at 18–24 months (optional at 18–24 months if low risk); nodules over 8 mm get CT at 3 months, PET/CT or tissue sampling. Subsolid nodules under 6 mm need no routine follow-up; a ground-glass nodule 6 mm or more is rechecked at 6–12 months and then with repeat CT spaced over the following years (AFP gives every 2–5 years), and a part-solid nodule 6 mm or more at 3–6 months and then yearly for 5 years.

Nodules found on a screening CT are managed by Lung-RADS, not Fleischner. Refer to pulmonology for a prior cancer, immunocompromise, multiple nodules without a dominant one, or a mass over 3 cm. Calculators such as Mayo and Brock (PanCan) estimate malignancy probability (under 5% low, 5–65% intermediate, over 65% high).

Obstructive sleep apnea

USPSTF (I, 2022) found insufficient evidence to screen adults without symptoms, and questionnaires such as STOP-BANG and the Epworth scale are not validated for screening in primary care. Patients with symptoms (snoring, witnessed apneas, unrefreshing sleep, daytime sleepiness) are evaluated and tested. AASM recommends against diagnosing OSA from questionnaires or prediction tools alone. Polysomnography or a technically adequate home sleep apnea test is appropriate for an uncomplicated adult with signs of moderate to severe OSA; use in-lab polysomnography for significant heart or lung disease, possible respiratory muscle weakness, awake or suspected sleep hypoventilation, chronic opioid use, prior stroke or severe insomnia, and repeat with polysomnography if a home test is negative or inadequate.

Severity is graded by the apnea-hypopnea index; USPSTF describes 5–15 events an hour as mild, 16–30 as moderate and over 30 as severe. Positive airway pressure is the main treatment; mandibular advancement devices are an alternative, weight loss is advised for overweight or obesity, and surgery is generally not first line. In December 2024 the FDA approved tirzepatide as the first drug for moderate to severe OSA in adults with obesity, used with diet and exercise.

Smoking cessation pharmacotherapy

USPSTF (A, 2021) recommends asking every adult about tobacco, advising them to quit, and offering behavioral support plus FDA-approved drugs: nicotine replacement, bupropion SR and varenicline. In pregnancy, behavioral counseling is A and drug therapy is I. Evidence on e-cigarettes for quitting is insufficient (I) for all adults. The 2020 ATS guideline goes further: varenicline over the nicotine patch and over bupropion (including in adults with psychiatric illness), starting varenicline even in people not yet ready to quit, treating for longer than 12 weeks, and, conditionally, adding a patch to varenicline.

Varenicline (label): set a quit date and start 1 week before it, or start and quit between days 8 and 35. Titrate 0.5 mg daily on days 1–3 and 0.5 mg twice daily on days 4–7, then 1 mg twice daily, after food with water, for 12 weeks, with another 12 weeks for those who have quit. A gradual-reduction schedule is also labeled. With CrCl under 30 mL/min start 0.5 mg daily and do not exceed 0.5 mg twice daily. The label warns of neuropsychiatric events (mood change, agitation, suicidal thoughts) and seizures; patients stop it and call if these occur.

Bupropion SR (Zyban label): start while still smoking, because steady state takes about a week, and set a quit date within the first 2 weeks. Give 150 mg daily for 3 days, then 150 mg twice daily at least 8 hours apart, never over 300 mg a day, for 7–12 weeks. It is contraindicated with a seizure disorder, current or past bulimia or anorexia, abrupt withdrawal from alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates or antiepileptics, and MAOIs. Combined with a nicotine patch it raises the rate of treatment-emergent hypertension, so check blood pressure.

Nicotine patch (the OTC Drug Facts label cited): start on the quit day. Over 10 cigarettes a day: 21 mg for weeks 1–4, 14 mg for weeks 5–6, 7 mg for weeks 7–8; 10 or fewer: start at 14 mg for 6 weeks, then 7 mg for 2 weeks. Step lengths can differ between products, so follow the package directions. One patch at a time, a new skin site each day, and remove it at bedtime if dreams are vivid.
