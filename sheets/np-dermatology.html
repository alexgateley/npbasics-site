Try one

Dermatology Primary Care practice question

A 71-year-old Black woman reports a dark stripe on her right thumbnail that she first noticed about a year ago and that has since widened. She recalls no injury. The thumbnail has a single brown-black longitudinal band 5 mm wide, darker and wider near the cuticle, with irregular shades of brown within it. Brown pigment extends onto the proximal nail fold. Her other nails show no bands, and there is no nail thickening or debris. Which is the MOST likely diagnosis?

  1. Subungual hematoma
  2. Subungual melanoma
  3. Benign ethnic (racial) melanonychia
  4. Onychomycosis with fungal pigmentation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. A hematoma is red-purple to black, usually follows trauma, grows out toward the free edge and does not widen at the base or extend onto the nail fold.
  • B. Correct. A single widening band on the thumb, over 3 mm, with irregular color, a wider proximal end and pigment on the nail fold (Hutchinson sign) in an older adult is a red flag for nail melanoma.
  • C. Pigmented nail bands are common and benign in people with darker skin, but they typically involve several nails with narrow, uniform, stable bands and no nail-fold pigment.
  • D. Some fungi cause dark nail discoloration, but onychomycosis brings thickening, subungual debris and crumbling and usually affects toenails, not a single clean band on the thumb.

Subungual melanoma is a form of acral lentiginous melanoma, the most common melanoma subtype in Black and Asian patients, and it most often affects the thumb or great toe in older adults. Nail warning signs, sometimes taught as the nail ABCDEF, are age, a band that is brown to black, broad (3 mm or wider) or irregular, change in the band, a single digit, and extension of pigment onto the nail fold (Hutchinson sign). Because it hides under the nail, it is often diagnosed late. The next step is prompt dermatology referral for nail matrix biopsy.

Sources: AAD Guidelines of Care for the Management of Primary Cutaneous Melanoma (2019); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Zoster, hives and the red flags

  • Zoster: painful dermatomal vesicles that do not cross the midline. Valacyclovir 1 g three times daily for 7 days, ideally within 72 hours. Cover lesions until crusted.
  • Vesicles on the tip of the nose (Hutchinson sign): ophthalmic zoster risk; refer to ophthalmology.
  • Shingrix: 2 doses, 2-6 months apart, at 50+, or 19+ if immunocompromised; prior shingles or Zostavax does not exclude.
  • Hives: each wheal lasts under 24 hours; acute is under 6 weeks. Second-generation antihistamine, raised up to 4 times the dose for chronic hives, then omalizumab. No long-term steroids.
Safety SJS/TEN: fever and malaise, then painful red skin, blisters and erosions of 2 or more mucous membranes, usually 4 days to 6 weeks after a new drug (sulfonamides, allopurinol, aromatic antiepileptics). Stop every suspect drug and send to a burn or ICU unit. SJS under 10% of the body, TEN over 30%.

Lacerations and bites

  • Irrigate with tap water. Close clean wounds up to 18 hours, head wounds up to 24 hours.
  • Lidocaine with epinephrine is safe in fingers and toes when circulation is normal.
  • Sutures out: face 3-5 days; scalp and arms 7-10; trunk, legs, hands and feet 10-14; palms and soles 14-21.
  • Tetanus: clean minor wound, booster if over 10 years; other wounds, if more than 5 years. Fewer than 3 doses or unknown: vaccine, plus tetanus immune globulin for a dirty wound.
  • Bites: amoxicillin-clavulanate for cat bites, hand, face or genital bites, punctures, crush injuries, late presentation or immunosuppression. Dog bites at low risk may be closed; cat bites usually left open.
  • Rabies: a healthy dog or cat can be observed for 10 days; otherwise ask public health about PEP.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Describing what you see

Board items describe a rash in standard words and expect you to translate. The primary lesion is the one the disease makes itself. A flat color change is a macule when it is under about 1 cm and a patch when larger. A raised solid lesion is a papule under 1 cm and a plaque when larger; plaques often form when papules run together. A blister is a vesicle under 1 cm and a bulla above it, and a pustule is a vesicle filled with pus. A nodule sits deeper, in the dermis or fat, and is felt as much as seen. A wheal is a swelling of dermal edema that comes and goes within 24 hours: the lesion of urticaria (Merck).

Secondary changes come from evolution, scratching or treatment. Scale is heaped-up stratum corneum; crust is dried serum, blood or pus (the honey-colored crust of impetigo); an erosion loses part or all of the epidermis and heals without a scar, while an ulcer reaches the dermis. Lichenification is thickened skin with exaggerated markings from chronic rubbing, typical of long-standing eczema. Petechiae and purpura are bleeding into the skin and do not blanch, which separates them from erythema. Arrangement narrows the list: annular rings with a clearing center (tinea corporis), targets with a dusky center (erythema multiforme), a dermatomal band (zoster), grouped vesicles (herpes simplex) and a lacy, reticular pattern (the body rash of fifth disease).

Acne: choosing and combining

Acne combines follicular plugging (comedones), sebum, Cutibacterium acnes and inflammation, so effective regimens hit more than one target. The AAD 2024 guideline gives strong recommendations for benzoyl peroxide, topical retinoids, topical antibiotics, oral doxycycline and isotretinoin, and conditional ones for topical clascoterone, azelaic and salicylic acid, oral minocycline and sarecycline, combined oral contraceptives and spironolactone. Its good-practice statements: combine topicals with different mechanisms, limit systemic antibiotics, and always pair a systemic antibiotic with topical therapy.

Topical retinoids (adapalene, tretinoin, tazarotene) suit every severity: alone for comedonal acne, in combination for inflammatory acne, and as maintenance once the skin clears (AFP 2019). Tazarotene is more effective than tretinoin or adapalene, and adapalene is less irritating than tazarotene. Benzoyl peroxide kills bacteria without inducing resistance and is safe in pregnancy, but it oxidizes tretinoin if applied at the same time. Topical clindamycin is preferred over erythromycin because of erythromycin resistance, and is used with benzoyl peroxide, not alone.

Oral doxycycline or minocycline is added for moderate to severe inflammatory acne, combined with a retinoid and benzoyl peroxide, for the shortest effective course (generally 12 weeks or less), followed by topical retinoid maintenance. Tetracyclines are avoided in pregnancy and under age 8; a macrolide is the fallback there, and TMP-SMX is reserved for patients who fail or cannot take both. Combined oral contraceptives are FDA approved for acne in females over 15 who also want contraception and take about 3 months to work; spironolactone helps hormonally patterned acne in women and is used with contraception (AFP 2019). Allow at least 8 weeks before calling a regimen a failure, and refer when sequential regimens fail.

Isotretinoin is labeled for severe recalcitrant nodular acne (multiple inflammatory nodules of 5 mm or more) unresponsive to conventional therapy including systemic antibiotics, in patients 12 and older; guidelines also use it for scarring or acne causing significant distress. The label dose is 0.5 to 1 mg/kg/day in two divided doses with food for 15 to 20 weeks. It is a potent teratogen, available only through the iPLEDGE REMS: patients who can become pregnant use two forms of contraception at once (or commit to abstinence) for 1 month before, during and 1 month after treatment; they need a negative screening test and a negative confirmatory test at least 30 days later before starting, a test within 7 days before each 30-day prescription, and tests at the end of treatment and 1 month later. No refills, and no blood donation during treatment or for 1 month after.

  • Labs: fasting lipids and LFTs before treatment, repeated until the response is known (the label says usually within 4 weeks). Triglycerides above 800 mg/dL have been reported; pancreatitis means stop.
  • Psychiatric: the label warns of depression, psychosis and suicidal thoughts; ask before and at each visit and stop if they appear. AFP 2019 notes later studies did not confirm a causal link, but the label rule stands.
  • Interactions: no tetracyclines (both cause intracranial hypertension: headache, papilledema) and no vitamin A supplements.
  • Other warnings: dry lips, skin and eyes (the common effects); decreased night vision; hearing loss; IBD symptoms; bone loss; SJS/TEN.

Atopic dermatitis

Atopic dermatitis is a chronic, relapsing, itchy eczema with a lifetime prevalence of about 10%. The diagnosis is clinical: itch, eczema in an age-typical pattern and a relapsing course are essential, and atopy, early onset and dry skin support it (AFP 2020). Infants have it on the cheeks, scalp, trunk and extensor surfaces; older children in the flexures; adults as dry, scaly patches on the limbs. Children with it have higher rates of food allergy, asthma and allergic rhinitis.

Skin care is the base of every regimen: fragrance-free emollients once or twice daily (AFP 2020, SORT A; the 2023 AAAAI/ACAAI guideline prefers over-the-counter moisturizers to prescription ones), and a daily lukewarm bath of 5 to 10 minutes with a soap-free cleanser, moisturizing within 3 minutes afterward. Flares are treated with a topical steroid matched to the site (low potency on the face, neck, axillae and groin), usually twice daily for 2 weeks or less; the 2023 guideline suggests once daily for mid- to high-potency products. Topical calcineurin inhibitors (pimecrolimus, tacrolimus; age 2 and older) spare the steroid on thin skin and do not cause atrophy; they carry an FDA boxed warning about malignancy, which AFP 2020 says has no firm supporting evidence.

Maintenance prevents flares better than treating each flare: a mid-potency steroid once daily or a calcineurin inhibitor two or three times a week (AFP 2024 summary of the AAAAI/ACAAI guideline; low- to mid-potency steroid or calcineurin inhibitor maintenance is a strong recommendation). Dilute bleach baths are suggested for moderate to severe disease, not for mild disease. The same guideline advises against elimination diets, systemic steroids and topical antimicrobials when there are no signs of infection. Oral antihistamines do not relieve the itch, although a short course of a sedating one can help sleep. Antibiotics are for clinically infected eczema (S. aureus, streptococci), not prevention. Crisaborole 2% ointment is an option for mild to moderate disease. Moderate to severe disease that fails topical therapy goes on to narrow-band UVB phototherapy (second line, SORT B) or dupilumab.

Psoriasis

Psoriasis affects about 3.2% of the US population, including 1% of children, and 90% of adults with it have the plaque form (AFP 2023): well-demarcated red plaques with silvery scale on the scalp, elbows, knees, sacrum and gluteal cleft. Nails show pitting, oil spots, thickening and onycholysis (Merck). Guttate psoriasis is an abrupt shower of small plaques on the trunk of a child or young adult, often after streptococcal pharyngitis. Inverse psoriasis sits in the folds and may have no scale. Pustular and erythrodermic psoriasis are rare and can be life-threatening; inappropriate systemic or topical steroid use is a common trigger. Skin injury can trigger new plaques (Koebner). Drugs that provoke or worsen psoriasis include beta-blockers, lithium, antimalarials, ACE inhibitors, indomethacin, terbinafine and withdrawal of systemic steroids.

Up to 30% of adults with plaque psoriasis develop psoriatic arthritis, a median of 10 to 11 years after the skin, though in about 1 in 5 the joints come first, so ask about joint pain and morning stiffness at each visit. Psoriasis travels with cardiovascular disease, obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes and IBD; AFP 2023 advises screening for metabolic syndrome.

Mild to moderate disease is treated topically: a topical steroid is first line, and combining it with a vitamin D analog such as calcipotriene works better than either alone (SORT A). Systemic therapy or phototherapy is generally considered once more than about 5% of the body surface is involved (AFP 2023, SORT C), and psoriatic arthritis pushes the choice toward systemic therapy (Merck). Systemic steroids are not used for psoriasis, because stopping them can precipitate pustular or erythrodermic flares. Methotrexate needs blood count, renal and liver monitoring and an experienced prescriber.

Contact dermatitis

Irritant contact dermatitis is direct damage, usually to the hands, and burns or stings more than it itches; the skin is dry and fissured with vague borders. Allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed (type IV) hypersensitivity: intensely itchy, often vesicular, with sharp borders, angles and lines that outline what touched the skin (AFP 2010). Linear streaks of vesicles suggest poison ivy, oak or sumac (urushiol), to which more than 70% of exposed people can become sensitized. A rash under a watch, earring or belt buckle suggests nickel, the commonest patch-test allergen (14.3%), ahead of fragrance and neomycin. Shoe dermatitis affects the top of the foot, whereas tinea pedis favors the toe webs and soles.

Identifying and avoiding the cause comes first. Localized allergic dermatitis gets a mid- to high-potency steroid (triamcinolone 0.1% or clobetasol 0.05%), with a low-potency agent such as desonide on the eyelids, face, flexures and anogenital skin; cool compresses and oatmeal baths soothe weeping skin. When more than 20% of the skin is involved, oral prednisone 0.5 to 1 mg/kg daily for 5 to 7 days, then half that for 5 to 7 days; severe poison ivy is tapered over 2 to 3 weeks because shorter courses rebound. AFP advises against dose packs (too short and too low) and finds no evidence for long-acting injectable steroids. Patch testing is for persistent or recurrent dermatitis whose allergen is unknown; prick and RAST tests measure type I allergy and do not answer the question.

Skin cancer and actinic keratosis

Actinic keratoses are rough, scaly spots on chronically sun-exposed skin. Each one rarely progresses (about 0.1% a year or less), but the risk adds up with the number of lesions, which is why they are treated (FP Essentials 2026). Cryotherapy suits one or a few lesions; field therapy (topical fluorouracil, imiquimod, photodynamic therapy, tirbanibulin) suits many lesions or a sun-damaged field.

Basal cell carcinoma is the commonest skin cancer, about 2 million US cases a year, mostly on the head and neck (about 80%) of fair, sun-exposed patients. It begins as a shiny, pearly papule with surface telangiectasias and may form a central dell or ulcer. It grows slowly and rarely metastasizes, but it destroys tissue locally (Merck). Low-risk tumors are excised, curetted and electrodesiccated, or treated topically if superficial; high-risk tumors and those on cosmetically or functionally sensitive sites get Mohs surgery.

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma usually starts as a red papule or plaque with a scaly or crusted surface, may become hyperkeratotic or ulcerate, and can arise within an actinic keratosis or a scar. Any nonhealing lesion on sun-exposed skin needs a biopsy. Risk rises with fair skin, cumulative UV, older age, male sex and chronic immunosuppression. Tumors on the ear and the lip (vermilion border), those over 2 cm across or 2 mm deep, and poorly differentiated or perineural tumors are more likely to metastasize (Merck). Low-risk invasive tumors are excised with 4 to 6 mm margins; high-risk ones go to Mohs.

Melanoma is suspected by ABCDE (asymmetry, irregular border, uneven color, diameter over 6 mm, evolution) and by the ugly duckling sign, a mole unlike the patient's others (USPSTF 2023). UV exposure, sunburns, many or atypical moles and a personal or family history raise risk. Patients with darker skin are often diagnosed later, and acral lentiginous melanoma on the palms, soles or under the nails is the most common type in Black patients. The preferred biopsy for a suspected melanoma is full-thickness excision with 1 to 3 mm margins, so that the depth (Breslow thickness) can be measured; a deep shave (saucerization) is acceptable only if it allows accurate depth measurement (FP Essentials 2026). For melanoma that has not spread, survival varies with tumor thickness (Merck). The USPSTF gives an I statement to clinician whole-body skin exams in asymptomatic adolescents and adults; it does not apply to patients with a history of skin cancer or a lesion that is changing.

Cellulitis, abscess and impetigo (IDSA 2014)

IDSA first sorts skin infections into purulent (abscess, furuncle, carbuncle) and nonpurulent (cellulitis, erysipelas), then by severity. Purulent infections are treated as staphylococcal (MSSA or MRSA); typical nonpurulent cellulitis is treated as streptococcal. Cultures are not needed for typical cellulitis; Gram stain and culture of pus are recommended for abscesses, though typical cases can be treated without them.

An abscess, carbuncle or large furuncle needs incision and drainage (strong, high-quality evidence). Antibiotics active against S. aureus are added when there are systemic signs (temperature above 38 C or below 36 C, heart rate over 90, respiratory rate over 24, or an abnormal white count), immunocompromise, or failure of drainage alone. Oral MRSA-active choices are TMP-SMX 1 to 2 double-strength tablets twice daily or doxycycline 100 mg twice daily (not under age 8). For recurrent abscesses, IDSA suggests drainage and culture early and considers a 5-day decolonization regimen: intranasal mupirocin twice daily with daily chlorhexidine washes.

Typical nonpurulent cellulitis without systemic signs is treated with an agent active against streptococci, such as cephalexin 500 mg four times daily or penicillin VK 250 to 500 mg every 6 hours, for 5 days, extended if it has not improved. IDSA adds coverage for MRSA as well as streptococci after penetrating trauma, with MRSA infection elsewhere or nasal colonization, with injection drug use, or with SIRS. Orally, that means clindamycin alone, or TMP-SMX or doxycycline combined with a beta-lactam: the guideline notes that the activity of TMP-SMX and doxycycline against beta-hemolytic streptococci is not known, so neither is used alone for nonpurulent cellulitis. Elevate the limb, treat edema, eczema, venous insufficiency and toe-web tinea, and consider prophylactic penicillin or erythromycin for 3 to 4 episodes a year despite that. Admit for suspected deep infection, poor adherence, severe immunocompromise, failure of outpatient therapy, hypotension or altered mental status.

Impetigo is most common at ages 2 to 5 and begins as papules and pustules that break into thick, honey-colored crusts on the face and limbs (CDC); bullous impetigo is caused by toxin-producing S. aureus. A few lesions get topical mupirocin or retapamulin twice daily for 5 days; numerous lesions or outbreaks get 7 days of an oral agent: cephalexin or dicloxacillin, or doxycycline, clindamycin or TMP-SMX when MRSA is suspected (IDSA). Children can return to school at least 12 hours after starting antibiotics with lesions covered. Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis can follow, most often 1 to 2 weeks after the infection; CDC notes newer evidence that rheumatic fever can follow strep skin infection too.

Scabies and lice

Scabies itches intensely, worse at night, with a papular rash and thin burrows between the fingers, in the folds of the wrist, elbow, knee and axilla, and on the waist, buttocks, nipples and penis. Infants and young children can have it on the head, face, neck, palms and soles. A first infestation takes 4 to 8 weeks to itch, because the itch is an allergic response; a repeat infestation itches sooner. Spread is by prolonged skin-to-skin contact (CDC).

Permethrin 5% cream is applied to the whole body from the neck down and washed off after 8 to 14 hours; one application often works, but a second about a week later may be needed. It is approved from 2 months of age. Oral ivermectin, 200 mcg/kg repeated 7 to 14 days later, is an alternative but is not FDA approved for scabies, and its safety under 15 kg and in pregnancy is not established. Treat household, sexual and close contacts at the same time. Wash bedding and clothing in hot water and dry them hot, or seal them in a bag for at least 72 hours; mites survive only 2 to 3 days off the skin. Itch can persist for several weeks; retreat if it lasts beyond 2 to 4 weeks or new burrows appear. Crusted scabies (thick crusted plaques with little itch in older, immunocompromised or insensate patients) carries vast numbers of mites and needs repeated oral ivermectin with permethrin.

Head lice: over-the-counter permethrin 1% lotion (from 2 months) or pyrethrins with piperonyl butoxide (from 2 years) kill lice but not all eggs, so treatment is repeated on day 9 (pyrethrins day 9 to 10). Prescription options: ivermectin 0.5% lotion (6 months and older, one application on dry hair, no nit combing needed), spinosad 0.9% (6 months and older, repeat only if live lice at 7 days), benzyl alcohol 5% (6 months and older, repeat after 7 days) and malathion 0.5% (6 years and older). Do not use the same product more than 2 to 3 times if it is failing, and do not combine lice products (CDC).

Herpes zoster

Zoster is reactivated varicella in a sensory ganglion. Burning or lancinating pain comes first, then within 2 to 3 days grouped vesicles on a red base in one or two adjacent dermatomes, usually thoracic or lumbar, that do not cross the midline; new lesions form for 3 to 5 days and the rash heals in 2 to 4 weeks (Merck, CDC). Lesions are contagious to people who have never had varicella or the vaccine (they would get chickenpox, not shingles) until they crust, so cover them.

Antivirals (valacyclovir, famciclovir, acyclovir) shorten the rash and acute pain and are most effective within 72 hours of onset (CDC). The valacyclovir dose is 1 g three times daily for 7 days, reduced for creatinine clearance under 50 mL/min (label). Merck treats patients who are immunocompromised or in severe pain, or who have facial zoster suggesting eye or ear involvement, and notes that starting after 72 hours is less likely to help, especially once new lesions have stopped forming; severely immunocompromised or disseminated disease needs IV acyclovir.

  • Ophthalmic zoster (V1): vesicles on the forehead and around the eye. Vesicles on the tip, side or root of the nose (Hutchinson sign) mean nasociliary involvement and a higher risk of severe eye disease; the eye can be involved without them. Refer to ophthalmology.
  • Ramsay Hunt syndrome: ear pain, vesicles in the ear canal and facial paralysis, sometimes vertigo and taste loss (geniculate ganglion).
  • Postherpetic neuralgia: pain lasting more than 3 months after the rash crusts. Gabapentin, pregabalin, tricyclic antidepressants, topical lidocaine or capsaicin.
  • Prevention: recombinant zoster vaccine, 2 doses 2 to 6 months apart, for adults 50 and older, whether or not they had shingles or Zostavax (wait 8 weeks after Zostavax), and for adults 19 and older who are or will be immunosuppressed (second dose may be 1 to 2 months after the first). No varicella history or serology is needed. Not during an acute episode (CDC).

Urticaria and the dangerous drug rashes

Urticaria is mast-cell histamine release: itchy, red, edematous wheals, each lasting less than 24 hours. Acute urticaria lasts less than 6 weeks, is usually self-limited, and needs no testing. Chronic urticaria gets limited testing (CBC with differential, ESR or CRP, total IgE, TSH and anti-TPO antibodies). Treatment is stepwise: a second-generation H1 antihistamine first, raised up to 4 times the standard dose if needed; omalizumab for antihistamine-resistant chronic urticaria; a short oral steroid course may be used for acute urticaria that does not respond, but long-term steroids are not recommended (SORT A). Second-generation agents are preferred in pregnancy, breastfeeding, children and older adults (AFP 2026).

Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis are one disease graded by detached skin: SJS under 10% of body surface area, overlap 10 to 30%, TEN over 30% (Marks 2023). A nonspecific prodrome of fever, malaise and mucosal discomfort precedes painful red skin that blisters and sloughs (a positive Nikolsky sign), with erosions of two or more mucous membranes in more than 85% of patients. Suspect any drug started about 4 days to 6 weeks earlier; the highest-risk drugs are aromatic antiepileptics (carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine), sulfonamide antibiotics and allopurinol. HLA-B*15:02 links carbamazepine to SJS/TEN and HLA-B*58:01 links allopurinol. Mortality is 1 to 5% for SJS and 25 to 40% for TEN (AFP 2015), 15 to 20% overall (Marks 2023).

Safety Primary-care action for suspected SJS/TEN: stop every possible culprit drug, arrange same-day transfer to a burn unit or ICU, start eye care early, and record the drug as a permanent allergy. Never rechallenge the drug or a cross-reacting one.

Rosacea and seborrheic dermatitis

Rosacea affects about 5% of adults. The 2017 classification diagnoses it from one diagnostic feature (fixed central facial redness or phymatous thickening) or two major ones (flushing, papules and pustules, telangiectasia, eye involvement). Redness and telangiectasia are harder to see on darker skin, so diagnosis there is often delayed (AFP 2024).

Every patient gets trigger counseling (alcohol is one of the best documented), daily broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher sunscreen, a fragrance-free moisturizer and gentle twice-daily cleansing. Papules and pustules: ivermectin 1% cream, metronidazole 0.75% gel or azelaic acid 15% gel first (SORT A), and low-dose doxycycline 40 mg as first-line systemic therapy for moderate to severe disease (SORT A). Flushing: topical alpha-agonists or a nonselective beta-blocker. Refer for phymatous change or ocular rosacea. Topical steroids are avoided on the face: rosacea and perioral dermatitis are listed adverse effects (AFP 2021).

Seborrheic dermatitis is symmetric, poorly defined redness with greasy yellow scale where sebaceous glands are dense: the scalp, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, nasal bridge, chest and folds; on darker skin it may show as hypopigmentation. Topical antifungals treat and maintain face and scalp disease (SORT A), and topical steroids work (SORT A) but are kept short on the face; calcineurin inhibitors (pimecrolimus 1%, tacrolimus 0.1%) are the steroid-sparing option (SORT A). Infant cradle cap usually clears by 6 months; mineral oil and gentle combing are enough for mild cases (AFP 2025).

Childhood exanthems in more detail

  • Measles: fever up to 105 F with cough, coryza and conjunctivitis, then Koplik spots (pathognomonic) and a maculopapular rash spreading from the head to the trunk and legs, about 14 days after exposure. Contagious from 4 days before to 4 days after the rash. Airborne isolation with N95 respirators, report immediately to the health department, and confirm with serum IgM and RT-PCR of a throat or nasopharyngeal swab. Vitamin A (two daily doses; 200,000 IU at 12 months and older) for children with severe measles (CDC).
  • Varicella: incubation 10 to 21 days (average 14 to 16). Lesions go from macule to papule to vesicle to crust, so all stages appear together. Contagious from 1 to 2 days before the rash until all lesions crust; about 90% of susceptible close contacts catch it. Bacterial skin infection is the commonest complication in children, pneumonia in adults (CDC).
  • Roseola (HHV-6, less often HHV-7): ages 6 months to 2 years; high fever for 3 to 4 days, then a pink macular or maculopapular rash on the trunk as the fever breaks, fading in 2 to 4 days. Febrile seizures in 10 to 15%. Supportive care (Leung 2024).
  • Fifth disease (parvovirus B19): about 1 in 4 infections have no symptoms. A slapped-cheek face rash follows a mild febrile illness, then a lacy, sometimes itchy rash on the trunk and limbs that fades in 7 to 10 days but can recur for weeks. Adults, especially women, get symmetric arthritis of the hands, feet and knees for 1 to 3 weeks. Patients with sickle cell disease or other hemolytic anemias can have a severe anemic crisis; infection early in pregnancy slightly raises the risk of miscarriage and of blood, heart or liver problems in the fetus (CDC).
  • Hand, foot and mouth disease: mostly under 5; fever and sore throat, then painful mouth ulcers and flat or raised red spots, sometimes blisters, on the palms, soles and buttocks. Recovery in 7 to 10 days. Dehydration is the main risk; nail shedding and viral meningitis are rare. See again if fever lasts over 3 days or the child is under 6 months (CDC).
  • Scarlet fever (group A strep): mostly ages 5 to 15. Red, blanching, sandpaper rash that starts on the trunk and spreads, sparing palms and soles, with accentuation in skin creases (Pastia lines), flushed cheeks with circumoral pallor, and a strawberry tongue; peeling may follow. Confirm with a rapid test, NAAT or throat culture; treat for 10 days with penicillin V or amoxicillin (50 mg/kg once daily, maximum 1,000 mg), or one dose of IM benzathine penicillin G. Back to school when afebrile and after at least 12 hours of antibiotics (CDC).

Lacerations and bites in more detail

There is no fixed golden period: wounds from clean objects can be closed up to 18 hours after injury and head wounds up to 24 hours (AFP 2017, SORT B). Contamination, length over 5 cm, a leg location and diabetes raise infection risk. Potable tap water is as good as sterile saline for irrigation (SORT A), about 50 to 100 mL per centimeter of wound, and clean nonsterile gloves are fine. Lidocaine with epinephrine up to 1:100,000 is safe in digits unless circulation is compromised. Tissue adhesive suits small, clean, low-tension wounds under 12 hours old that do not cross a joint; staples are fast and suit the scalp but are avoided where appearance matters. Prophylactic systemic antibiotics do not help simple nonbite lacerations; topical antibiotic ointment on the repair is reasonable.

Bites become infected more often than other wounds: cat bites in 47 to 58%, dog bites 8 to 14% and human bites 7 to 9% (AFP 2017). Prophylaxis is not needed for bites that did not break the skin. It is given for higher-risk bites: any cat bite through the skin; bites to the hand, foot, face or genitals or near a joint or prosthesis; punctures and crush injuries; presentation 8 to 12 or more hours late; heavy contamination; immunosuppression, asplenia, advanced liver disease or edema of the area (AFP 2023, IDSA). Amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg twice daily (children 45 mg/kg/day of amoxicillin in two doses) covers Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga, staphylococci, streptococci and anaerobes; the penicillin-allergic adult alternative is clindamycin plus ciprofloxacin, and in children clindamycin plus TMP-SMX. Courses are 3 to 5 days (IDSA; AFP 2023 allows 3 to 7). Human bites get the same drug.

Low-risk dog bites may be closed primarily, which improves the cosmetic result; higher-risk wounds, including skin-penetrating cat bites, are left to heal by secondary intention, with the face as the usual exception after thorough irrigation and with preemptive antibiotics (IDSA). Hand bites need a check of tendons, nerves and joint motion, and referral for suspected deep injury. Every bite needs a tetanus and rabies decision. A healthy dog or cat that bit someone can be confined and observed for 10 days, and no rabies prophylaxis is needed if it stays well; if the animal is rabid or suspected rabid, give human rabies immune globulin plus vaccine on days 0, 3, 7 and 14; if it cannot be found, consult public health (AFP 2023).

Using topical steroids well

Potency is ranked in seven classes by a vasoconstriction assay; AFP 2021 groups them as super-high (class I: clobetasol 0.05%, halobetasol 0.05%, augmented betamethasone dipropionate 0.05%), high (class II: fluocinonide 0.05%, betamethasone dipropionate 0.05% ointment, desoximetasone 0.25%), medium (classes III to V: triamcinolone acetonide 0.1%, mometasone 0.1%, betamethasone valerate 0.1%, hydrocortisone butyrate 0.1%) and low (classes VI and VII: hydrocortisone 1% and 2.5%, desonide 0.05%, alclometasone 0.05%). The same molecule can sit in different classes by strength and vehicle (triamcinolone 0.1% is medium, 0.025% is low). Ointments were traditionally thought stronger, but evidence suggests vehicles perform similarly; ointments are occlusive and are avoided in folds and hairy areas.

Match potency to the skin: low potency for the face, groin, folds, children and widespread mild disease; medium to high for the trunk and limbs; super-high for thick skin (palms, soles) and psoriatic plaques. Super-high potency is limited to 3 weeks or less, high and medium to 12 weeks or less, and the face, groin and folds are treated in 1- to 2-week stretches. Once or twice daily is enough. One fingertip unit, about 0.5 g, covers about 2% of an adult's body surface (two adult palms); 30 g covers an area of two adult palms twice daily for 30 days. Risk rises with potency, area, occlusion and duration: skin atrophy, striae, telangiectasia, purpura, steroid rosacea and perioral dermatitis, masked tinea, and rarely adrenal suppression, Cushing syndrome, glaucoma or slowed growth in children (AFP 2021).
