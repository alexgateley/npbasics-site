Try one

Hematology Primary Care practice question

A 4-year-old girl has had scattered petechiae and easy bruising for 3 days. She had a cold 2 weeks ago. She is active and eating well, with no fever, weight loss or limb pain. Data are: platelets 16,000/µL, hemoglobin 12.1 g/dL, WBC 7,800/µL with a normal differential, large platelets on the peripheral smear. Which exam finding would MOST strongly suggest a diagnosis other than immune thrombocytopenia?

  1. A few blood blisters on the buccal mucosa
  2. A 10-minute nosebleed that stopped with pressure
  3. Small, mobile anterior cervical nodes under 1 cm
  4. Spleen palpable 4 cm below the left costal margin

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Oral wet purpura reflects severe thrombocytopenia and matters for bleeding risk and treatment decisions, but it is consistent with ITP.
  • B. Mild mucosal bleeding such as a brief nosebleed is common in childhood ITP and does not point to another diagnosis.
  • C. Small, mobile cervical nodes are common in young children after a viral illness; large, firm or generalized nodes would be more concerning.
  • D. Correct. Marked splenomegaly is not expected in ITP and suggests leukemia, another infiltrative process, hypersplenism or a viral illness such as mononucleosis, so further evaluation is needed.

Childhood immune thrombocytopenia is a diagnosis of exclusion: a well-appearing child, often after a viral illness, with isolated thrombocytopenia, normal hemoglobin and white count, and a smear showing large platelets but no blasts. ASH 2019 guidance does not require a bone marrow exam in a typical presentation. Atypical features call for more evaluation: significant splenomegaly or hepatomegaly, large or generalized lymphadenopathy, bone pain, fever, weight loss, other cytopenias or abnormal cells on the smear. These suggest leukemia, aplastic anemia or another systemic process. Most children with ITP and only skin or mild mucosal bleeding are managed with observation.

Sources: ASH Guidelines for Immune Thrombocytopenia (2019)

Iron deficiency: find the cause, then replace

  • Men and postmenopausal women: EGD and colonoscopy (up to 9% have a cancer).
  • Everyone: celiac serology and a noninvasive H. pylori test.
  • Premenopausal with heavy menses: treat the bleeding and replace iron.
  • Oral iron first: ferrous sulfate 325 mg (65 mg elemental) every other day, 30 minutes before a meal, not with antacids, milk or tea.
  • Reticulocytes rise in about 1 week and Hb about 1 g/dL by 2 weeks; recheck at 2–4 weeks; anemia corrects in about 2 months; continue about 3–6 months more to refill stores.
  • IV iron: intolerance, no response, malabsorption (IBD, bariatric surgery), ongoing losses, CKD, heart failure, need for rapid repletion.
Exam tip No response to oral iron: missed doses, ongoing bleeding, malabsorption (celiac, H. pylori, gastric bypass) or the wrong diagnosis (thalassemia, inflammation).

High hemoglobin

  • Polycythemia vera is considered with Hb above 16.5 g/dL (Hct 49%) in men or 16.0 g/dL (Hct 48%) in women plus a JAK2 mutation.
  • Look for a secondary cause first: smoking, hypoxemia (lung disease, hypoventilation, altitude), testosterone, SGLT2 inhibitors, EPO-secreting tumors, kidney transplant, or dehydration and diuretics.
  • Tests: oxygen saturation, EPO (low or low-normal in PV, often high in secondary), JAK2.
  • PV clues: itching after a hot shower, red painful hands and feet, big spleen, clots, high WBC and platelets. Hematology phlebotomizes to an Hct below 45% (Merck: below 42% in women).
  • Testosterone: check the Hct at baseline, at 3–6 months, then yearly; stop until it falls if it is elevated.

Lead

  • CDC blood lead reference value: 3.5 µg/dL (97.5th percentile for ages 1–5); a screening benchmark, not a safe level.
  • Medicaid requires a blood lead test at 12 and 24 months (24–72 months if never tested). Bright Futures: risk assessment at 6, 9 and 18 months and 3–6 years.
  • Confirm a capillary result with a venous sample: 3.5–9 within 3 months, 10–19 within 1 month, 20–44 within 2 weeks, 45 or more within 48 hours.
  • 3.5 and up: report, exposure history, test and treat iron deficiency, check development. 20 and up: home investigation. 45 and up: chelation with a toxicologist.

Hemochromatosis

  • Suspect: transferrin saturation above 45% with ferritin above 300 ng/mL (men) or 200 (women); confirm with HFE testing (C282Y homozygous).
  • Ferritin below 1,000 at diagnosis: low risk of advanced fibrosis. Above 1,000: assess for fibrosis.
  • Treat: phlebotomy of 500 mL weekly to a ferritin of about 50–100, then 3–4 times a year.
  • Stop alcohol; no iron or vitamin C supplements; vaccinate for hepatitis A and B.
  • Cirrhosis: liver imaging every 6 months for life. Offer testing to adult first-degree relatives; no population screening.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Working up anemia

WHO defines anemia as a hemoglobin below 13 g/dL in adult men and below 12 g/dL in adult women; AFP 2018 notes that normal ranges also vary with age and race. Sort by MCV first: microcytic below 80 fL, normocytic 80–100 fL, macrocytic above 100 fL. Then the reticulocyte count tells you whether the marrow is responding. A high count points to blood loss, hemolysis or recovery (for example, after iron is started); a low or normal count points to underproduction: iron, B12 or folate deficiency, inflammation, kidney disease or marrow failure.

Hemolysis raises LDH and indirect bilirubin and lowers haptoglobin, which separates it from blood loss. These markers are not specific: ineffective red cell production in B12 deficiency can raise LDH and bilirubin too, and a large hematoma being reabsorbed can mimic hemolysis. The smear adds clues: schistocytes in microangiopathic hemolysis (TTP, HUS, DIC), target cells and basophilic stippling in thalassemia, spherocytes in hereditary spherocytosis and warm autoimmune hemolysis, and hypersegmented neutrophils with oval macrocytes in B12 or folate deficiency.

Macrocytosis affects about 3% of people; alcohol is the most common cause in some groups, and with alcohol the MCV is usually below 110 fL. AFP 2009 starts every patient with a smear, a reticulocyte count and a B12 level. A megaloblastic smear means B12 or folate; a nonmegaloblastic smear sends you to alcohol, drugs (hydroxyurea, antifolates), liver disease, hypothyroidism, or reticulocytosis from bleeding or hemolysis. If nothing explains it, check a metabolic panel, TSH, MMA and homocysteine, and refer to hematology for a marrow exam to look for myelodysplasia.

Iron deficiency anemia

Iron deficiency is the most common micronutrient deficiency in the world; in the US it affects about 15% of adults and 25% of pregnant women in the third trimester (AFP 2025). Recurrent blood loss accounts for most cases. Ferritin falls first, before the hemoglobin. The AGA guideline uses a ferritin below 45 ng/mL to diagnose iron deficiency in a patient with anemia; AFP 2025 adds that a ferritin of 46–99 with a transferrin saturation below 20% also fits, and that with inflammation a ferritin below 100 is diagnostic, because ferritin is an acute-phase reactant. Merck calls a ferritin below 30 specific. TIBC rises and the saturation falls. Symptoms beyond fatigue include pica (including ice), restless legs and, when severe, spoon nails.

Finding the cause matters as much as replacing iron. The AGA recommends bidirectional endoscopy (EGD and colonoscopy) for men and postmenopausal women, since up to 9% have a gastrointestinal cancer, and suggests endoscopy for premenopausal women too, although AFP 2025 accepts treating heavy menstrual bleeding and replacing iron in a younger patient with an obvious cause. The AGA also suggests celiac serology and noninvasive H. pylori testing; small bowel biopsy only if celiac serology is positive. Up to 30% of patients are iron deficient 5 years after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass.

Oral iron is first line. A typical start is 65 mg elemental iron (325 mg ferrous sulfate) every other day: higher or more frequent doses raise hepcidin, are poorly absorbed and cause more side effects (about half of patients have adherence problems from GI effects). Low doses (as little as 15 mg elemental iron a day in older adults) can work. Take it 30 minutes before a meal, away from antacids, milk, tea and phytates; a 2020 trial found added vitamin C made no difference to hemoglobin or ferritin. Expect a reticulocyte response after about 1 week and a hemoglobin rise of about 1 g/dL by 14 days; evaluate the response at 2–4 weeks. The anemia should correct within about 2 months, and iron continues to refill stores: 3–6 months more in AFP 2018, at least 6 months in Merck.

IV iron is for patients who cannot tolerate or do not respond to oral iron, malabsorption (IBD, bariatric surgery), ongoing losses that outrun oral iron, CKD, or rapid repletion (severe anemia, upcoming surgery, third trimester). Patients with heart failure and iron deficiency should receive IV iron to improve exercise capacity (AFP 2025). Hypersensitivity to newer IV products is under 1%.

Children: Bright Futures calls for an anemia risk assessment at 4 months, a hemoglobin or hematocrit at 12 months, and risk assessment at later visits. AFP 2024 gives 2–6 mg/kg/day of elemental iron in 1 to 3 doses for 3 to 6 months for iron deficiency and suggests considering universal anemia screening at 9 to 12 months. Delaying cord clamping (at least 2 minutes in term infants) lowers the risk of iron deficiency anemia at 6 months. Pregnancy: USPSTF (2024) found insufficient evidence for or against routine screening or supplementation in asymptomatic pregnant people.

Thalassemia trait versus iron deficiency

Both cause microcytosis. Thalassemia trait is suspected when a microcytic anemia comes with a normal or high ferritin (AFP 2022). The Mentzer index (MCV divided by the RBC count in millions per µL) is a quick clue: below 13 favors thalassemia, 13 or more favors iron deficiency. Both AFP reviews give it for children, and it does not replace a ferritin. RDW is high in more than 90% of iron deficiency but in only about half of thalassemia (AFP 2009), so a normal RDW with microcytosis points to thalassemia; AFP 2022 adds that RDW is not reliable enough to use alone.

Beta thalassemia trait (one gene) shows an elevated HbA2 on electrophoresis (trait range about 3.5% to 9%, against 2% to 3.5% normally), often with a slightly raised HbF, and an MCV often below 75 fL. Alpha thalassemia trait (two of four genes deleted) has a normal electrophoresis in adults and needs DNA testing to confirm; a silent carrier (one deletion) has a normal Hb and MCV. Alpha thalassemia is most common in people of African and Southeast Asian descent; beta thalassemia in people of Mediterranean, African and Southeast Asian descent.

Trait carriers need no treatment and should not take iron unless they are also iron deficient. The point of the diagnosis is family planning: ACOG (through AFP 2022) advises a CBC in every pregnancy and electrophoresis when the MCV is low, and AFP 2009 strongly advises preconception genetic counseling, since two beta trait carriers have a 1 in 4 chance of a child with thalassemia major.

Anemia of inflammation and kidney disease

Anemia of chronic disease (anemia of inflammation) comes with chronic infection, autoimmune disease, cancer, heart failure and kidney disease. Inflammation raises hepcidin, which traps iron in macrophages and blocks its absorption, and it blunts erythropoietin production and the marrow's response. The anemia is usually mild (Hb above 8 g/dL), normocytic at first and sometimes microcytic later, with a low reticulocyte count. Serum iron and transferrin (TIBC) are low and ferritin is normal or high.

The trap is coexisting iron deficiency. With inflammation, a ferritin below 100 ng/mL (below 200 in CKD, per Merck) suggests superimposed iron deficiency, and soluble transferrin receptor or reticulocyte hemoglobin can help when the picture is unclear. Treatment is the underlying disease; iron only when iron deficiency coexists, and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents mainly in CKD and selected chemotherapy patients (AFP 2018 advises a hemoglobin target of about 11 g/dL and caution).

B12 and folate deficiency

About 2% to 3% of US adults are B12 deficient. Universal screening is not recommended; test patients with a risk factor and a clinical feature (AFP 2025). Risks (AFP 2017 table) are reduced intrinsic factor (pernicious anemia, atrophic gastritis, gastrectomy or gastric bypass), ileal disease or resection, strict vegan diets, age over 75, and long-term metformin (over 4 months), PPIs or H2 blockers (over 12 months). In a metformin trial, low or borderline B12 (below 298 pg/mL) occurred in 19.1% on metformin against 9.5% on placebo.

Cutoffs vary by source: AFP 2025 calls a B12 below 180 pg/mL diagnostic and 180–350 borderline, needing an MMA. MMA rises only in B12 deficiency; homocysteine rises in both B12 and folate deficiency, so a normal MMA with a high homocysteine points to folate. B12 can be artificially raised in liver disease, alcoholism and cancer (AFP 2017). If no cause is clear, look for atrophic gastritis: H. pylori, anti-intrinsic factor and anti-parietal cell antibodies.

B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia, glossitis and demyelination: peripheral neuropathy, loss of vibration and position sense, and later areflexia, which may become permanent. Neurologic signs can appear without anemia. Oral B12 1,000 mcg daily is noninferior to IM therapy, including in pernicious anemia and after gastric bypass; IM cyanocobalamin 1 mg is used for severe deficiency or neurologic signs (AFP 2017: three times weekly for 2 weeks, or every other day for up to 3 weeks with neurologic deficits). After bariatric surgery give 1 mg orally daily indefinitely. Reticulocytes, MMA and homocysteine respond in about 1 week, the anemia and MCV in about 8 weeks, and nerves over 6 weeks to 3 months. Folic acid is replaced with 400–1,000 mcg daily (Merck), but B12 deficiency must be excluded first and, when both are low, B12 comes first: folate alone can correct the anemia while subacute combined degeneration of the cord progresses.

Sickle cell disease in primary care

The family clinician is often the main source of care. The 2014 NHLBI expert panel (summarized in AFP 2015) gives children with sickle cell anemia (HbSS) oral penicillin, 125 mg twice daily before age 3 and 250 mg twice daily from age 3, until at least age 5, and vaccinates everyone with sickle cell disease against pneumococcus. Children with sickle cell anemia get a transcranial Doppler every year from age 2 until at least 16: velocities of 170–199 cm/s are conditional and 200 cm/s or more are abnormal, and both need specialist review for transfusion to prevent stroke. Do not screen asymptomatic children with CT or MRI. From age 10, refer for a yearly dilated retinal exam and check urine for albumin and protein yearly; refer proteinuria above 300 mg a day to nephrology.

Hydroxyurea is offered to infants from 9 months and to children with sickle cell anemia regardless of severity. Adults start it after 3 or more moderate to severe pain crises in 12 months, severe or recurrent acute chest syndrome, severe symptomatic anemia, or pain that disrupts daily life. Start at 20 mg/kg/day in children and 15 mg/kg/day in adults, and check a CBC with differential and reticulocytes at least every 4 weeks while adjusting, keeping neutrophils at least 2,000/µL and platelets at least 80,000/µL. Check a pregnancy test first and stop it in pregnancy and breastfeeding. Progestin-only methods, implants, injections and barrier methods carry no restriction in sickle cell disease.

Functional asplenia makes fever above 101°F (38.3°C) urgent: evaluate, culture and give empiric antibiotics. For a pain crisis, treat fast, using NSAIDs for mild pain and opioids promptly for severe pain, guided by what has worked before, scheduled rather than as needed; avoid meperidine; use incentive spirometry. A new infiltrate is acute chest syndrome until proved otherwise (antibiotics, oxygen, transfusion when the Hb falls 1 g/dL below baseline). Priapism lasting 4 hours or more, acute splenic sequestration and stroke all need emergency care. Do not transfuse for asymptomatic anemia or an uncomplicated pain crisis.

Warfarin in practice

Warfarin blocks vitamin K-dependent clotting factors; its effect begins within 24 hours but peaks at 72–96 hours. The label suggests a start of 2–5 mg daily without loading doses, with lower doses for elderly or debilitated patients and Asian patients (patients 60 and older respond more than expected), and AFP 2019 notes most patients start at 5 mg with lower doses in older, frail or liver patients. For VTE, overlap heparin or LMWH for at least 5 days and until the INR has been therapeutic for 24 hours; the overlap also protects against warfarin skin necrosis in protein C or S deficiency. Check the INR after 2–3 doses, then twice weekly until therapeutic, then stretch the interval; ACCP allows every 12 weeks once the INR has been stable for 3 months. Adjust the total weekly dose by 5% to 20% when the INR is off target after other causes are excluded.

Warfarin is metabolized by CYP2C9, 1A2 and 3A4. Inhibitors raise the INR (amiodarone, fluconazole, metronidazole through 2C9; ciprofloxacin and cimetidine through 1A2; clarithromycin and ketoconazole through 3A4), and AFP 2019 adds TMP-SMX, sertraline and acute alcohol. Inducers lower it (carbamazepine, phenobarbital, rifampin, St. John's wort); AFP 2019 lists phenytoin among drugs that raise the INR, while it can also induce warfarin metabolism, so check the INR either way. Garlic and ginkgo add to bleeding; coenzyme Q10 and ginseng may reduce the effect. Patients should keep vitamin K intake steady rather than avoid greens. Check the INR whenever a drug, including a short antibiotic or antifungal course, starts or stops.

Warfarin is contraindicated in pregnancy except for a pregnant patient with a mechanical valve at high risk of clotting; it was not found in breast milk and is compatible with breastfeeding with infant monitoring. Stop it for skin necrosis, calciphylaxis or purple toe syndrome. The label gives starting-dose ranges by CYP2C9 and VKORC1 genotype when it is known. Choosing Wisely (ASH): do not give plasma or PCC for nonurgent warfarin reversal.

Choosing and dosing a DOAC

DOACs are first line for VTE and for nonvalvular AF that needs anticoagulation (AFP 2019; the stroke score is on the cardiovascular page). Warfarin remains the drug for a mechanical valve and for AF with moderate to severe mitral stenosis. Dabigatran is contraindicated with mechanical valves (RE-ALIGN), the labels do not recommend DOACs for triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome (more recurrent clots than with warfarin), and in pregnancy the apixaban label says not recommended and the rivaroxaban label says use with caution. Dabigatran is about 80% renally cleared and edoxaban about 50%, so kidney function drives the choice; apixaban has no renal dose change for VTE, including on dialysis. Liver disease: avoid rivaroxaban and edoxaban in Child-Pugh B or C; apixaban is not recommended in Child-Pugh C and the label gives no dosing advice for Child-Pugh B. AFP 2019 suggested avoiding DOACs above a BMI of 40 or a weight of 120 kg, or checking drug-specific levels if one is used.

Apixaban for AF is 5 mg twice daily, or 2.5 mg twice daily with at least two of age 80 or older, weight 60 kg or less, or creatinine 1.5 mg/dL or more; halve the dose with combined P-gp and strong CYP3A4 inhibitors such as ketoconazole or ritonavir. Rivaroxaban 15 and 20 mg tablets must be taken with food; for AF, 15 mg is used at CrCl 50 or less, and the label models the same dose on hemodialysis without outcome data. For VTE, avoid rivaroxaban below CrCl 15. Edoxaban for VTE also drops to 30 mg for weight 60 kg or less or certain P-gp inhibitors. Dabigatran capsules are swallowed whole, never opened, and dyspepsia is common. Edoxaban loses efficacy in AF when CrCl is above 95 mL/min (more ischemic strokes than warfarin), so another drug is used. For extended VTE prevention after 6 months, apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily or rivaroxaban 10 mg daily are options. Switching from warfarin: start apixaban or dabigatran when the INR is below 2.0, edoxaban at 2.5 or less, rivaroxaban below 3.0.

Bleeding and reversal: idarucizumab reverses dabigatran within minutes, and dabigatran can be removed by dialysis. For apixaban and rivaroxaban, andexanet alfa was the specific reversal agent from 2018, but the FDA judged its clotting risks to outweigh its benefits and US sales ended on December 22, 2025. That leaves 4-factor PCC, which the labels say may be considered although it has not been tested in trials. Older review material may still name andexanet. All DOAC labels warn that stopping early raises clotting risk and that neuraxial anesthesia can cause spinal hematoma.

Thrombocytopenia

Thrombocytopenia is a platelet count below 150,000/µL. First exclude clumping (pseudothrombocytopenia): repeat in a heparin or citrate tube with a manual count, and review old counts; with no prior counts, treat it as acute until proved otherwise. Ask about bleeding, heparin, drugs and supplements, alcohol, liver disease, autoimmune disease and recent illness; examine for petechiae, mucosal bleeding, nodes and a big liver or spleen. Everyone gets a smear for schistocytes. Isolated mild thrombocytopenia has a good outlook: in a 10-year study of 217 people, about two-thirds normalized or stayed mildly low.

ITP is the most common cause of isolated thrombocytopenia and a diagnosis of exclusion. Newly diagnosed ITP with platelets of 20,000 or more and little mucosal bleeding can be managed as an outpatient with hematology follow-up in 24–72 hours; first-line treatment is a corticosteroid or IV immune globulin, and Choosing Wisely (ASH) advises not treating ITP without bleeding or a very low count. Drug-induced thrombocytopenia starts 3–10 days after exposure and recovers within 7–10 days of stopping the drug. Alcohol and liver disease (reduced thrombopoietin and splenic pooling) are common chronic causes; in the 10-year study, 2% developed myelodysplasia, all of them older adults.

Hospitalize for major bleeding, a count below 10,000, hemolysis on the smear, new neurologic or kidney dysfunction, recent heparin, abnormal coagulation tests or vascular signs. HIT occurs in 0.2% to 5% of heparin-exposed patients, with a fall of more than 50% at 5–10 days and thrombosis in up to one-third: stop all heparin and start a nonheparin anticoagulant. TTP, HUS and DIC are thrombotic microangiopathies with schistocytes and are emergencies. In pregnancy, gestational thrombocytopenia (5% to 11% of pregnancies) is the most common cause, stays mild (usually above 80,000), resolves after delivery and needs no treatment; HELLP and preeclampsia need admission. Most major procedures need platelets of at least 40,000–50,000.

Erythrocytosis and polycythemia vera

Secondary erythrocytosis is far more common than polycythemia vera. It comes from tissue hypoxia (smoking, through carboxyhemoglobin; lung disease, hypoventilation, right-to-left shunts, altitude), excess erythropoietin (EPO-secreting tumors, surreptitious EPO, after kidney transplant) or drugs (androgens, SGLT2 inhibitors). Hemoconcentration from burns, diarrhea or diuretics gives a spurious rise. Check oxygen saturation and serum EPO: an EPO that is high without hypoxia, or microscopic hematuria, calls for imaging to look for a tumor. Treat the cause (stop smoking, oxygen); phlebotomy is rarely needed and only to relieve symptoms.

Polycythemia vera is a JAK2-driven myeloproliferative neoplasm. A working diagnosis needs a JAK2 mutation with Hb or Hct above 16.5 g/dL or 49% in men, or 16.0 g/dL or 48% in women (Tefferi 2023); JAK2 V617F is found in about 90% and exon 12 mutations in about 5%, and EPO is low or low-normal. Clues are aquagenic pruritus (often the first symptom), erythromelalgia, splenomegaly, gout, and arterial or unusual venous clots (Budd-Chiari, portal vein). Hematology manages it: phlebotomy to a hematocrit below 45% is standard (Merck sets below 42% for women). Experts differ on the rest: Tefferi gives low-dose aspirin to all and cytoreduction (hydroxyurea or peginterferon) to high-risk patients (over 60 or prior thrombosis); Merck reserves aspirin for microvascular symptoms and prefers peginterferon or ruxolitinib to hydroxyurea.

Testosterone therapy commonly raises the hematocrit. Labels ask for a hematocrit before starting, again 3 to 6 months later and then yearly, and to stop therapy until the hematocrit falls to an acceptable level if it becomes elevated, since a higher red cell mass may raise thrombotic risk.

Lymphadenopathy and leukemia

Most nodes in primary care are reactive. A node is abnormal by size (generally over 1 cm) or consistency; epitrochlear nodes over 5 mm and any palpable supraclavicular, popliteal or iliac node are abnormal, while inguinal nodes up to 2 cm are common in healthy adults. Generalized adenopathy (two or more regions) suggests systemic disease and calls for labs and testing for infectious and autoimmune causes. Malignancy risk rises with age over 40, duration over 4–6 weeks, generalized nodes, supraclavicular location (34% to 50% malignant in the studies cited), systemic symptoms and nodes that have not returned to baseline by 8–12 weeks. A localized node with low risk can be watched for 4 weeks. Avoid corticosteroids until a diagnosis is made, because they can mask leukemia or lymphoma. Imaging: ultrasound first for a neck mass in children up to 14, CT for older children and adults. FNA is about 90% accurate in adults; excisional biopsy remains an option, and the largest, most suspicious node is sampled.

Leukemia presents through marrow failure. Children with ALL commonly have fever, fatigue, pallor, weight loss, bruising, leg or back pain, hepatosplenomegaly (up to 60%) and nodes. AML causes fatigue, fever, bleeding from low platelets and sometimes gum infiltration. About half of patients with CLL have no symptoms and are found from a high white count; it is diagnosed by at least 5,000/µL monoclonal B lymphocytes confirmed by flow cytometry, and early-stage CLL without anemia or thrombocytopenia can be watched. CML often brings splenomegaly and a very high WBC; the Philadelphia chromosome (BCR-ABL1 fusion) is found in 90% to 95%. A WBC over 100,000/µL is typical of CML and CLL. Start with a CBC with differential and smear; acute leukemia means more than 20% blasts in the marrow, and a smear without blasts does not exclude it. Refer promptly. Fever with neutrophils below 500/µL needs immediate broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Lead screening and follow-up

CDC uses a blood lead reference value of 3.5 µg/dL for children aged 1–5 (the 97.5th percentile in national data), replacing 5 µg/dL in October 2021; it identifies children with more lead than most and is not a health-based standard. CMS requires a blood lead test for every child in Medicaid at 12 and 24 months, or at 24–72 months if never tested. For other children, CDC advises targeting high-risk children and neighborhoods (older housing, social risk) and universal testing where no local plan exists. The AAP/Bright Futures schedule calls for risk assessment at 6, 9 and 18 months and at 3, 4, 5 and 6 years, and a test or risk assessment at 12 and 24 months depending on Medicaid and local prevalence. USPSTF has marked its 2019 lead statement inactive, will not update it, and points readers to CDC.

A capillary result at or above 3.5 µg/dL is confirmed with a venous sample, faster as the level rises (within 3 months for 3.5–9, 1 month for 10–19, 2 weeks for 20–44, 48 hours for 45 or more). For confirmed levels of 3.5–19: report to the health department, take an exposure history, test for and treat iron deficiency, discuss iron and calcium intake and watch development; repeat the venous level about 3 months later for 3.5–9 and at 1–3 months for 10–19. At 20–44, add a full exam, a home environmental investigation and consideration of an abdominal x-ray. At 45 or more, chelation is considered with a medical toxicologist, and children with symptoms are admitted.

Hereditary hemochromatosis

HFE hemochromatosis (mostly C282Y homozygotes, occasionally C282Y/H63D compound heterozygotes) affects about 1 in 200 to 400 people of northern European descent, and up to 25% of homozygotes never have symptoms. It is equally common in both sexes, but women present later, after menopause. Today it usually shows up as raised liver enzymes or abnormal iron studies; early symptoms are fatigue and joint pain, and the classic diabetes, cirrhosis and bronze skin are uncommon. Untreated iron overload can also cause heart failure, arrhythmias and hypogonadism.

Transferrin saturation above 45% is often the earliest abnormality, and with a ferritin above 300 ng/mL in men or 200 in women it strongly suggests the disease; ferritin also rises with fatty liver, alcohol, inflammation and infection, which are more common causes in patients who are not C282Y homozygotes (ACG 2019). Confirm with HFE testing. A ferritin below 1,000 at diagnosis identifies patients at low risk of advanced fibrosis; above that, assess fibrosis with elastography, MRI or biopsy. Population screening is not recommended; offer testing to first-degree relatives, starting in adulthood.

Phlebotomy is the treatment: about 500 mL weekly until the ferritin reaches about 50–100 ng/mL, then maintenance 3–4 times a year (or every 2–4 months) guided by ferritin. Patients should stop alcohol and avoid iron and vitamin C supplements; other diet changes do little. Vaccinate against hepatitis A and B, and patients with liver damage should avoid raw seafood. With cirrhosis, the risk of liver cancer is 20 to 200 times higher, so liver imaging every 6 months continues for life, even after iron depletion.
