Without unequivocal hyperglycemia, confirm with a second abnormal result: the same test on another day, or two different tests. Screen adults with overweight or obesity from age 35 (BMI 23 or more in Asian Americans), earlier with risk factors; repeat about every 3 years if normal.

Exam tip Prediabetes: refer to an intensive lifestyle program first. Metformin is the drug option (off label), helping most in those under 60, with BMI 35 or more, or with prior gestational diabetes.

Glycemic goals

  • A1C below 7% for many nonpregnant adults; time in range (70–180 mg/dL) above 70% and time below range under 4% on CGM.
  • Tighter (below 6.5%) if it can be reached safely: few comorbidities, low hypoglycemia risk, no known CVD (AFP 2026 also lists pregnancy).
  • Looser (about 8%) for older adults with comorbidities, poor function, limited life expectancy or high hypoglycemia risk.
  • VA/DoD suggests a 7.0–8.5% range for most patients.

Try one

Endocrine and Metabolic Primary Care practice question

A 62-year-old man with type 2 diabetes takes metformin 1,000 mg twice daily, lisinopril and carvedilol. His A1C is 7.9%. He has heart failure with an ejection fraction of 35%, an eGFR of 52 mL/min/1.73 m², a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 180 mg/g and a BMI of 33 kg/m². He has no history of myocardial infarction or stroke. Which medication is MOST appropriate for the nurse practitioner to add?

  1. Empagliflozin
  2. Saxagliptin 5 mg daily
  3. Pioglitazone 15 mg daily
  4. Weekly subcutaneous semaglutide

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. An SGLT2 inhibitor lowers heart failure hospitalization and cardiovascular death in reduced ejection fraction and slows kidney decline in albuminuric CKD, and it can be started at this eGFR.
  • B. Saxagliptin raised heart failure hospitalization in its outcome trial, and DPP-4 inhibitors offer no heart or kidney protection, so it is a poor fit for this patient.
  • C. Thiazolidinediones cause fluid retention and can worsen heart failure; they carry a boxed warning and should be avoided in symptomatic heart failure.
  • D. A GLP-1 receptor agonist suits his obesity and has kidney benefit, but it has not been shown to reduce events in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. The SGLT2 inhibitor comes first here, and a GLP-1 receptor agonist can be added later.

The ADA Standards tell clinicians to choose added therapy by comorbidity, not by A1C alone. In heart failure with reduced or preserved ejection fraction, an SGLT2 inhibitor with proven benefit is recommended. In CKD with an eGFR of 20 or more and albuminuria, KDIGO and ADA also recommend an SGLT2 inhibitor to slow progression. A GLP-1 receptor agonist is the preferred choice for established atherosclerotic disease or when weight is the main goal, and it can be added to the SGLT2 inhibitor if he still needs glucose lowering or weight loss. Warn him about genital yeast infection and volume depletion, and give sick-day advice.

Sources: ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2026); KDIGO CKD Guideline (2024); ESC Heart Failure Guideline (2021); Jardiance (empagliflozin) label, Boehringer Ingelheim (2026)

Starting basal insulin

  1. Keep metformin and any GLP-1 RA or SGLT2i; add basal insulin (a long-acting analogue has less hypoglycemia than NPH).
  2. Start 10 units a day or 0.1–0.2 units/kg once daily.
  3. Titrate every 2–3 days to the patient's fasting goal.
  4. Basal above about 0.5 units/kg with a high A1C: add prandial insulin or a GLP-1 RA rather than more basal.
  5. Prescribe glucagon for anyone at risk of severe hypoglycemia.

Hypoglycemia

  • Below 70 mg/dL: alert value (level 1). Below 54 mg/dL: clinically significant (level 2). Severe (level 3): confusion or worse needing another person's help, whatever the number.
  • Alert patient: 15 g fast carbohydrate (4 oz juice, 3–4 glucose tablets), recheck in 15 minutes, repeat until above 70, then a snack with protein.
  • Cannot swallow: glucagon, then emergency care; food once awake.
  • Beta-blockers, long diabetes duration and frequent lows blunt warning symptoms.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Diagnosis and screening in more detail

Diabetes is diagnosed on A1C or plasma glucose. The A1C threshold of 6.5% was chosen where the risk of retinopathy starts to climb. A1C must come from a laboratory method standardized to the NGSP, not a point-of-care device. A1C can read higher in some racial and ethnic groups at the same glucose (in the Diabetes Prevention Program, African American participants averaged 6.2% against 5.8% in White participants with comparable glycemia), so VA/DoD confirms an A1C of 6.5–7.0% with a fasting glucose. Without unequivocal hyperglycemia, a single abnormal result is not enough: repeat the same test on another day, or use two different tests (they can come from the same blood draw).

USPSTF (2021, grade B) screens adults aged 35 to 70 with overweight or obesity (BMI 25 or more) using fasting glucose, A1C or an OGTT, and suggests considering earlier screening in American Indian or Alaska Native, Black, Hispanic or Latino and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander adults, those with a family history, prior gestational diabetes or PCOS, and screening Asian Americans from a BMI of 23. ADA also screens all adults from age 35, and VA/DoD suggests considering screening at any age in adults with overweight and a risk factor. Risk factors listed by VA/DoD include a first-degree relative with diabetes, high-risk ethnicity, hypertension (140/90 or treated), HDL under 35 mg/dL or triglycerides over 250 mg/dL, cardiovascular disease, PCOS, gestational diabetes, acanthosis nigricans, inactivity, HIV, and drugs such as antipsychotics, glucocorticoids and statins. If results are normal, repeating about every 3 years is reasonable.

In prediabetes, intensive lifestyle programs (diet, activity or both) are the most effective option and also improve weight, blood pressure and lipids. Metformin lowers progression but is not FDA approved for this use; in post hoc analyses it worked best in people under 60, with a BMI of 35 or more, with a fasting glucose of 110 or more, or with prior gestational diabetes. In the DPP, metformin cut diabetes incidence by 53% at BMI 35 or more but by only 3% at BMI 22 to under 30.

Choosing and combining drugs

Metformin has long been first-line because it is effective, cheap, weight neutral and rarely causes hypoglycemia on its own. The 2022 ADA/EASD consensus kept it as a reasonable start but separated glucose lowering from organ protection: in established or high-risk cardiovascular disease, heart failure or CKD, a GLP-1 RA or SGLT2 inhibitor with proven benefit is chosen for that benefit, independent of A1C and of metformin. GLP-1 RAs were prioritized for atherosclerotic disease; SGLT2 inhibitors for heart failure (especially reduced ejection fraction) and CKD. The 2026 consensus goes further, advocating these drugs earlier, potentially from diagnosis, and considering earlier combination of an SGLT2 inhibitor with a GLP-1-based drug in people who also have CVD, CKD or heart failure.

In CKD, ADA/KDIGO recommends an SGLT2 inhibitor for people with type 2 diabetes and an eGFR of 20 or more, and metformin when the eGFR is 30 or more, with the dose adjusted below 45 (and for some patients at 45–59). The labels match: Jardiance is not recommended for glucose lowering below an eGFR of 30, where it barely lowers glucose, but its kidney and heart indications do not depend on that. Metformin is contraindicated below 30, should not be started between 30 and 45, and needs a risk review if the eGFR falls below 45. Start metformin at 500 mg twice daily or 850 mg once daily with meals and increase by 500 mg weekly or 850 mg every 2 weeks toward a maximum of 2,550 mg a day. Before iodinated contrast, stop it in patients with an eGFR of 30–60, liver disease, alcoholism or heart failure, or before intra-arterial contrast, and restart once the eGFR 48 hours later is stable. The label asks for a blood count yearly and vitamin B12 every 2 to 3 years.

When A1C is 1.5% or more above goal at diagnosis (8.5% or higher for most), start two agents; combinations reach goals faster and delay treatment failure. Sulfonylureas lower glucose well and cheaply but do not last, cause hypoglycemia and weight gain, and are a poor fit for frail older adults. GLP-1 RAs cause nausea mainly during dose increases, so the dose is stepped up slowly (Ozempic starts at 0.25 mg weekly for 4 weeks). Rapid A1C improvement on semaglutide can temporarily worsen diabetic retinopathy, so a patient with known retinopathy needs eye follow-up. GLP-1 RAs slow gastric emptying, and aspiration under anesthesia has been reported; patients should tell the team before a procedure. Hold an SGLT2 inhibitor at least 3 days before surgery or a prolonged fast, because ketoacidosis can occur with near-normal glucose.

Insulin in type 2 diabetes

Insulin is first-line when hyperglycemia is severe (A1C above 10% or glucose 300 mg/dL or more) or there are symptoms of catabolism such as weight loss, and it is otherwise added when other drugs fail to reach goal. Basal insulin is the usual start. The Lantus label gives 0.2 units/kg or up to 10 units once daily for a patient new to insulin; ADA/EASD gives 0.1–0.2 units/kg/day. Long-acting analogues lower A1C as well as NPH with less hypoglycemia, especially at night. The dose can be adjusted as often as every 2 to 3 days until the fasting goal is met.

When basal insulin has been pushed beyond about 0.5 units/kg and the fasting glucose is acceptable but A1C remains high, the problem is usually after meals; more basal insulin only adds hypoglycemia and weight. The next step is prandial insulin at the largest meal or a GLP-1 RA. GLP-1 RAs and SGLT2 inhibitors should be started or continued in insulin users, with an insulin dose reduction when they start to avoid lows. Real-time CGM can be offered to insulin users (VA/DoD suggests it for those not at goal); in trials it lowered A1C modestly. Out-of-pocket insulin cost is now capped at about $35 a month for many products.

Hypoglycemia

Glucose below 70 mg/dL is the alert value and below 54 mg/dL is clinically significant hypoglycemia; the ADA/EASD statement keeps 'severe' for a low with cognitive impairment that needs another person's help, whatever the reading (CDC's patient pages call any reading below 54 severe). Symptoms are adrenergic (tremor, sweating, fast heartbeat, hunger, anxiety) and neuroglycopenic (confusion, odd behavior, blurred vision, seizures). Awareness fades with more than 5 to 10 years of diabetes, frequent lows and beta-blockers, so these patients should check more often. Treat a conscious patient with 15 g of fast carbohydrate, such as 4 oz of juice or regular soda, 1 tablespoon of sugar or 3–4 glucose tablets; recheck in 15 minutes and repeat until the glucose is above 70, then give a snack with protein. A patient who cannot swallow needs glucagon and emergency care; most wake within 15 minutes. Anyone on insulin or a sulfonylurea should be taught this and, if at risk of severe lows, prescribed glucagon.

Obesity medicines

Obesity is a chronic disease managed with nutrition, activity, behavioral counseling, drugs and, when criteria are met, surgery. Drugs are offered to nonpregnant adults with a BMI of 30 or more, or 27 or more with hypertension, type 2 diabetes or dyslipidemia, alongside lifestyle care. Weight returns when drugs stop: in one semaglutide study, people regained about two thirds of the weight lost within a year of stopping.

Wegovy (semaglutide) starts at 0.25 mg weekly and rises every 4 weeks to the usual 2.4 mg maintenance dose; the label now allows 7.2 mg for adult weight reduction after at least 4 weeks on 2.4 mg, and also covers cardiovascular risk reduction in established CVD and noncirrhotic MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis. It is stopped when pregnancy is recognized and at least 2 months before a planned pregnancy. Zepbound (tirzepatide) starts at 2.5 mg weekly and rises in 2.5 mg steps at least every 4 weeks; it also treats moderate to severe sleep apnea in obesity. Because it slows gastric emptying, oral hormonal contraception becomes less reliable: switch to a non-oral method or add a barrier for 4 weeks after starting and after each dose increase. Both carry the MTC and MEN 2 contraindication and warnings for pancreatitis and gallbladder disease.

Phentermine-topiramate is a schedule IV drug and a teratogen: get a negative pregnancy test before starting and monthly, and use effective contraception. It is contraindicated in glaucoma, hyperthyroidism and with an MAOI (or within 14 days). At 12 weeks on 7.5/46 mg, an adult who has not lost 3% escalates (11.25/69 mg for 14 days, then 15/92 mg); at 12 weeks on 15/92 mg, under 5% means stop. Naltrexone-bupropion carries the antidepressant suicidality boxed warning, is contraindicated with uncontrolled hypertension, seizures, bulimia or anorexia, chronic opioid use, an MAOI or abrupt withdrawal of alcohol or sedatives, and is stopped if weight loss is under 5% after 12 weeks at the maintenance dose. Thyroid hormone is never a weight-loss drug; the levothyroxine label carries that as a boxed warning.

Hypothyroidism and levothyroxine

TSH is the first and usually only test. The normal range runs from about 0.4 to 4.0–4.5 mIU/L; a high TSH with a low free T4 is overt hypothyroidism and with a normal free T4 is subclinical. Do not order T3 for a high TSH or use it to guide levothyroxine dosing. Autoimmune (Hashimoto) thyroiditis is the commonest cause of primary hypothyroidism; positive TPO antibodies support it and predict progression from subclinical to overt disease, but antibodies alone do not diagnose hypothyroidism. Ultrasound is for a palpable nodule, not for abnormal function. USPSTF finds insufficient evidence to screen asymptomatic nonpregnant adults. Biotin interferes with many assays: stop it at least 2 days before testing.

Levothyroxine full replacement is about 1.6 mcg/kg/day (AFP: 1.5–1.8 mcg/kg in patients under 60 without heart disease). Older adults and those with known or suspected ischemic heart disease start lower, 25–50 mcg (12.5 mcg in the most fragile), because over-replacement can provoke angina or atrial fibrillation; it also speeds bone loss in postmenopausal women. Adjust in 12.5–25 mcg steps and recheck TSH 6 to 8 weeks after each change, then every 6 to 12 months once stable. Doses above 200 mcg are seldom needed; poor response at high doses usually means missed doses, malabsorption or an interaction. Calcium, iron, antacids and bile acid binders block absorption (separate by 4 hours), and PPIs reduce it. Estrogen and pregnancy raise binding globulin, so judge total T4 with care and use free T4. T3 alone or added to levothyroxine and desiccated thyroid are not recommended.

Subclinical hypothyroidism often resolves on its own, especially with a TSH of 10 or less (in a pooled analysis of older adults, about 61% normalized within a year). Treat a nonpregnant adult when TSH is above 10 mIU/L or TPO antibodies are elevated; in adults 65 or older with TSH under 10, levothyroxine may increase mortality. In pregnancy, a woman already on levothyroxine needs about 30% more as soon as pregnancy is confirmed (two extra doses a week), with TSH checked every 4 weeks against trimester-specific ranges and the dose returned to the pre-pregnancy level after delivery. For new hypothyroidism in pregnancy the label starts 1.6 mcg/kg/day if TSH is 10 or more and 1.0 mcg/kg/day if below 10.

Hyperthyroidism

A suppressed TSH (below 0.1) with a high free T4 or total T3 is overt hyperthyroidism. The radioactive iodine uptake and scan find the cause: normal uptake is 15–25% at 24 hours; Graves disease is high and diffuse, a toxic adenoma one hot area, a toxic multinodular goiter several, and thyroiditis (painless, subacute or postpartum) near zero. TSH receptor antibodies confirm Graves when the signs are not classic or a scan is not possible; ultrasound replaces the scan in pregnancy and breastfeeding. A low TSH with normal hormone levels can also come from nonthyroidal illness, glucocorticoids or dopamine.

A beta-blocker controls adrenergic symptoms whatever the cause; atenolol is safer in asthma and COPD, and propranolol also slows conversion of T4 to T3. Methimazole is the preferred antithyroid drug except in the first trimester, when propylthiouracil is used because methimazole can cause birth defects; switch back after the first trimester because propylthiouracil carries a boxed warning for severe liver injury. Get a CBC with differential and liver panel before starting. Routine CBCs are not needed, but fever or sore throat means stop the drug and check a CBC for agranulocytosis. Check free T4 and total T3 at 4 weeks, then every 4 to 8 weeks; TSH can stay suppressed for months. A course lasts 12 to 18 months; relapse occurs in 30% to 70%, mostly within a year.

Radioactive iodine is the most common US treatment for Graves disease and is recommended, with surgery, for toxic nodules. It is contraindicated in pregnancy (exclude pregnancy within 48 hours and avoid it for 6 months after), and moderate to severe orbitopathy, especially in smokers, is a relative contraindication. Most patients become permanently hypothyroid 2 to 6 months later. Thyroidectomy is preferred for a compressive goiter and when radioiodine or drugs cannot be used; risks include recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and hypoparathyroidism.

Subclinical hyperthyroidism (low TSH, normal free T4 and T3) is confirmed on repeat testing in 3 to 6 months, sooner (2 to 6 weeks) in high-risk patients. A TSH below 0.1 more than doubles the risk of atrial fibrillation and raises fracture and heart failure risk. Treat when TSH is below 0.1 in adults 65 or older, and in younger adults with heart disease, osteoporosis or symptoms, including postmenopausal women not on estrogen or a bisphosphonate; at TSH 0.1–0.4, consider treatment in the same groups and observe the rest. Treatment follows the cause, with radioiodine preferred for autonomous nodules, which rarely remit.

Thyroid nodules

Every nodule gets a TSH and an ultrasound that includes the neck lymph nodes. A low TSH points to a functioning nodule: an uptake scan showing a hot nodule means malignancy is unlikely and no biopsy is needed. Cold nodules carry a 14–22% malignancy risk and are sorted by ultrasound features: hypoechoic, solid, irregular margins, microcalcifications, taller than wide, extrathyroidal extension, disrupted rim calcification and abnormal nodes. Biopsy nodules of 1 cm or more with a suspicious pattern; spongiform or mostly cystic nodules only at 2 cm or more; pure cysts and nodules under 1 cm generally not.

Adrenal insufficiency

Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease, autoimmune in 8 or 9 of 10 cases in developed countries) destroys both cortisol and aldosterone production. Early symptoms are vague: fatigue, weight loss, poor appetite, abdominal pain and postural dizziness. Hyperpigmentation (scars, skin folds, knuckles, lips, buccal mucosa) is specific to primary disease because ACTH is high; salt craving and postural hypotension reflect aldosterone loss, and labs show hyponatremia and then hyperkalemia. Secondary disease (pituitary) and tertiary disease (most often from stopping long-term glucocorticoids, the commonest cause overall) spare aldosterone, so there is no hyperpigmentation or hyperkalemia, though hyponatremia can occur.

The 250 mcg cosyntropin test is the diagnostic standard: a peak cortisol below 18 mcg/dL (500 nmol/L) at 30 or 60 minutes indicates adrenal insufficiency (assay cutoffs vary). If it is not available, a morning cortisol below 5 mcg/dL with a high ACTH is suggestive, and ACTH above twice the upper limit fits primary disease; renin is high when mineralocorticoid is lacking. 21-hydroxylase antibodies identify autoimmune disease. Replacement in primary disease is hydrocortisone 15–25 mg a day in 2 or 3 doses, the largest on waking, plus fludrocortisone (usually 0.05–0.2 mg; secondary disease rarely needs it), titrated to symptoms, postural blood pressure, potassium and renin rather than cortisol levels.

Sick-day rules: double the hydrocortisone for fever above 38 °C and triple it above 39 °C until recovery; vomiting or inability to keep pills down needs 100 mg hydrocortisone IM or SC and urgent care. Every patient needs a steroid emergency card, medical ID and an injection kit with training. Adrenal crisis (hypotension, severe weakness, abdominal pain, confusion, hypoglycemia) is treated with hydrocortisone 100 mg IV or IM immediately, then about 200 mg per 24 hours, and about 1 L of isotonic saline in the first hour; never wait for test results.

Glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency is unlikely after courses shorter than 3–4 weeks. After longer use above a physiologic dose (prednisone 4–6 mg or hydrocortisone 15–25 mg a day), taper quickly while the dose is supraphysiologic and slowly near physiologic doses (for example, 1 mg steps every 1–4 weeks from 5–10 mg prednisone). Once at a physiologic dose, a morning cortisol (with the dose held 24 hours) above 10 mcg/dL shows recovery and steroids can stop; 5–10 mcg/dL means continue and repeat in weeks to months; below 5 means continue and repeat in a few months. Think of it also after intra-articular injections in the previous 2 months, long-term high-dose inhaled or topical steroids, or a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor. Stress dosing is double the physiologic dose.

Cushing syndrome

The first step is to exclude exogenous glucocorticoids with a full drug history including creams, inhaled and injected steroids, herbal products and megestrol. Test for endogenous Cushing syndrome when features are multiple and progressing, when osteoporosis or hypertension is unusual for the patient's age, in a child gaining weight while growth slows, and in an adrenal incidentaloma. The most discriminating signs are easy bruising, facial plethora, proximal myopathy and reddish-purple striae wider than 1 cm; obesity, hypertension, diabetes and depression are common but nonspecific.

Use one high-accuracy screen: 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression (cortisol above 1.8 mcg/dL the next morning is abnormal), two late-night salivary cortisols, or at least two 24-hour urine free cortisols. Oral estrogen raises cortisol-binding globulin and gives false-positive dexamethasone tests (urine cortisol is unaffected); CYP3A4 inducers such as phenytoin, carbamazepine and rifampin speed dexamethasone clearance; shift workers are poor candidates for salivary testing; and depression, alcohol, obesity and uncontrolled diabetes can cause mild cortisol excess. An abnormal result goes to endocrinology for a second test; concordant normal results end the workup.

Osteoporosis

USPSTF (2025, grade B) recommends screening women 65 and older and postmenopausal women under 65 with a risk factor (low weight, parental hip fracture, smoking, excess alcohol) whose risk is raised on a tool such as FRAX or the OST; evidence in men is insufficient, though BHOF screens men from 70. Central DXA of the hip and lumbar spine is the test (the one-third radius if those sites are unreliable). T-scores of −1.0 or above are normal, −1.0 to −2.5 is low bone mass, and −2.5 or below is osteoporosis; a fragility or vertebral fracture establishes osteoporosis whatever the T-score. In adults 20 to 50, use a Z-score (−2.0 or below is low for age). Do not repeat DXA more often than every 2 years for screening; on treatment, every 1 to 2 years until stable.

Before treating, look for secondary causes: CBC, metabolic panel, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, PTH, phosphate, TSH and 24-hour urine calcium, and review glucocorticoids, PPIs and aromatase inhibitors. Treat with a T-score of −2.5 or below, a hip or vertebral fragility fracture, or low bone mass with a 10-year FRAX risk of 3% or more for hip or 20% or more for major osteoporotic fracture. Fixed FRAX thresholds may under-identify Asian, Black and Hispanic patients. Everyone needs calcium (about 1,000 mg a day for men 50–70; 1,200 mg for women over 50 and men over 70) and vitamin D (800–1,000 IU, treating deficiency to at least 30 ng/mL), weight-bearing exercise, falls prevention, smoking cessation and limited alcohol.

Bisphosphonates are first-line. Alendronate is 70 mg weekly (or 10 mg daily), taken on rising with 6–8 oz of plain water at least 30 minutes before food, drink or other drugs, staying upright until after eating; it is contraindicated with esophageal stricture or achalasia, inability to sit or stand upright for 30 minutes and hypocalcemia, and avoided with malabsorption after bypass and a creatinine clearance below 35. Zoledronic acid 5 mg IV once a year suits those who cannot take oral drugs; it is contraindicated below a creatinine clearance of 35 and in acute kidney injury, and creatinine is checked before each dose. Denosumab 60 mg subcutaneously every 6 months is second-line (ACP), an option when a bisphosphonate is not tolerated or kidney function rules one out, but in advanced CKD (eGFR below 30) it can cause severe hypocalcemia (boxed warning). Raloxifene suits women at high risk of vertebral fracture and breast cancer with low clot risk; it does not prevent hip fracture.

Very high risk (T-score −3.0 or below, multiple or recent vertebral fractures, fractures on treatment) calls for an anabolic drug first: teriparatide 20 mcg daily (no more than 2 years over a lifetime unless risk stays high), abaloparatide daily (AFP 2023 gives an 18-month limit) or romosozumab monthly for 12 doses, which is not started within a year of a myocardial infarction or stroke. Bone density falls quickly after an anabolic, so an antiresorptive always follows.

Drug holidays: osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femoral fractures are rare with bisphosphonates and denosumab, but femoral fracture risk rises with longer bisphosphonate use and falls after stopping. Reassess after 5 years of an oral bisphosphonate or 3 years of zoledronic acid; patients still at high risk (prior fracture, hip T-score −2.5 or below) may continue to 10 years oral or 6 years IV. The holiday usually lasts 2 to 4 years by expert opinion, and treatment restarts when risk rises. Denosumab is different: its effect wears off within months, and multiple vertebral fractures have followed stopping it, so it is never simply stopped; a bisphosphonate is started when it ends.

Calcium and parathyroid disorders

Hypercalcemia causes polyuria and thirst, stones, nausea, constipation, pancreatitis, confusion, bone pain and a short QT. Check calcium with albumin (or ionized calcium), PTH, creatinine, 25-hydroxyvitamin D and 24-hour urine calcium and creatinine. A high or inappropriately normal PTH means a PTH-driven cause, most often primary hyperparathyroidism from a single adenoma; familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia looks similar but has low urine calcium and needs no surgery; lithium and MEN syndromes are other causes. A low PTH points away from the parathyroids: malignancy (PTHrP or bone metastases, often with rapid onset), vitamin D excess including granulomatous disease, thiazides, vitamin A and milk-alkali syndrome.

Asymptomatic primary hyperparathyroidism goes to surgery if any one of these applies (Fifth International Workshop, 2022): calcium more than 1 mg/dL above the upper limit; T-score −2.5 or below at any site or a vertebral fracture on imaging; creatinine clearance or eGFR below 60, stones or nephrocalcinosis, or urine calcium over 250 mg/day in women or 300 mg/day in men; or age under 50. Workup includes three-site DXA (spine, hip and distal one-third radius) and vertebral imaging. Without surgery, check calcium and vitamin D yearly, creatinine clearance yearly, and DXA every 1 to 2 years. Keep 25-hydroxyvitamin D above 30 ng/mL and calcium intake at the usual amount for age (800–1,000 mg a day in the workshop's figures); restricting calcium raises PTH and is not advised. Cinacalcet lowers calcium but not bone loss; alendronate or denosumab protects bone.

Secondary hyperparathyroidism (high PTH with normal or low calcium) is most often vitamin D deficiency or CKD and is treated by correcting the cause. Hypoparathyroidism follows neck surgery in about 75% of cases and shows low calcium with low or normal PTH and normal magnesium; look for a Chvostek sign (facial twitch when the facial nerve is tapped) and a Trousseau sign (carpal spasm under a blood pressure cuff). It is treated with oral calcium (1.5–3 g elemental a day) and calcitriol, with IV calcium gluconate for severe symptoms.
