Quick start and backup (US SPR 2024)

Start any method on any day if you are reasonably certain the patient is not pregnant: no signs of pregnancy and one of: 7 days or less since normal menses began; no sex since last normal menses; correct, consistent use of a reliable method; 7 days or less after an abortion or miscarriage; within 4 weeks postpartum; or under 6 months postpartum, amenorrheic and fully or nearly fully breastfeeding.

Try one

Women's Health and Prenatal Care practice question

A 42-year-old woman, G2P1, at 33 weeks of gestation has a blood pressure of 150/96 mm Hg and, 4 hours later, 152/98 mm Hg. She was normotensive before pregnancy. Urine protein-to-creatinine ratio is 0.5. She has no headache, visual change or abdominal pain. Which additional finding would indicate preeclampsia with severe features?

  1. Platelet count of 92,000/microL
  2. A 24-hour urine protein of 5.4 g
  3. Serum creatinine of 1.0 mg/dL
  4. Repeat blood pressure of 158/106 mm Hg

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Thrombocytopenia below 100,000/microL is a severe feature and raises concern for HELLP syndrome.
  • B. Massive proteinuria above 5 g was removed from the severe-feature criteria because the amount of protein does not predict maternal or fetal outcomes well.
  • C. Renal insufficiency as a severe feature is a creatinine above 1.1 mg/dL or a doubling without other renal disease. A value of 1.0 is still below that.
  • D. Severe-range pressure is systolic 160 or higher or diastolic 110 or higher. A reading of 158/106 is just below both thresholds.

Two blood pressures of 140/90 mm Hg or higher at least 4 hours apart after 20 weeks, with a protein-to-creatinine ratio of 0.3 or more, establish preeclampsia. ACOG Practice Bulletin 222 lists severe features: systolic 160 or diastolic 110 mm Hg or higher on two readings 4 hours apart, platelets below 100,000/microL, liver transaminases twice normal or severe persistent right upper quadrant pain, creatinine above 1.1 mg/dL or doubling, pulmonary edema, new headache unresponsive to medication, or visual disturbances. Proteinuria amount and fetal growth restriction no longer define severity. Any severe feature changes management to inpatient care: before 34 weeks a stable patient may be managed expectantly with antenatal corticosteroids and delivery at 34 weeks, sooner if she worsens, so the NP arranges same-day obstetric evaluation.

Sources: ACOG PB 222: Preeclampsia (2020); AAFP: Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy (2016)

Menopause and hormone therapy

  • Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes and genitourinary syndrome, and prevents bone loss.
  • Under 60 or within 10 years of menopause, no contraindication: benefit outweighs risk.
  • Uterus present: add a progestogen to systemic estrogen. Low-dose vaginal estrogen alone needs none.
  • No arbitrary stop age; reassess periodically.
  • Contraindications: unexplained vaginal bleeding, liver disease, estrogen-sensitive cancer (including breast), CHD, stroke, MI, VTE, high inherited clot risk.
  • Nonhormonal: fezolinetant 45 mg daily or elinzanetant 120 mg at bedtime; both need liver tests before starting.
Exam tip Starting after 60 or more than 10 years out raises absolute risks of CHD, stroke, VTE and dementia. Transdermal estrogen has not been tied to VTE in observational studies. FDA is removing the heart, breast cancer and dementia language from the boxed warning (2025); the endometrial cancer box for estrogen alone stays.

Drugs in pregnancy and lactation, postpartum depression

  • Letter categories (A, B, C, D, X) are gone from labels since 2015; read sections 8.1 Pregnancy, 8.2 Lactation and 8.3 Reproductive potential.
  • Contraindicated in pregnancy: isotretinoin (iPLEDGE, two methods), warfarin (except some mechanical valves), methotrexate for non-cancer use, valproate for migraine prevention, ACE inhibitors and ARBs, live vaccines.
  • Also avoid in pregnancy: statins (stop in most); oral fluconazole; tinidazole; paroxetine and fluoxetine when possible. No breastfeeding on methotrexate.
  • Hypertension in pregnancy: labetalol or nifedipine (methyldopa also used); treat chronic hypertension at 140/90 or higher.
  • Postpartum depression: symptoms beyond 2 weeks (blues start in 2-3 days and clear by about 10). Screen with EPDS or PHQ-9 in pregnancy, postpartum and at well-baby visits. Ask about mania and suicide.
  • Treatment: CBT or interpersonal therapy; an SSRI if moderate to severe; zuranolone 50 mg nightly with fat-containing food for 14 days (no driving for 12 hours after a dose).

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Reading the MEC: estrogen, clots and the postpartum weeks

The U.S. Medical Eligibility Criteria (CDC, 2024) rate each method for each condition from 1 to 4. Categories 1 and 2 mean the method can be used; 3 means it is usually not recommended unless more suitable methods are unavailable or unacceptable, and needs careful judgment; 4 means it should not be used. Most category 4 entries for combined hormonal contraception (pill, patch and ring) share one mechanism: estrogen raises the risk of venous and arterial thrombosis. That is why migraine with aura (a stroke risk), smoking at 35 or older (15 or more cigarettes a day is 4, fewer is 3), BP of 160/100 or higher (140-159/90-99 and adequately controlled hypertension are 3), thrombophilia, a history of VTE at higher recurrence risk, ischemic heart disease, stroke, complicated valvular disease, SLE with positive or unknown antiphospholipid antibodies and major surgery with prolonged immobilization rule it out.

Progestin-only methods avoid most of this. Migraine with aura is category 1 for the implant, DMPA and the POP, and most cardiovascular conditions are category 2. DMPA is the exception within the group: it is category 3 for multiple cardiovascular risk factors, BP 160/100 or higher, vascular disease, ischemic heart disease, stroke, a VTE history at higher recurrence risk off anticoagulation, and SLE with antiphospholipid antibodies (where the 2024 tables put the implant, POP and LNG-IUD at 2 and the copper IUD at 1). Current breast cancer is category 4 for every hormonal method, the LNG-IUD included; breast cancer with no evidence of disease for 5 years is 3. The copper IUD has no hormone and is category 1 in breast cancer.

Other combined-method entries boards use: decompensated cirrhosis, hepatocellular adenoma and liver cancer are 4; symptomatic gallbladder disease is 3; enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants and rifampin are 3 because they lower contraceptive effectiveness; a malabsorptive bariatric procedure is 3 for pills (the patch and ring are 1); diabetes with nephropathy, retinopathy or neuropathy, or diabetes over 20 years, and multiple cardiovascular risk factors are 3 or 4 by severity; drospirenone POPs are 4 with hyperkalemia in CKD with nephrotic syndrome or on dialysis.

IUD entries are about infection and the uterus, not clots. Current PID, purulent cervicitis, chlamydia or gonorrhea, unexplained vaginal bleeding before evaluation, and cervical or endometrial cancer awaiting treatment are 4 to start but 2 to continue an IUD already in place; pelvic TB is 4 to start and 3 to continue. A distorted uterine cavity, postpartum sepsis and placement just after a septic abortion are 4. Nulliparity and adolescence are 2, so neither is a reason to withhold an IUD. Before placement, screen for STIs per the usual guidelines but do not delay placement waiting for results.

Starting, switching and late doses

The 2024 Selected Practice Recommendations remove barriers: methods can be started on the day of the visit if the clinician is reasonably certain the patient is not pregnant (the six criteria in the one-pager, which together have a negative predictive value of 99-100%). A negative urine test alone is not enough when none of the criteria is met, and routine pregnancy testing for everyone is not needed. Blood pressure is the only measurement needed before combined methods; if it cannot be measured, readings from other settings may be used. The implant needs no exam or tests in a healthy patient; an IUD needs a bimanual exam and cervical inspection.

Backup rules follow how fast each method suppresses ovulation or thickens mucus. The copper IUD works at once. The LNG-IUD and DMPA need no backup if started in the first 7 days of bleeding; the implant and combined methods in the first 5 days; otherwise 7 days of abstinence or condoms. Norethindrone and norgestrel POPs need 2 days of backup after day 5. The drospirenone POP needs none only if started on the first day of bleeding, otherwise 7 days.

Late and missed doses. A combined pill is 'late' if under 24 hours overdue and 'missed' once 24 hours or more have passed. One late or missed pill (under 48 hours): take it now, keep going, no backup. Two or more missed (48 hours or more): take the most recent missed pill, discard the others, and use backup until 7 consecutive days of active pills; if the misses fell in the last week of active pills, finish the active pills and start a new pack without the hormone-free days; if they fell in the first week and there was unprotected sex in the previous 5 days, consider emergency contraception (not ulipristal). A norethindrone or norgestrel POP is missed when more than 3 hours late; a drospirenone POP is missed when 24 hours or more late. DMPA is given every 13 weeks; up to 2 weeks late (15 weeks) needs no backup. Later than that, give it if pregnancy is reasonably excluded, add 7 days of backup, and consider emergency contraception other than ulipristal.

DMPA carries a boxed warning for loss of bone density that grows with duration and may not fully reverse; the label advises against use longer than 2 years unless other methods are inadequate. MEC still rates DMPA as 2 from menarche to under 18 years, so the warning is a counseling point, not a ban. Return of fertility is delayed: in the label's US study the median time to conception after the last injection was 10 months. The label also describes meningioma reported with long-term use.

Emergency contraception in more depth

Offer emergency contraception to anyone with unprotected or inadequately protected sex in the past 5 days who does not want a pregnancy. The copper IUD can be placed within 5 days of the first unprotected act (or later, up to 5 days after an estimated ovulation) and then serves as regular contraception. Ulipristal 30 mg and levonorgestrel 1.5 mg (or 0.75 mg twice, 12 hours apart) are equally effective within 3 days; between days 3 and 5 ulipristal is more effective, and levonorgestrel pregnancy rates rise at 4-5 days. The combined (Yuzpe) regimen is less effective and causes more nausea. MEC gives no category above 2 for emergency pills: they are 1 in breastfeeding, migraine and repeated use, and 2 in obesity, severe cardiovascular disease, severe liver disease and with CYP3A4 inducers.

Ulipristal is an antiprogestin, so starting a progestin method at the same time may weaken it. Start hormonal contraception no sooner than 5 days after ulipristal (a visit-dependent method such as an implant or DMPA may be started at once after weighing the risks), with backup for 7 days after starting or until the next menses, whichever is first. After levonorgestrel, any method can start the same day with 7 days of backup. Nonhormonal methods can start immediately after either. A pregnancy test is advised if there is no withdrawal bleed within 3 weeks.

Cervical screening, breast problems

The USPSTF final statement (2018) screens with cytology every 3 years from 21 to 29, and from 30 to 65 allows cytology every 3 years, high-risk HPV testing alone every 5 years, or cotesting every 5 years. It recommends against screening before 21 regardless of sexual history, after 65 when prior screening was adequate and risk is not high (adequate means 3 negative cytology results in a row or 2 negative cotests in a row in the past 10 years, the latest within 5 years), and after a hysterectomy that removed the cervix for a benign reason. It does not apply to people with prior high-grade lesions or cancer, in utero DES exposure, or immunocompromise such as HIV, who need closer follow-up. The December 2024 draft keeps the ages but makes primary HPV testing every 5 years the preferred test at 30-65 and adds patient-collected HPV samples, which are about as accurate as clinician-collected ones.

Breast screening (USPSTF 2024): mammography every 2 years from 40 to 74 (B). Evidence is insufficient (I) at 75 and over and for supplemental ultrasound or MRI in dense breasts. The statement covers women at average or moderately increased risk (a first-degree relative, dense breasts) but not BRCA carriers, those with chest radiation at a young age, or prior breast cancer or high-risk lesions.

A palpable mass needs a clinical breast exam and imaging, which comes before biopsy. Ultrasound is preferred under 30 and diagnostic mammography at 40 and over; between 30 and 39 neither is clearly better and many guidelines use the 40-and-older pathway. BI-RADS 0 needs more imaging and 4 and 5 need tissue: image-guided core needle biopsy is preferred to fine-needle aspiration. A simple cyst may be aspirated, a complicated (BI-RADS 3) cyst followed or aspirated, and a complex (BI-RADS 4) cyst biopsied. MRI is generally not used for a palpable mass.

Nipple discharge that is bilateral, from several ducts and not bloody is usually physiologic; galactorrhea calls for a pregnancy test, prolactin, TSH and a medication review. Discharge that is spontaneous, one-sided, bloody or accompanied by a mass is pathologic and needs diagnostic imaging (ultrasound under 30, mammography at 30 and over). Cyclic breast pain, about two-thirds of mastalgia, needs no imaging when screening is current and the exam is normal; topical diclofenac is first-line, while caffeine avoidance, evening primrose oil and vitamin E are not supported.

Abnormal uterine bleeding

Abnormal uterine bleeding is a symptom, classified by PALM-COEIN: structural causes (polyp, adenomyosis, leiomyoma, malignancy and hyperplasia) and nonstructural ones (coagulopathy, ovulatory dysfunction, endometrial, iatrogenic, not otherwise classified). Begin with the pattern (frequency, duration, regularity, volume), a speculum and bimanual exam, and an up-to-date cervical screen. Every patient gets a pregnancy test and a CBC. TSH, prolactin or androgens are checked when the history points to them. Heavy bleeding since menarche, bleeding after childbirth or procedures, or easy bruising suggests a bleeding disorder such as von Willebrand disease; normal platelets, PT and PTT do not exclude it. Transvaginal ultrasound is the first imaging test; saline-infusion sonohysterography finds intracavitary lesions better.

Sample the endometrium (office biopsy first) at 45 or older, and in younger patients with long unopposed estrogen exposure (for example chronic anovulation or obesity), persistent bleeding or failed medical treatment. Bleeding after menopause is a separate problem that always needs evaluation for endometrial cancer.

Medical treatment by effect on blood loss: the LNG-IUD (20 mcg a day) reduces it most (71-95%), continuous oral progestins about 87% but with poor long-term satisfaction, combined pills 35-69% (and they regulate anovulatory cycles), tranexamic acid 26-54% and NSAIDs 10-52%, both taken only during bleeding and usable while trying to conceive. For acute heavy bleeding in a stable patient: high-dose oral estrogen or combined pills, oral progestins or IV tranexamic acid; an unstable patient needs tamponade, IV estrogen, D&C or embolization.

PCOS and amenorrhea

The 2023 international PCOS guideline keeps the Rotterdam framework in adults: two of hyperandrogenism (clinical or biochemical), ovulatory dysfunction and polycystic ovarian morphology, after other causes are excluded. AMH can now replace ultrasound for the ovarian criterion in adults, but neither is needed when irregular cycles and hyperandrogenism are both present. In adolescents both irregular cycles and hyperandrogenism are required, and neither ultrasound nor AMH is used. Irregular cycles 3 or more years after menarche mean cycles under 21 or over 35 days, or fewer than 8 a year. Measure total and free testosterone (or the free androgen index); androgen tests are unreliable on a combined pill, which has to be stopped for at least 3 months if testing is essential.

Every patient with PCOS has glucose status checked at diagnosis (the 75-g OGTT is the most accurate test, regardless of BMI) and every 1-3 years by risk, a lipid profile at diagnosis, blood pressure yearly, and screening for depression and anxiety and for sleep apnea symptoms. Treatment: healthy lifestyle first; combined pills for hirsutism and irregular cycles; metformin for weight and metabolic outcomes, especially at BMI 25 or more; letrozole as the first-line drug for ovulation induction in anovulatory infertility.

Amenorrhea: always exclude pregnancy first, then check FSH, LH, prolactin and TSH, adding testosterone and DHEA-S for hyperandrogenism, a morning 17-hydroxyprogesterone for late-onset CAH, karyotype or imaging as directed. Causes by level: hypothalamic (functional hypothalamic amenorrhea from low energy availability, exercise or stress, overlapping the female athlete triad), pituitary (prolactinoma, medications, Sheehan syndrome), ovarian (primary ovarian insufficiency, Turner syndrome, chemotherapy or radiation), and outflow tract (Müllerian agenesis, imperforate hymen, transverse septum, Asherman syndrome). A progestin challenge (medroxyprogesterone 10 mg for 10 days) shows whether estrogen is present but predicts poorly.

Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea is treated by restoring energy balance; combined pills do not improve bone density and are not used for that purpose, and a DXA scan is considered after 6 months of amenorrhea. Primary ovarian insufficiency is diagnosed under 40 with two menopausal-range FSH levels at least a month apart; offer a karyotype and FMR1 premutation testing, give hormone therapy until the natural age of menopause (about 50-51) to protect bone and the heart, and provide contraception, because about 10% may still conceive.

Menopause and hormone therapy in more depth

The 2022 NAMS (now The Menopause Society) position statement: hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms and the genitourinary syndrome of menopause, and prevents bone loss and fracture. Risks depend on type, dose, duration, route, timing and whether a progestogen is used. For women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause onset without contraindications, the benefit-risk ratio is favorable for bothersome hot flashes and for preventing bone loss. Starting after 60 or more than 10 years out is less favorable because of higher absolute risks of coronary disease, stroke, VTE and dementia.

A woman with a uterus on systemic estrogen needs a progestogen to prevent endometrial hyperplasia and cancer. Low-dose vaginal estrogen for genitourinary symptoms does not need a progestogen, and vaginal DHEA or oral ospemifene are alternatives when over-the-counter lubricants and moisturizers fail. The absolute risks are rare (under 10 per 10,000 a year): VTE and gallbladder disease with both regimens, plus stroke and breast cancer with estrogen-progestogen. Breast cancer risk becomes detectable after 3-5 years of estrogen-progestogen; with estrogen alone it did not rise over 7 years in the WHI. Transdermal estrogen has not been linked to VTE in observational studies, but no randomized comparison exists. There is no arbitrary age to stop; longer use needs a documented reason, shared decisions and periodic review. Women with premature or early menopause should take hormone therapy at least until the average age of menopause unless contraindicated.

In November 2025 FDA announced that it would remove the cardiovascular, breast cancer and probable dementia language from the boxed warning on menopausal hormone products, keep the endometrial cancer boxed warning for systemic estrogen alone, and label starting systemic therapy within 10 years of menopause or before 60. Labels are being revised product by product.

Nonhormonal options for moderate to severe hot flashes: fezolinetant (an NK3 receptor antagonist, 45 mg once daily) carries a boxed warning for liver injury; check liver tests before starting (do not start if ALT, AST or bilirubin is 2 times normal or higher), then monthly for 3 months and at 6 and 9 months, and stop for symptoms of liver injury. It is contraindicated in cirrhosis, severe renal impairment and with CYP1A2 inhibitors. Elinzanetant (an NK1 and NK3 antagonist, 120 mg at bedtime) causes somnolence and dizziness, needs liver tests before starting and at 3 months, interacts with CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers, and is contraindicated in pregnancy.

Vaginitis details

Bacterial vaginosis is diagnosed clinically by 3 of the 4 Amsel criteria (thin homogeneous discharge, clue cells, pH above 4.5, fishy odor with 10% KOH) or by Gram stain (Nugent 7-10). Recommended treatment is metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for 7 days, metronidazole 0.75% gel once daily for 5 days, or clindamycin 2% cream at bedtime for 7 days; alternatives include oral clindamycin, clindamycin ovules, secnidazole 2 g once and tinidazole. Partners are not treated. Treat symptomatic pregnant patients; asymptomatic BV is not screened for in pregnancy. For repeated recurrences, suppressive metronidazole gel twice weekly for more than 3 months is an option.

Vulvovaginal candidiasis has a normal pH (under 4.5) and shows budding yeast, hyphae or pseudohyphae on a saline or KOH mount; culture if the mount is negative but symptoms persist, and do not treat Candida found without symptoms. Uncomplicated infection: fluconazole 150 mg once or a short topical azole course. Complicated infection (recurrent, severe, non-albicans, or in diabetes, immunocompromise or steroid use) needs culture and longer treatment. Recurrent means 3 or more symptomatic episodes in under a year: 7-14 days of topical azole or fluconazole every third day for 3 doses, then fluconazole weekly for 6 months. In pregnancy only a 7-day topical azole is recommended; oral fluconazole, even one 150 mg dose, is avoided because of possible links to miscarriage and birth defects.

Trichomoniasis: NAAT is preferred, since wet mount sensitivity is only 44-68% and falls further within an hour of collection. Women take metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for 7 days; men take metronidazole 2 g once, and tinidazole 2 g once is the alternative. Treat all current partners (expedited partner therapy where state law allows) and abstain until both are treated. Retest women about 3 months after treatment. Metronidazole is low risk in pregnancy; tinidazole is avoided in pregnancy, and breastfeeding is deferred 72 hours after a 2 g tinidazole dose. CDC found no convincing evidence of a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol.

Prenatal care in more depth

Care that begins by 10 weeks improves outcomes (ideally 6-10 weeks). The traditional schedule of 10-12 visits remains standard; an AHRQ review found no evidence of harm from fewer visits or from mixing in telehealth, though the evidence base is small. The first visit sets the baseline: weight, height, BMI and BP; a urine culture for asymptomatic bacteriuria (a single organism over 100,000 CFU/mL is treated, because untreated bacteriuria progresses to pyelonephritis in about 30%); ABO, RhD and antibody screen; rubella and, without a history, varicella immunity; universal screening for HIV (opt-out), syphilis, hepatitis B (HBsAg in every pregnancy) and hepatitis C (every pregnancy, except where prevalence is under 0.1%); and chlamydia and gonorrhea under 25 or at risk, with a third-trimester retest for those groups. At every visit: BP, weight and fetal heart tones; fundal height is measured and plotted at each visit from 20 weeks.

Aneuploidy screening is offered to everyone regardless of age; cell-free DNA is the most sensitive screen, and a positive screen leads to chorionic villus sampling at 11-13 weeks or amniocentesis at 15 weeks or later. TSH is checked only with thyroid history or symptoms. Gestational diabetes screening is a one-time test at 24 weeks or later (USPSTF B; evidence before 24 weeks is insufficient). The two-step method is a nonfasting 50-g challenge (positive at 130-140 mg/dL or more at 1 hour) followed by a 100-g 3-hour OGTT needing 2 abnormal values (Carpenter-Coustan 95, 180, 155, 140); the one-step 75-g OGTT is diagnostic with one value at or above 92 fasting, 180 at 1 hour or 153 at 2 hours. Patients at risk for preexisting diabetes are tested at the first visit.

Syphilis is tested at the first visit and again at 28 weeks and delivery for those at risk by geography or behavior (some states require third-trimester testing for everyone). RhD-negative patients get 300 mcg of anti-D immune globulin at 28 weeks and again within 72 hours of delivery if the infant is RhD positive. Group B strep culture is collected at 36-37 weeks for intrapartum prophylaxis; it is not repeated in a patient who had GBS bacteriuria in this pregnancy. Total weight gain targets by prepregnancy BMI: under 18.5, 28-40 lb; normal, 25-35 lb; overweight, 15-25 lb; obesity, 11-20 lb. Depression and anxiety are screened in the first and third trimesters.

Vaccines: Tdap in every pregnancy at 27-36 weeks, early in that window for the best antibody transfer; inactivated influenza in any trimester during the season; one dose of Abrysvo at 32 0/7-36 6/7 weeks from September through January in most of the continental US (not after 36 6/7 weeks, and not repeated in later pregnancies), or nirsevimab for the infant instead; hepatitis B if not already vaccinated. MMR, varicella and live attenuated influenza vaccine are contraindicated in pregnancy and given after delivery; HPV doses wait until after pregnancy.

Low-dose aspirin (81 mg daily from 12 weeks, ideally before 16, until delivery) is recommended for anyone with one high-risk factor: prior preeclampsia, multifetal gestation, chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes, kidney disease, or autoimmune disease such as SLE or antiphospholipid syndrome. Moderate risks include nulliparity, BMI over 30, a mother or sister with preeclampsia, Black race (a marker of social, not biologic, risk), low income, age 35 or more, a prior adverse pregnancy outcome or an interval over 10 years, and IVF; 2 or more moderate risks may also justify aspirin. Folic acid 400-800 mcg daily starts at least a month before conception; 4 mg daily from 3 months before conception is used after a neural-tube-defect pregnancy or with folate antagonists such as carbamazepine, methotrexate or trimethoprim.

Gestational dating

The due date has traditionally been set at 280 days after the first day of the last menstrual period; Naegele's rule is the shortcut: subtract 3 months and add 7 days (then add a year if needed). It assumes a reliable LMP and a regular 28-day cycle, and up to 40% of people recall the LMP wrongly. ACOG, AIUM and SMFM (Committee Opinion 700) make a first-trimester ultrasound, up to 13 6/7 weeks, the most accurate way to set or confirm the date; pregnancies from assisted reproduction use the ART-derived date. Once the LMP, the first accurate ultrasound or both are known, the due date is set, discussed and documented, and later changes should be rare. A pregnancy without an ultrasound confirming or revising the date before 22 0/7 weeks is considered suboptimally dated.

Ultrasound accuracy falls as pregnancy advances, so the discrepancy that justifies changing the LMP date widens: more than 5 days up to 8 6/7 weeks, more than 7 days from 9 0/7 to 15 6/7, 10 days from 16 0/7 to 21 6/7, 14 days from 22 0/7 to 27 6/7, and 21 days from 28 weeks on. Ultrasound also takes precedence when the LMP is unknown or unreliable.

Hypertensive disorders and ectopic pregnancy

Chronic hypertension is BP of 140/90 or higher before 20 weeks (or persisting beyond 12 weeks postpartum). Gestational hypertension is new hypertension after 20 weeks without proteinuria or severe features; about half of those diagnosed at 24-35 weeks go on to preeclampsia. Preeclampsia is new hypertension after 20 weeks (140/90 or higher on two readings at least 4 hours apart) with proteinuria (300 mg or more in 24 hours, or a protein/creatinine ratio of 0.3 or more; quantitative testing is preferred to dipstick) or, without proteinuria, any severe feature. HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets) complicates about 20% of preeclampsia with severe features and can occur with normal BP. Eclampsia can occur before, during or after delivery (28% postpartum), often after a headache or visual change.

Management (AFP 2024): treat chronic hypertension to below 140/90, starting or adjusting medication at 140/90. Labetalol, nifedipine and methyldopa are the usual oral agents; ACE inhibitors and ARBs are contraindicated (fetal growth restriction, oligohydramnios, neonatal renal failure). Gestational hypertension or preeclampsia without severe features is delivered at 37 weeks; preeclampsia with severe features is managed in the hospital with magnesium sulfate and, if preterm, antenatal corticosteroids, with expectant management to 34 weeks only if mother and fetus are stable. After a hypertensive disorder, monitor BP closely for the first 72 hours postpartum and recheck 7-10 days after discharge (sooner with symptoms). Preeclampsia is a risk factor for later cardiovascular disease.

Ectopic pregnancy: risk factors include prior ectopic, tubal surgery or pathology, PID, infertility, smoking, age over 35 and pregnancy with an IUD in place, but about half of patients have none. Suspect it with bleeding or lower abdominal pain in early pregnancy when no intrauterine pregnancy has been confirmed. An intrauterine pregnancy on transvaginal ultrasound essentially excludes it (except heterotopic pregnancy). A viable intrauterine pregnancy starting below 1,500 mIU/mL rises at least 49% in 48 hours in 99% of cases; a slower rise or a fall suggests ectopic or failing pregnancy. For a desired pregnancy, use a discriminatory level as high as 3,500 mIU/mL to avoid interrupting a viable pregnancy.

Intramuscular methotrexate (50 mg/m²) is the medical option: a single dose usually when hCG is below about 3,600 mIU/mL, a two-dose regimen considered at higher levels, especially above 5,000; cutoffs are not firmly established. Contraindications include renal insufficiency, moderate to severe anemia, leukopenia or thrombocytopenia, liver disease or alcohol use disorder, active peptic ulcer and breastfeeding; get a CBC and metabolic panel first. Measure hCG on days 1, 4 and 7: a fall of at least 15% from day 4 to day 7 means weekly checks until undetectable. Patients avoid folic acid and NSAIDs, alcohol, intercourse and strenuous exercise, and know that rupture remains possible until hCG is gone. Refer for surgery urgently with instability, peritoneal signs, an extrauterine embryo with cardiac activity or substantial free fluid, and also for an initial hCG above roughly 5,000-10,000, a mass over 4 cm, an inadequate fall or unreliable follow-up.

Medications in pregnancy and lactation

The Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (effective June 30, 2015) removed the A, B, C, D and X letters from prescription labels. Section 8.1 (Pregnancy) gives a risk summary, clinical considerations, the data and any pregnancy exposure registry; 8.2 (Lactation) covers the amount in milk and effects on the breastfed infant; 8.3 (Females and Males of Reproductive Potential) covers pregnancy testing, contraception and fertility. Boards now expect reasoning from these narrative sections, not a letter.

  • ACE inhibitors and ARBs: contraindicated in pregnancy (fetal growth restriction, oligohydramnios, neonatal renal failure).
  • Statins: FDA removed the blanket pregnancy contraindication in 2021, but most patients should stop once pregnant; exposure before pregnancy is recognized is unlikely to harm the fetus. Breastfeeding is not recommended while taking one.
  • Antifungals and nitroimidazoles: oral fluconazole is avoided in pregnancy; metronidazole is low risk in pregnancy and passes into milk at low levels; tinidazole is avoided in pregnancy.
  • Methotrexate: boxed warning for embryo-fetal toxicity; contraindicated in pregnancy when used for non-cancer conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis or psoriasis. Contraception for 6 months after the last dose (3 months for a male partner); no breastfeeding during treatment and for 1 week after. It is also a folate antagonist.
  • Isotretinoin: boxed warning, contraindicated in pregnancy and dispensed only through the iPLEDGE REMS: two forms of contraception (at least one primary) from 1 month before to 1 month after treatment, unless abstinent or not able to conceive, with two negative pregnancy tests at least 30 days apart before the first prescription and one within 7 days before each later prescription. A progestin-only minipill does not count.
  • Warfarin: contraindicated in pregnancy except for high-risk patients with mechanical heart valves; not detected in breast milk, but watch the breastfed infant for bruising or bleeding.
  • Valproate: boxed warning for neural tube defects and lower IQ after exposure in utero; contraindicated for migraine prevention in pregnancy and in those who can become pregnant without effective contraception; for epilepsy or bipolar disorder, used in pregnancy only when other drugs have failed.
  • Antidepressants: paroxetine and fluoxetine are avoided in pregnancy when possible; sertraline, citalopram and escitalopram have not been linked to birth defects. Watch a breastfed infant for poor feeding, irritability or lethargy.
  • Elinzanetant is contraindicated in pregnancy (contraception during use and for 2 weeks after); zuranolone requires effective contraception during treatment and for 1 week after.

Postpartum care and depression

Postpartum care starts with contact within 3 weeks of delivery and a full assessment by 12 weeks. After gestational diabetes, a 75-g 2-hour OGTT at 4-12 weeks postpartum, then screening every 1-3 years. Progestin-only methods may start immediately; estrogen-containing methods wait (category 4 under 21 days).

Peripartum depression affects about 12-14% of births. DSM-5 requires onset within 4 weeks of delivery, but most experts count onset any time in pregnancy through 12 months postpartum. Baby blues begin within 2-3 days and resolve by about 10 days; symptoms lasting beyond 2 weeks are depression. Risk factors include prior depression, abuse, an unplanned pregnancy, traumatic birth and low income. Screen with the EPDS or PHQ-9 at prenatal and postpartum visits; AAP advises screening the birthing parent at the 1-, 2-, 4- and 6-month well-child visits. Ask about mania (for example with the Mood Disorder Questionnaire), because bipolar disorder changes treatment, and send anyone actively suicidal or psychotic for immediate psychiatric care.

Prevention: the USPSTF (B, 2019) recommends counseling such as CBT or interpersonal therapy for pregnant and postpartum people at increased risk; an update is in progress. Treatment: psychotherapy is the mainstay; an SSRI is added for moderate to severe depression. Zuranolone, an oral neuroactive steroid approved for postpartum depression in adults, is taken as 50 mg once each evening with fat-containing food for 14 days, alone or with an antidepressant; it is Schedule IV, carries a boxed warning against driving or hazardous activity for at least 12 hours after each dose, and appears in breast milk at low levels (relative infant dose about 0.36%).
