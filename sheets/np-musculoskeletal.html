Try one

Musculoskeletal and Rheumatology Primary Care practice question

A 24-year-old woman felt a pop in her right knee when she planted her foot and pivoted during a soccer game yesterday. The knee swelled within 2 hours. She can walk four steps, has no tenderness of the patella or fibular head and can flex the knee past 90 degrees, so radiographs are not indicated by the Ottawa knee rule. Which examination maneuver is MOST sensitive for the injury most likely present?

  1. McMurray test with rotation of the flexed knee
  2. Lachman test
  3. Pivot shift test
  4. Anterior drawer test at 90 degrees of flexion

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. McMurray testing looks for a meniscal tear, which can accompany an ACL injury, but it does not assess ligament stability and is less suited to this mechanism and rapid effusion.
  • B. Correct. A noncontact pivot with a pop and rapid hemarthrosis suggests an ACL tear, and the Lachman test, done at 20 to 30 degrees of flexion, is the most sensitive examination for it.
  • C. The pivot shift is highly specific for ACL tear but has low sensitivity in an awake patient with a painful, guarded knee.
  • D. The anterior drawer tests the same ligament but is less sensitive, especially acutely, because hamstring guarding and the effusion limit tibial translation at 90 degrees.

Noncontact deceleration or pivoting with a pop, inability to continue play and an effusion within a few hours (hemarthrosis) point to an anterior cruciate ligament tear. The Lachman test is the most sensitive maneuver (about 85%), and the pivot shift is the most specific. Meniscal tears usually swell more slowly, over a day, and cause joint-line tenderness, locking or catching. The Ottawa knee rule orders radiographs for age 55 or older, isolated patellar tenderness, fibular head tenderness, inability to flex to 90 degrees or inability to bear weight for four steps. MRI confirms the tear.

Sources: AAFP: Knee Pain in Adults and Adolescents, the Initial Evaluation (2018); Merck Manual Professional: Overview of Fractures (2025)

Ankle sprain and the Ottawa ankle rule

  • Ankle X-ray: pain in the malleolar zone plus bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus, or inability to bear weight for 4 steps right after injury and at the visit.
  • Foot X-ray: midfoot pain plus tenderness at the navicular or the base of the fifth metatarsal, or the same 4-step failure.
  • Sensitivity is close to 100%: a negative rule means no film. Specificity is only 30% to 50%.
  • Inversion injures the lateral ligaments: anterior drawer tests the ATFL, talar tilt the calcaneofibular ligament.
  • Treat functionally: brief protection, ice, an air-stirrup or lace-up brace, early motion, then neuromuscular rehabilitation.
Exam tip Pain above the ankle with a positive squeeze or external rotation test is a high (syndesmotic) sprain: slower recovery, and MRI is more accurate than X-ray.

Lupus clues and the ANA

  • Think SLE in a woman of 15 to 44 with fatigue, arthralgia, rash (malar or photosensitive), oral ulcers, pleuritic pain, Raynaud, cytopenias or an abnormal urinalysis.
  • ANA is the screen: sensitive (about 95%) but not specific, so test only with clinical suspicion. Do not repeat a positive ANA.
  • If positive: anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith (specific), anti-RNP, antiphospholipid antibodies, C3 and C4, CBC, metabolic panel, urinalysis.
  • Classification: ANA 1:80 or higher is the entry criterion, then 10 or more weighted points.
  • Drug-induced lupus (procainamide, hydralazine): antihistone antibodies.
  • Every SLE patient: hydroxychloroquine, no more than 5 mg/kg of actual body weight a day, plus sun protection.
Safety Hydroxychloroquine retinal damage is irreversible: baseline eye exam in the first year, then yearly (from year 5 if low risk). Anti-Ro/SSA in pregnancy carries a 1% to 3% risk of congenital heart block.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Back pain: sorting the few serious causes from the many benign ones

Most acute low back pain is nonspecific and settles on its own, so the visit is about finding the rare serious cause and keeping the patient moving. AFP's 2018 red flag table grades the clues. Strong clues: new urinary retention, overflow or fecal incontinence, saddle anesthesia, reduced anal tone or deficits across several nerve roots (cauda equina); a history of metastatic cancer; a spinal procedure in the past 12 months, fever or a wound over the spine (infection); significant trauma for the patient's age (fracture). Intermediate clues include unexplained weight loss, IV drug use, immunosuppression and long-term corticosteroid use. Age over 70 and osteoporosis are weak fracture clues. Red flags are imperfect, so they are weighed together, not counted one by one.

Imaging follows the clues. Without red flags, imaging is not recommended for nonspecific back pain; AFP 2018 suggests considering it after about 6 weeks of failed conservative care, and AFP 2025 after 8 weeks of persistent pain, when labs can be added. When red flags are present, MRI is the preferred test (CT if MRI cannot be done); plain films are kept for trauma, including low-energy trauma in a patient at risk for osteoporotic fracture. The VA/DoD guideline, summarized in AFP 2023, found that early MRI without red flags or neurologic deficit raised the probability of surgery 13-fold, and early imaging is linked to more opioid use and cost.

The straight leg raise (pain down the leg below 60°) is sensitive for lumbar disk herniation (0.80) but not specific (0.40), so a negative test helps rule it out. The crossed straight leg raise is the opposite: specific (0.90), insensitive (0.35). Radiculopathy without red flags is still treated conservatively first; NSAIDs do not beat placebo for radicular pain, and a short steroid course helps radicular pain only slightly (AFP 2025; AFP 2018 found no benefit over placebo).

Treatment: ACP 2017 (strong recommendation) starts acute and subacute pain with nonpharmacologic care: superficial heat, massage, acupuncture or spinal manipulation; if a drug is wanted, an NSAID or a skeletal muscle relaxant. Muscle relaxants beat placebo but carry adverse effects, and adding cyclobenzaprine to naproxen adds nothing. For chronic pain, ACP puts exercise, rehabilitation, acupuncture, mindfulness, tai chi, yoga and CBT first, then NSAIDs, then tramadol or duloxetine, and opioids only after those fail. Acetaminophen, benzodiazepines, gabapentin and systemic steroids do not help (VA/DoD). Reassurance that the course is usually benign, with advice to stay active, is itself effective.

Osteoarthritis: the ACR 2019 ladder

OA is a clinical diagnosis: activity-related joint pain that is worst after rest (gelling), stiffness under 30 minutes, bony enlargement, crepitus, and normal ESR and CRP. Heberden nodes sit at the DIPs, Bouchard nodes at the PIPs, and the first CMC joint is a common hand site; hip OA limits internal rotation. Rheumatic panels are not ordered without signs of inflammation because false positives are common. X-rays, if needed, show joint space narrowing and osteophytes, and weight-bearing films are used for the knee.

  • Strongly recommended: exercise for every OA site; weight loss for knee or hip OA with overweight; self-management programs; tai chi (knee, hip); a cane or a tibiofemoral brace when the joint limits walking; a hand orthosis for first CMC OA.
  • Drugs, strong: topical NSAIDs for knee OA, considered before oral NSAIDs because exposure is lower; oral NSAIDs for any site, at the lowest dose for the shortest time, watching GI, cardiovascular and renal effects; intra-articular glucocorticoid for knee or hip (image-guided for the hip).
  • Drugs, conditional: acetaminophen, duloxetine, tramadol; topical capsaicin for the knee only.
  • Against: other opioids (conditional), glucosamine (strong), chondroitin for knee or hip (strong), hyaluronic acid (conditional for the knee, strong for the hip), PRP and stem cells (strong), TENS (strong), fish oil and vitamin D (conditional), bisphosphonates, hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate and TNF or IL-1 blockers (strong).

Rheumatoid arthritis: diagnosis and the first year

RA peaks between 30 and 50 and is more common in women, smokers and those with a family history. It causes symmetric pain, swelling and stiffness of the wrists, MCPs and PIPs, sparing the DIPs and the lumbar spine, with morning stiffness over an hour and often fatigue or anemia. RF is moderately sensitive and specific (69%, 85%); anti-CCP is about as sensitive and far more specific (67%, 95%), so a positive anti-CCP carries more weight. Early disease is often seronegative: in one new-onset cohort only 44% were RF positive and 39% anti-CCP positive. The 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria classify RA at 6 or more of 10 points from joint count (0–5), serology (0–3; high positive is over 3 times normal), acute-phase reactants (0–1) and symptoms for 6 weeks or more (1).

Start a DMARD as soon as RA is diagnosed; treating within about 3 months of onset roughly doubles the chance of sustained remission. ACR 2021 strongly prefers methotrexate monotherapy for DMARD-naive patients with moderate to high activity, over hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine or a biologic alone, and strongly recommends treat-to-target. For low activity it suggests hydroxychloroquine first. The Trexall label starts RA at 7.5 mg once weekly; ACR suggests reaching at least 15 mg weekly within 4 to 6 weeks, with oral before subcutaneous. Give folic or folinic acid, check blood counts, liver tests and renal function at baseline and periodically, and counsel about alcohol and liver disease, which raise hepatotoxicity. Methotrexate is contraindicated in pregnancy for nonmalignant disease; contraception continues 6 months after the last dose in women and 3 months in men.

ACR 2021 conditionally prefers starting a csDMARD without short-term glucocorticoids and strongly advises against 3 months or more of steroids, so any bridge should be short and low. If methotrexate at the maximum tolerated dose does not reach target, add a biologic or a JAK inhibitor rather than triple therapy (conditional). Before a biologic or JAK inhibitor, screen for latent TB, hepatitis B and hepatitis C, and update vaccines. Avoid TNF inhibitors in NYHA class III or IV heart failure. JAK inhibitors carry an FDA boxed warning for cardiovascular events and thrombosis in patients 50 and older with cardiovascular risk factors. Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in RA.

Gout: diagnosis and long-term care

A typical flare is a hot, red, swollen joint, often the first MTP, reaching maximum within a day. Risk factors are male sex, obesity, hypertension, diuretics, CKD, alcohol (beer most), meat, seafood and fructose drinks. The diagnosis is usually clinical; aspirate the joint when infection is possible or the diagnosis is uncertain. Urate crystals are negatively birefringent; positively birefringent crystals mean CPPD (pseudogout). Serum urate can be normal or low during a flare, so it is not measured then. Flares usually resolve in 1 to 2 weeks.

Flares: colchicine, NSAIDs and glucocorticoids are equally first line (strong), chosen by comorbidity. Use low-dose colchicine (label: 1.2 mg at the first sign, 0.6 mg an hour later, no more than 1.8 mg in that hour). Colchicine interacts with strong CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibitors (clarithromycin, ketoconazole, most protease inhibitors, cyclosporine) and with grapefruit; the combination is contraindicated with renal or hepatic impairment, and myopathy is more likely with statins or fibrates. If the patient cannot take oral drugs, use IM, IV or intra-articular steroid. IL-1 blockers are reserved for when the others fail or are contraindicated.

Urate-lowering therapy: allopurinol is first line for everyone, including CKD stage 3 or worse (strong). Start at 100 mg/day or less (the label gives 50 mg/day at eGFR 30–60) and raise it, by 100 mg weekly per the label in normal kidney function, until urate is under 6 mg/dL; doses above 300 mg are often needed, up to 800 mg/day. Febuxostat starts at 40 mg; its label carries a boxed warning for higher cardiovascular death than allopurinol and restricts it to patients who cannot take or do not respond to allopurinol, and ACR suggests switching if the patient has cardiovascular disease. With azathioprine or mercaptopurine, allopurinol requires cutting their dose to a third or a quarter, and febuxostat is contraindicated. Probenecid is not used in CKD stage 3 or worse.

Lifestyle advice (all conditional): limit alcohol, purines and high-fructose corn syrup, and lose weight if overweight. Switch hydrochlorothiazide to another drug when feasible and prefer losartan for blood pressure. Do not stop low-dose aspirin taken for a good indication, do not add vitamin C, and do not switch lipid drugs to fenofibrate for gout.

Knee and ankle injuries in more detail

Pretest probability matters in the knee: at a 10% pretest probability, a positive Lachman raises ACL tear to about 58% and a negative one drops it to about 2%. McMurray is specific (97%) but insensitive (52%), so a negative test does not rule out a tear; the Thessaly test is preferred for the first meniscal exam when pain allows. Joint-line tenderness barely changes the probability. Normal anterior drawer laxity is no more than 6 to 8 mm. A hot knee without trauma suggests gout, pseudogout, an inflammatory flare or infection, and a suspected septic joint is aspirated for Gram stain, culture and cell count.

The Ottawa knee rule is 97% to 100% sensitive and cuts knee films by 28% to 35%; the Pittsburgh rule (age under 12 or over 50, fall or blunt trauma, cannot take 4 steps) is a little more sensitive. Knee films are otherwise kept for pain over 6 weeks, using AP, lateral and patellar views, and weight-bearing views when OA is suspected. MRI is for suspected surgical lesions, locking, or swelling that persists after a trial of care. Patellofemoral pain is a dull anterior ache worse with stairs and prolonged sitting.

In Bachmann's meta-analysis of 15,581 patients, a negative Ottawa ankle rule had a likelihood ratio of 0.08 for the ankle and midfoot, and 0.07 in children, so fewer than 1.4% of rule-negative patients had a fracture at a 15% prevalence; the rule cuts films by 30% to 40%. AFP 2025 suggests delaying a detailed ligament exam to 4 to 7 days after injury, limited immobilization for 5 to 10 days within a functional plan, 8 to 12 weeks of neuromuscular rehabilitation, and a brace during sport for up to 12 months to prevent recurrence. Acetaminophen, NSAIDs and opioids give similar pain relief. A previous sprain is the greatest risk for another; reassess significant sprains at 4 to 6 weeks for instability, and image (MRI if films were negative) when recovery is slower than expected.

Shoulder: cuff, impingement and frozen shoulder

Rotator cuff disease causes pain with overhead use, pain lying on the shoulder and a painful arc of abduction between about 60° and 100°. Patients over 40 are more likely to have partial or full tears. Test accuracy (AFP 2008): Hawkins 72% sensitive and 66% specific; empty can (supraspinatus) 44% and 90%; external rotation strength (infraspinatus) 42% and 90%; lift-off (subscapularis) 62% and 100%; drop arm 27% and 88%. Weakness on the empty can and external rotation tests plus a positive impingement sign gave a 98% probability of a tear. MRI findings need context: 54% of people over 60 without symptoms have cuff tears.

Radiographs (AP, scapular Y and axillary) are reasonable for chronic shoulder pain; MRI or ultrasound is used when the result would change care. First-line care for tendinopathy is rehabilitation focused on scapular stabilization, at home or in a clinic, with a subacromial steroid injection for short-term relief to allow therapy. Persistent symptoms after 6 months may justify shock wave therapy, PRP or referral.

Adhesive capsulitis affects about 3% of people, mostly aged 40 to 70, more often women and people with diabetes or prediabetes (check fasting glucose if diabetes is not known) and thyroid disease. Active and passive motion are both lost in every plane, most in external rotation, while cuff strength is preserved; a patient with a cuff tear is weak but can usually be moved passively, which is the bedside distinction. Films are normal. Acetaminophen and NSAIDs come first; an intra-articular steroid gives better short-term relief than oral prednisone, and physical therapy plus an injection beats therapy alone. Pain and stiffness can last 1 to 2 years and about 10% never regain full motion. Refer if there is little improvement after 6 to 12 weeks.

Elbow, wrist and heel

Lateral epicondylitis is an overuse tendinopathy at the origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis and affects 1% to 3% of people a year, more often from work than sport. Tenderness is about 1 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle, with pain on resisted wrist extension, supination, gripping and resisted middle finger extension. Eccentric exercise has short- and long-term benefit; a counterforce strap or wrist brace eases pain. Steroid injections help for under 6 months, repeated injections do more harm, and AFP 2025 advises using them rarely because recurrence is high. Medial epicondylitis involves the flexor-pronator origin; resisted pronation is the most sensitive test, and about 60% have an ulnar neuropathy as well.

Carpal tunnel syndrome is the most common upper-limb entrapment: pain and paresthesia in the thumb, index and middle fingers, often waking the patient, sometimes relieved by shaking the hand. Risk factors include BMI over 25, repetitive or forceful wrist work, female sex, diabetes, arthritis, pregnancy and hypothyroidism. Phalen (57% sensitive, 58% specific) and Tinel (36%, 75%) add little; hypalgesia in the median distribution is more specific (88%), and thenar atrophy is a late, specific sign of severe disease. A classic presentation needs no testing; nerve conduction studies are for atypical cases or to grade severity, and a normal study does not rule it out. NSAIDs and acetaminophen do not help. Mild to moderate disease: a neutral splint (night-only is as good as full-time) or a steroid injection, which helps for up to 6 months and reduces surgery at 1 year. Objective weakness or sensory loss: offer surgical release.

Plantar fasciitis is the most common adult heel pain: sharp medial heel pain worst with the first steps in the morning, easing with activity and returning later in the day. Limited ankle dorsiflexion is the strongest risk factor in nonathletes, along with BMI over 27, long standing and running. Tenderness sits at the anteromedial calcaneus; the windlass test is specific (100%) but insensitive (32%). Image only after 3 months without response; heel spurs are common and not diagnostic. About 80% improve within 12 months with stretching, ice massage, NSAIDs, strengthening and heel padding; off-the-shelf orthoses work about as well as custom ones. Shock wave therapy is an option for chronic cases.

Fibromyalgia, PMR and GCA in more detail

Fibromyalgia (2016 ACR revision): generalized pain in at least 4 of 5 regions, a widespread pain index of 7 or more with a symptom severity score of 5 or more (or an index of 4 to 6 with a severity score of 9 or more), and symptoms at a similar level for at least 3 months. The diagnosis stands regardless of other diagnoses, but joint swelling, inflammation or deformity should prompt a search for a second condition. A CBC, metabolic panel and TSH are reasonable for fatigue; RF and ANA are not ordered without rheumatologic features. Aerobic exercise at moderate intensity, started low and built gradually, has the strongest evidence, followed by CBT, education, tai chi and yoga. Duloxetine, milnacipran and pregabalin are FDA approved; amitriptyline and cyclobenzaprine also help. Give a drug at least 3 months and continue a good response for at least 12. Opioids lack benefit and worsen hyperalgesia; NSAIDs are no better than placebo.

PMR is a clinical diagnosis in a patient 50 or older: at least 2 weeks of bilateral shoulder or pelvic girdle pain, morning stiffness over 45 minutes, constitutional symptoms in up to half, and a raised ESR or CRP in about 90%. Prednisone 12.5 to 25 mg/day usually brings improvement within 2 to 4 weeks (the 2025 EULAR update gives 15 to 25 mg). Taper to 10 mg/day over 4 to 8 weeks, then by 1 mg every 4 weeks. Up to half relapse; raise the dose 10% to 20% and retaper. Methotrexate 7.5 to 10 mg weekly helps patients at high risk of relapse.

GCA affects people over 50, most often of Northern European descent. Clues are a new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw or tongue claudication (39% sensitive, 98% specific), visual symptoms, an abnormal temporal artery, and PMR symptoms (about half of GCA patients). ESR is often 40 to 60 and CRP markedly high. Start prednisone 40 to 60 mg/day (ACR 2021 defines high dose as 1 mg/kg up to 80 mg) the same day; use pulse IV steroids for threatened vision. The 2025 EULAR update asks for specialist assessment within 24 hours. Temporal artery biopsy (one side first, more than 1 cm long) should be done within 2 weeks of starting steroids; ultrasound, MRI or PET can support the diagnosis if the biopsy is negative. ACR 2021 suggests adding tocilizumab to steroids, and aspirin only for critical carotid or vertebral narrowing. The taper typically lasts 1 to 5 years.

Lupus: the workup and long-term care

SLE is about 10 times more common in women and twice as common in Black patients as in White patients; it is most often diagnosed between 15 and 44. Fatigue is the most common symptom; arthralgia or myalgia occurs in up to 95%, skin lesions in 70% to 80% and renal involvement in about half, while a malar rash is a presenting feature in only about 31%. ANA by immunofluorescence is 93% sensitive and 57% specific: positive in nearly everyone with SLE, but also in systemic sclerosis, autoimmune thyroid disease, diabetes, viral infections and many healthy people, so most patients with a positive ANA do not have lupus. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith are the specific antibodies; anti-dsDNA and complement levels track activity. Anti-RNP suggests mixed connective tissue disease. Antihistone antibodies are positive in about 95% of drug-induced lupus, classically from procainamide or hydralazine.

The 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria require an ANA of 1:80 or higher on HEp-2 cells at least once, then add weighted items (2 to 10 points each) from seven clinical domains (constitutional, hematologic, neuropsychiatric, mucocutaneous, serosal, musculoskeletal, renal) and three immunologic domains (antiphospholipid antibodies, low complement, anti-dsDNA or anti-Smith); 10 or more points classify SLE, with 96.1% sensitivity and 93.4% specificity in validation. They are classification criteria for research, but boards use them to show which findings carry weight.

Hydroxychloroquine is for every patient with SLE: it reduces flares and constitutional symptoms and may take 2 to 8 weeks to work. Keep the dose at or below 5 mg/kg of actual body weight a day. The label asks for a baseline eye exam (visual fields and spectral-domain OCT) within the first year, yearly exams for higher-risk patients, and yearly exams from year 5 for low-risk patients; it also warns of cardiomyopathy, QT prolongation and hypoglycemia. Keep prednisone at 7.5 mg/day or less, or stop it. Screen for nephritis with a urinalysis and creatinine every 3 to 6 months. Patients with antiphospholipid antibodies avoid estrogen-containing contraception. Anti-Ro/SSA or anti-La/SSB in pregnancy carries a 1% to 3% risk of congenital heart block, and fetal echocardiography is recommended from 16 to 26 weeks.

The pediatric hip, knee and spine

DDH (AAP 2016 report, reaffirmed 2021): the newborn and infant exam is the screen; aim to find subluxation or dislocation by about 6 months. Ortolani gently abducts the flexed hip to reduce a dislocated femoral head (a clunk); Barlow adducts and pushes back to see whether a reduced hip can be dislocated. A positive Barlow warrants re-examination and referral if the instability persists; restricted or uneven abduction, uneven folds and a short leg are the later signs. Risk factors are breech presentation in the third trimester, family history, previous instability and tight swaddling with the legs straight (AAP); female sex and firstborn status are classic additions (AFP 2006). Universal ultrasound is not recommended; ultrasound is an option under 6 months (selective for high-risk infants from 6 weeks), and AP and frog-leg pelvis films after 4 months. DDH is not an emergency, but an orthopedist manages treatment, ideally within a few weeks of a positive Ortolani. Undiagnosed DDH can present later as a limp with a positive Trendelenburg or Galeazzi sign.

Limping child: transient synovitis is the most common nontraumatic cause (in one series of atraumatic limp, median age 4.4; often after a viral illness): the child is afebrile and well, with some hip motion and normal or mildly abnormal labs. Septic arthritis is the one to catch: the Kocher predictors are fever over 38.5 °C, refusal to bear weight, ESR over 40 mm/h and WBC over 12,000; a CRP over 2 mg/dL adds risk. Suspected septic arthritis needs urgent ultrasound-guided aspiration. Loss of internal rotation is a sensitive sign of any intra-articular hip problem.

Legg-Calvé-Perthes is idiopathic osteonecrosis of the femoral head, usually in boys (4 to 5 times more often) aged 4 to 8, bilateral in 10% to 24%. It presents over weeks with a mild or painless limp, sometimes knee pain, and lost abduction and internal rotation. Early films can be normal; AP and frog-leg views later show sclerosis, fragmentation and flattening, and MRI finds early disease. Refer every suspected case; onset after 6 and more head deformity predict a worse result.

SCFE affects about 11 per 100,000 children, usually 8 to 15 (mean 13.5 in boys, 12 in girls), and 63% are at or above the 90th weight percentile. Pain may be felt only in the thigh or knee (first symptom in 15%). Loss of internal rotation is the most telling sign, and the thigh rolls into external rotation when the hip is flexed (Drehmann sign). Order films of both hips (AP and frog-leg for a stable slip, cross-table lateral for an unstable one), because 18% to 50% become bilateral. Stop weight-bearing (crutches or wheelchair) and refer urgently for in situ fixation; never attempt reduction. Look for an endocrine cause (hypothyroidism, growth hormone, hypogonadism) when the child is under 8 or over 15, thin or short. Unstable slips carry up to a 50% risk of osteonecrosis.

Osgood-Schlatter is traction apophysitis of the tibial tubercle in active children aged 10 to 14 after a growth spurt: anterior knee pain with jumping and kneeling, and a tender, swollen, prominent tubercle; up to 30% are bilateral. It is a clinical diagnosis (films only if atypical) and self-limited: activity modification, ice, acetaminophen or NSAIDs, and stretching or physical therapy. Sinding-Larsen-Johansson disease is another knee apophysitis and is managed the same way.

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is a Cobb angle of 10° or more without another cause; 1% to 3% of children aged 10 to 16 have one, and girls are more likely to progress to large curves. USPSTF (2018; a 2025 scan found no new evidence) gives an I statement for screening ages 10 to 18. AAOS, SRS, POSNA and AAP recommend screening girls at 10 and 12 and boys at 13 or 14 by trained staff. The Adams forward bend test, with or without a scoliometer (5° to 7° of trunk rotation is the usual referral threshold), is the office screen; combined tests were most accurate. Bracing may slow progression of mild to moderate curves.
