In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Reading the CBC

Anemia has traditionally meant a hemoglobin under 13 g/dL in men and under 12 g/dL in women; AFP 2018 notes newer limits that vary with age, sex and race, so treat the cut point as a guide. The MCV sorts the cause: under 80 fL is microcytic, 80 to 100 normocytic and over 100 macrocytic. Microcytosis is classically iron deficiency, but up to 40% of patients with iron deficiency are still normocytic, so Short 2013 advises considering iron deficiency in any anemia unless the MCV is above 95 fL (a cutoff with 97.6% sensitivity). Other microcytic causes are thalassemia, chronic inflammation, lead and sideroblastic anemia. Once iron deficiency is excluded, anemia of chronic disease is the most likely cause of a microcytic or normocytic anemia, and in older adults it is most often from CKD. Macrocytosis points to B12 or folate deficiency, myelodysplasia or malignancy, and B12 deficiency can follow long-term PPI, H2 blocker or metformin use.

The reticulocyte count asks whether the marrow is answering. A reticulocyte index above 2% means increased production, as after bleeding or with hemolysis or hypersplenism; a low index points instead to reduced production. A smear and reticulocyte count come first in macrocytic anemia, and in normocytic or microcytic anemia once iron deficiency and CKD are excluded. For B12, Patel 2025 calls a level under 180 pg/mL deficiency; 180 to 350 is borderline and a raised methylmalonic acid confirms deficiency. Oral B12 1,000 mcg daily is noninferior to intramuscular B12, even in pernicious anemia and after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass; injections are considered for severe deficiency or neurologic symptoms. Periodic B12 checks are reasonable on long-term metformin.

The adult WBC runs 4,500 to 11,000/mm³; leukocytosis is above 11,000. Read the absolute counts, not the percentages: neutrophilia above 7,000, lymphocytosis above 4,500, monocytosis above 880, eosinophilia above 500 and basophilia above 100/mm³. Common reactive causes: infection, stress, surgery, exercise, smoking, obesity, asplenia, and drugs such as corticosteroids, lithium and beta agonists. A leukemoid reaction runs about 50,000 to 100,000/mm³; above 100,000 is almost always leukemia or a myeloproliferative disorder. Features that point to a hematologic malignancy and a hematology referral: fever, night sweats, weight loss, bruising, lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly, cytopenias in other lines, immature cells or a monomorphic lymphocytosis on the smear, or WBC above 30,000/mm³ (above 20,000 after initial management).

Thrombocytopenia is a platelet count under 150,000/µL: mild 100,000 to 150,000, moderate 50,000 to 99,000, severe under 50,000. Platelets that clump in the tube give a falsely low count (pseudothrombocytopenia), so repeat the count in a heparin or citrate tube with a manual count, and review a smear in every patient for schistocytes. Above 50,000 patients are usually symptom free; 20,000 to 50,000 brings easy bruising and petechiae; under 10,000 carries a high risk of spontaneous bleeding and calls for hospital evaluation, as do major bleeding and new neurologic or kidney dysfunction. Drug-induced thrombocytopenia appears 3 to 10 days after exposure and resolves 7 to 10 days after stopping; heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is a fall of more than 50% 5 to 10 days after heparin, with thrombosis as the danger. Hemolysis on the smear with fever, neurologic signs or kidney dysfunction suggests TTP and needs hospital care.

Electrolytes, acid-base and kidney function

The anion gap is Na minus (Cl plus HCO₃). Merck gives a typical value of about 12 mEq/L; albumin carries most of the unmeasured anions, so for each 1 g/dL fall in albumin the expected gap falls by about 2.5, and a normal-looking gap in a patient with low albumin can hide a high-gap acidosis. A high-gap metabolic acidosis means an added acid: ketoacidosis (diabetic, alcoholic, starvation), lactic acidosis, kidney failure, or methanol, ethylene glycol or salicylate. A normal-gap (hyperchloremic) acidosis means bicarbonate loss or failure to excrete acid: diarrhea or ostomy losses, renal tubular acidosis, acetazolamide, or rapid saline infusion. A delta gap (measured gap minus normal gap, added back to the measured bicarbonate) shows whether a metabolic alkalosis is hiding underneath.

The BUN to creatinine ratio helps place a rising creatinine. Above 20 with a normal sediment suggests a prerenal cause (volume loss, heart failure, NSAIDs or ACE inhibitors in a dry patient), along with a urine sodium under 10 and a FENa under 1%. Below 10 with tubular cells and many granular casts, often brown, suggests tubular injury. Urinary indices are unreliable in patients who are not oliguric. BUN rises without any kidney change after GI or other internal bleeding, with glucocorticoids, and with catabolism (surgery, trauma, burns, parenteral nutrition). KDIGO defines AKI as a creatinine rise of 0.3 mg/dL or more within 48 hours, 1.5 times baseline within the prior 7 days, or urine output under 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours.

Hyperkalemia is a potassium above 5.5 mEq/L, but pseudohyperkalemia is a common cause of a high result, most often from a hemolyzed sample; long tourniquet time, fist clenching, very high platelets or very high WBC also release potassium in the tube. Repeat it before acting unless the ECG is abnormal. ECG changes appear above about 5.5 (peaked T waves, longer PR), and above about 6.5 the QRS widens and P waves disappear, ending in a sine wave; levels and ECG changes match poorly between patients. In the office the usual causes are ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs and trimethoprim, especially with CKD.

eGFR, cystatin C and albuminuria (KDIGO 2024)

CKD is an abnormality of kidney structure or function present for more than 3 months. The two everyday markers are an eGFR under 60 mL/min/1.73 m² (G3a to G5) and albuminuria with an ACR of 30 mg/g or more; persistent hematuria, sediment or tubular abnormalities, imaging changes and a kidney transplant also count. A single abnormal eGFR or ACR is not CKD, because it may be AKI: repeat the test, and after an AKI wait 3 months from discharge before diagnosing CKD. KDIGO keeps the threshold of 60 in older adults too, because risk of death, cardiovascular events and kidney failure climbs below it at every age.

Stage by cause, GFR category and albuminuria category together (CGA): G1 90 or more, G2 60 to 89, G3a 45 to 59, G3b 30 to 44, G4 15 to 29, G5 under 15; A1 ACR under 30 mg/g, A2 30 to 300, A3 over 300. G1 and G2 count as CKD only with a marker of kidney damage. Risk rises with both axes, so G3a with A3 carries more risk than G3b with A1. KDIGO suggests a 5-year kidney failure risk from a validated equation (such as the kidney failure risk equation) to guide referral and planning.

Start with creatinine and an equation, never creatinine alone. KDIGO says race should not be used to compute eGFR, and the KDOQI US commentary supports the race-free 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation, the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equation and the 2012 CKD-EPI cystatin C equation for US adults. Creatinine reflects muscle and diet as well as GFR, so add cystatin C when creatinine is likely to mislead and the result changes a decision: very low or very high muscle mass (eating disorders, amputation, paraplegia, body builders, creatine supplements), class III obesity, vegetarian or keto diets, cirrhosis, heart failure, cancer, or a narrow-therapeutic-index drug. A change in eGFR of more than 20% on a later test is beyond the expected variability and needs a look. After starting a hemodynamically active drug (ACE inhibitor, ARB, SGLT2 inhibitor), the line is a fall of more than 30%; KDIGO says to continue an ACE inhibitor or ARB unless creatinine rises by more than 30% within 4 weeks. A doubling of the ACR on a later test also exceeds lab variability.

Test albuminuria with a urine ACR, ideally on a first morning midstream sample; confirm a random ACR of 30 mg/g or more on a first morning sample, and quantify any positive dipstick as an ACR or protein-to-creatinine ratio. Exercise, menstruation, a symptomatic UTI and hematuria all raise the ACR, and low muscle mass (lower urine creatinine) makes it read higher. KDIGO lists these reasons to refer: eGFR under 30, a sustained fall in eGFR of more than 20% (more than 30% after starting hemodynamically active drugs), a 5-year kidney failure risk of 3% to 5% or more, ACR consistently 300 mg/g or more together with hematuria, ACR consistently over 700, RBC casts or more than 20 RBCs/HPF that persist without explanation, hypertension refractory to 4 or more drugs, persistent potassium abnormalities, an uncertain or hereditary cause, or recurrent extensive stones.

Liver tests: injury, pattern and function

ACG 2017 asks that these be called liver chemistries or liver tests, not liver function tests, because ALT, AST and alkaline phosphatase show injury, not function. Function shows in the INR (factor VII has a half-life of about 6 hours, so it moves fast), albumin (under 3 g/dL suggests reduced synthesis, though malnutrition and inflammation also lower it, and its half-life of about 20 days makes it slow) and bilirubin. A truly healthy ALT is 29 to 33 IU/L in men and 19 to 25 IU/L in women, below most laboratory upper limits, and an elevated ALT is linked with higher liver-related mortality.

Hepatocellular injury is ALT and AST raised out of proportion to alkaline phosphatase; cholestatic injury is alkaline phosphatase raised out of proportion to the aminotransferases. The R ratio puts a number on it: divide ALT by its upper limit and alkaline phosphatase by its upper limit, then divide the first by the second. The EASL drug-induced liver injury guideline, as reviewed by Brennan 2022, calls R of 5 or more hepatocellular, 2 or less cholestatic and between 2 and 5 mixed. Levels add clues (Merck): over 500 IU/L suggests acute hepatocellular injury (acute viral hepatitis A or B, drugs or toxins such as acetaminophen, ischemic hepatitis, sometimes a passing duct stone); 300 to 500 fits chronic disease or obstruction; under 300 is nonspecific (MASLD, chronic viral hepatitis, alcohol, cirrhosis). In alcohol-related disease AST is characteristically more than twice ALT, and both usually stay under 300.

For a hepatocellular pattern ACG lists the workup: hepatitis A, B and C, MASLD and alcohol, hereditary hemochromatosis, autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson disease and alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, plus a full history of prescribed, over-the-counter and herbal products. A raised alkaline phosphatase first needs its source confirmed: GGT (or 5'-nucleotidase) rises with it when it comes from the liver, while bone, pregnancy (placenta) and growth in children raise it with a normal GGT; a mild isolated rise in an older adult is usually bone, such as Paget disease. A hepatic alkaline phosphatase then calls for testing for primary biliary cholangitis and primary sclerosing cholangitis. Fractionate a high bilirubin: more than 85% indirect, usually under 6 mg/dL, means increased production (hemolysis) or reduced conjugation (Gilbert syndrome); more than 50% direct means cholestasis or hepatocellular disease.

Glucose, A1C and the lipid panel

CDC and USPSTF give the same cut points as ADA. Diabetes: A1C 6.5% or more, fasting plasma glucose 126 mg/dL or more, 2-hour glucose 200 mg/dL or more after a 75 g load, or a random glucose of 200 or more. Prediabetes: A1C 5.7% to 6.4%, fasting glucose 100 to 125, or 2-hour glucose 140 to 199. USPSTF asks that a diagnosis be confirmed with repeat testing; a random glucose of 200 or more counts on its own only with classic symptoms of hyperglycemia. USPSTF screens adults 35 to 70 with overweight or obesity (BMI 25 or more, 23 or more in Asian Americans), earlier with risk factors, and about every 3 years if normal.

A1C reflects red cell lifespan as well as glucose. Merck lists the pitfalls: A1C reads falsely high with low red cell turnover (iron, B12 or folate deficiency anemia), and falsely normal or low with high turnover (hemolysis, hemoglobin S or C disease, or right after treating a deficiency anemia), in pregnancy, and in cirrhosis or CKD stages 4 and 5. In any of these, diagnose and monitor with plasma glucose.

Lipid results feed a risk decision more than a diagnosis. VA/DoD 2025 recommends at least a moderate-intensity statin for primary prevention with an LDL-C of 190 mg/dL or more; its appendix calls LDL-C above 190 severe hypercholesterolemia and describes familial hypercholesterolemia, which that level should bring to mind. Many labs still calculate LDL-C with the Friedewald equation, which errs as triglycerides rise and depends on whether the sample was fasting. Triglycerides above 500 mg/dL raise the risk of pancreatitis. For triglycerides that stay at 150 or more, look for secondary causes: alcohol, other conditions, and drugs such as hormones, beta blockers, thiazide and loop diuretics, bile acid sequestrants, atypical antipsychotics and isotretinoin. VA/DoD suggests measuring Lp(a) at least once to identify enhanced risk; it gives above 50 mg/dL (125 nmol/L) as an example of an elevated level while noting that this threshold has not improved risk-calculator prediction, that statins and lifestyle do not lower Lp(a), and that a low value does not cancel other risk factors.

Thyroid tests

TSH is the first test. The upper end of normal is about 4.0 to 4.5 mIU/L and the lower end 0.4. A TSH above 4.5 calls for a free T4: low means overt hypothyroidism, normal means subclinical hypothyroidism. Consider treating subclinical hypothyroidism in a nonpregnant adult when TSH is above 10 mIU/L or TPO antibodies are elevated; otherwise repeat TSH in about 6 to 12 months, since it often normalizes on its own. In adults 65 and older with a TSH under 10, treatment may increase mortality. TPO antibodies do not make the diagnosis, but they point to autoimmune disease and predict progression. TSH drifts up with age, so a mild rise in an older adult is not automatically disease.

Levothyroxine (Wilson 2021): the starting dose under age 60 is a full weight-based 1.5 to 1.8 mcg/kg/day; at 60 or older or with known or suspected heart disease, start lower (25 to 50 mcg/day, with 12.5 mcg for higher-risk patients). Recheck TSH every 6 to 8 weeks and change the dose by 12.5 to 25 mcg/day until it is normal. In pregnancy on a stable dose, increase by about 30% (nine doses a week) and check TSH every 4 weeks. A normal or low TSH with a low free T4 is central hypothyroidism (rare; review drugs and evaluate the pituitary) or euthyroid sick syndrome, the abnormal values of severe nonthyroidal illness.

Hyperthyroidism (Kravets 2016): overt disease has a suppressed TSH (under 0.1) with high free T4 and total T3; subclinical disease has a TSH under 0.1 or between 0.1 and 0.4 with normal hormone levels. A normal or high TSH with high T4 and T3 suggests a rare TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma. The radioactive iodine uptake (normal 15% to 25% at 24 hours) and scan name the cause: Graves disease is high and homogeneous, a toxic adenoma is one hot area, a toxic multinodular goiter several, and thyroiditis has very low uptake (0% to 2%). TSH receptor antibodies identify Graves disease when the uptake scan cannot be done.

Urinalysis and microscopy

Specific gravity runs 1.003 to 1.030: below 1.010 suggests a well-hydrated patient, above 1.020 a dry one, and a value fixed at 1.010 suggests intrinsic kidney disease. Urine pH runs 4.5 to 8. Nitrite is specific but not sensitive (sensitivity 19% to 48%, specificity 92% to 100%), because organisms that do not reduce nitrate and a low-nitrate diet give false negatives. Leukocyte esterase is the reverse (sensitivity 72% to 97%, specificity 41% to 86%). Normal urine has fewer than 2 WBCs/HPF in men and fewer than 5 in women; more than 5 WBCs/HPF is the microscopy cutoff AFP uses for UTI (sensitivity 90% to 96%, specificity 47% to 50%). Many squamous cells mean contamination; transitional cells are normal; renal tubular cells mean significant kidney disease.

The dipstick blood pad detects peroxidase activity, which hemoglobin and myoglobin share with intact red cells; dehydration, exercise and menstrual blood also give positives, and vitamin C can give false negatives. At least 20% of positive dipsticks show no red cells under the microscope (AFP 2021), so confirm every positive dipstick with microscopy before working up hematuria; a positive pad with no RBCs suggests hemoglobinuria (hemolysis) or myoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis). Casts localize disease to the kidney: RBC casts mean glomerulonephritis; WBC casts mean kidney inflammation (pyelonephritis, interstitial nephritis, glomerulonephritis); epithelial and pigmented granular casts mean tubular injury; waxy and broad casts mean advanced kidney disease; fatty casts go with nephrotic syndrome; hyaline casts can be normal. Crystals: square 'envelope' calcium oxalate, diamond or barrel uric acid, 'coffin lid' triple phosphate with alkaline urine and Proteus, and hexagonal cystine (diagnostic of cystinuria).

Microscopic hematuria is 3 or more RBCs/HPF on microscopy, and one properly collected specimen is enough (AFP 2021 summary of the 2020 AUA/SUFU guideline). Risk groups guide the workup. Low risk (women under 50 or men under 40, never or under 10 pack-years, 3 to 10 RBCs/HPF, no other urothelial cancer risk): repeat the urinalysis in 6 months, or offer cystoscopy and renal ultrasound. Intermediate risk (women 50 to 59 or men 40 to 59, 10 to 30 pack-years, 11 to 25 RBCs/HPF, or a low-risk patient with hematuria again on repeat): cystoscopy and renal ultrasound. High risk (age 60 or older, more than 30 pack-years, more than 25 RBCs/HPF, or any gross hematuria): cystoscopy and CT urography, or MR urography when contrast cannot be given. Dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts, proteinuria or a falling eGFR point to a glomerular cause and a kidney workup instead (KDIGO, Merck).

Iron studies

Ferritin is the first test for a microcytic or normocytic anemia. Latimer 2025 calls iron deficiency confirmed when ferritin is under 45 ng/mL without inflammation, or 46 to 99 with a transferrin saturation under 20%; with inflammation, ferritin is an acute-phase reactant and under 100 is diagnostic. Older cut points are stricter but miss cases: Short 2013 notes that a ferritin under 15 is consistent with iron deficiency, while a cutoff of 30 raises sensitivity from 25% to 92% with 98% specificity, and that a ferritin of 100 or more generally excludes iron deficiency. When ferritin is indeterminate, low serum iron, low transferrin saturation and a high TIBC support iron deficiency.

Recurrent blood loss causes about 94% of iron deficiency. In a younger woman with heavy menses, treat the bleeding and replace iron. In men and postmenopausal women, Latimer recommends upper and lower endoscopy, because up to 9% have a malignancy, plus noninvasive testing for H. pylori and celiac disease. Oral iron every other day is absorbed better than daily dosing; about half of patients take it inconsistently because of side effects. Reassess at 2 to 4 weeks, and move to IV iron if the patient cannot tolerate oral iron or does not respond.

Hepatitis serologies

CDC recommends that every adult 18 or older be screened for hepatitis B at least once with the triple panel (HBsAg, anti-HBs and total anti-HBc), that pregnant patients be tested for HBsAg in every pregnancy, and that susceptible people be retested periodically while their risk of exposure continues. HBsAg means current infection; IgM anti-HBc separates acute (positive) from chronic (negative). Anti-HBc with anti-HBs is resolved infection, which can reactivate with immunosuppression. Anti-HBs alone after a documented series is vaccine immunity; a level above 10 mIU/mL after the series means protection. All negative is susceptible. An isolated anti-HBc has several explanations (resolved infection with waned anti-HBs, occult infection, a false positive, or maternal antibody in an infant) and needs follow-up rather than reassurance.

Hepatitis C: CDC recommends screening every adult 18 or older at least once and every pregnant patient in every pregnancy, except where prevalence of HCV RNA is under 0.1%. Start with the antibody; a reactive result triggers HCV RNA, ideally from the same draw. Antibody reactive with RNA detected is current infection and needs linkage to treatment; antibody reactive with RNA not detected means no current infection (cleared infection or a false-positive antibody). The antibody can be negative early, so test RNA directly after a possible exposure in the past 6 months. Hepatitis A: IgM anti-HAV is acute infection (Merck).

ECG basics an NP reads

Read in order: rate and rhythm, intervals, axis, voltage, then ST segments, T waves and Q waves. Merck gives a normal QRS of 0.07 to 0.10 s; 0.10 to 0.11 suggests an incomplete bundle branch block or nonspecific delay, and 0.12 s or more a complete bundle branch block. A PR over 0.20 s is first-degree AV block. The normal axis runs from −30° to +90°; −30° to −90° is left axis deviation and +90° to 180° right axis deviation. The QT must be corrected for rate (usually Bazett): Merck's long QT page calls a QTc above 450 ms in men or 470 ms in women prolonged, with torsades risk climbing above 500 ms; drugs (class Ia, Ic and III antiarrhythmics, tricyclics, phenothiazines, some antifungals and antivirals), hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia are the usual office causes.

LVH by voltage (Merck table): Sokolow-Lyon, the S wave in V1 plus the R wave in V5 or V6 of 35 mm or more, or an R wave in aVL of 11 mm or more; Cornell, R in aVL plus S in V3 over 28 mm in men or over 20 mm in women. Potassium shows on the ECG: high potassium gives tall peaked T waves, then a longer PR, loss of P waves, a wide QRS and finally a sine wave; low potassium gives prominent U waves, low T waves and ST depression.

Ischemia (Fourth Universal Definition of MI): new ST elevation at the J point in 2 contiguous leads of 1 mm or more in all leads except V2 and V3. V2 and V3 normally show more elevation: up to 2.5 mm in healthy men under 40, less in older men, and less still in women, so the ischemic threshold there is higher (about 2 mm in men 40 and older, 2.5 mm in men under 40 and 1.5 mm in women). ST depression of 1 mm or more in 6 leads, sometimes with ST elevation in aVR or V1, suggests left main or multivessel disease. Isolated ST depression of 0.5 mm or more in V1 to V3 may mean circumflex occlusion (inferobasal, formerly called posterior, ischemia); record V7 to V9, where 0.5 mm of ST elevation counts (1 mm in men under 40). Hyperacute T waves can precede ST elevation, and deep (over 2 mm) symmetric T inversions in the anterior leads are associated with LAD occlusion. Q waves point to an old infarct most specifically when they are wider than 0.04 s or appear in several lead groups; smaller Q waves (0.02 to under 0.03 s and at least 1 mm deep) suggest prior MI when the T waves in the same leads are inverted. Lesser ST or T changes can still be ischemia in the right clinical setting.

Atrial fibrillation shows no discernible regular P waves and irregular ventricular complexes. ESC 2024 accepts a standard 12-lead ECG (10 s) or 30 s or more on a single-lead or multi-lead device to make the diagnosis. AFP 2024 lists the first workup after the ECG: transthoracic echocardiogram, electrolytes, CBC, and thyroid, kidney and liver function. USPSTF (2022, I statement) found insufficient evidence to screen asymptomatic adults 50 or older.

Spirometry

Check quality first (AFP 2014): exhalation of at least 6 seconds with a plateau, and the two best efforts within 0.2 L for FEV₁ and FVC. Then read the FEV₁/FVC ratio. GOLD 2026 requires a post-bronchodilator ratio under 0.7 to diagnose COPD in a patient with symptoms or exposures, and a normal pre-bronchodilator ratio can exclude COPD. A ratio between 0.6 and 0.8 should be repeated on another day, because it can change with biologic variation; a ratio under 0.6 is very unlikely to rise above 0.7. The fixed ratio over-diagnoses COPD in older adults and under-diagnoses it in some younger adults compared with the lower limit of normal (LLN, the 5th percentile), but GOLD still prefers it for simplicity and suggests the LLN or z scores in adults under 50 with a repeated ratio of 0.7 or more. AFP 2014 uses the ATS lower limit of normal in patients under 65 and keeps the fixed 0.7 ratio for symptomatic patients 65 or older who smoke or smoked.

GOLD grades airflow obstruction by post-bronchodilator FEV₁ % predicted: GOLD 1 is 80% or more, GOLD 2 50% to 79%, GOLD 3 30% to 49%, GOLD 4 under 30%. GOLD recommends the race-neutral GLI-Global reference equations, which ATS and ERS now endorse. PRISm (preserved ratio impaired spirometry) is a ratio of 0.7 or more with a post-bronchodilator FEV₁ under 80% predicted; it is common in smokers and people with obesity and can precede COPD. A normal ratio with a low FVC suggests restriction, but FVC alone predicts restriction poorly: confirm with lung volumes, where a total lung capacity below the LLN defines restriction, and add a diffusing capacity to narrow the cause. Both low is a mixed pattern, or obstruction with air trapping if the FVC normalizes after bronchodilator.

Bronchodilator responsiveness supports asthma: GINA 2026 counts an FEV₁ or FVC rise of 12% or more and 200 mL or more in adults (a PEF rise of 20% or more), and an FEV₁ rise of 12% or more of predicted in children. Asthma is variable, so a normal test does not exclude it; repeat testing when symptomatic, average daily PEF variability over 2 weeks (more than 10% in adults, per GINA), or a bronchial challenge test can show the variability (AFP 2014 calls methacholine positive with a fall in FEV₁ of more than 20% at 4 mg/mL or less). GOLD no longer recommends reversibility testing to guide COPD treatment decisions.

DXA: T scores and Z scores

A T score compares bone density with young adults; the WHO categories apply to postmenopausal women and men 50 or older: −1.0 or higher is normal, between −1.0 and −2.5 is low bone mass (osteopenia), and −2.5 or lower at the hip or lumbar spine is osteoporosis. Measure the femoral neck, total hip and lumbar spine by central DXA; use the one-third radius when arthritis or other structural changes make the hip and spine unreliable. A hip or vertebral fragility fracture establishes osteoporosis without any T score. FRAX adds fracture risk to density: with low bone mass, a 10-year FRAX risk of 20% or more for a major osteoporotic fracture or 3% or more for a hip fracture is the usual treatment threshold.

A Z score compares the patient with people of the same age. ISCD prefers Z scores to T scores in premenopausal women and men under 50: a Z score of −2.0 or lower is 'below the expected range for age', osteoporosis cannot be diagnosed in men under 50 from density alone, and ISCD suggests a report may note that an evaluation for secondary causes of low density may be appropriate. AFP 2023 summarizes the ISCD age band as 20 to 50. USPSTF (2025, grade B) screens women 65 and older and younger postmenopausal women whose risk is raised on a risk tool, and finds insufficient evidence for men. USPSTF sets no repeat interval (repeating at 4 to 8 years added little to fracture prediction in cohort studies), and the American College of Rheumatology's Choosing Wisely item (cited in AFP 2023) advises against routinely repeating DXA more often than every 2 years.

Choosing the first imaging test

Low back pain (AFP 2025): most patients need no imaging, and plain radiographs should not be ordered routinely. Image after trauma, including low-velocity trauma in a patient at risk of osteoporotic fracture. MRI is the preferred test when red flags are present, and CT is the fallback when MRI cannot be done; consider imaging and laboratory tests when pain persists beyond 8 weeks despite treatment. Ankle and midfoot injuries: the Ottawa ankle rules have a sensitivity close to 100% (pooled negative likelihood ratio 0.08, under 1.4% chance of a fracture at a 15% prevalence) and cut radiographs by 30% to 40% (Bachmann 2003).

Acute abdominal pain (AFP 2023): get a pregnancy test in every premenopausal woman, even with reliable contraception. Right upper quadrant pain: ultrasound first, with HIDA or CT if it is equivocal. Right lower quadrant pain or suspected appendicitis: CT with IV contrast in nonpregnant adults; ultrasound first in pregnancy, then MRI if inconclusive (CT if MRI is unavailable, even early in pregnancy). Left lower quadrant, left upper quadrant and generalized pain: CT with IV contrast, though uncomplicated diverticulitis can be diagnosed clinically in an immunocompetent patient. Suprapubic or pelvic pain in a patient with female reproductive organs: pelvic ultrasound. Suspected mesenteric ischemia: CT angiography even with kidney impairment. A patient under 35 with typical symptoms, hematuria, pain relief with analgesics and a prior stone may need no imaging; stone-protocol CT is for those likely to have complications.

Suspected DVT (AFP 2019): score with the Wells rule. Wells 1 or less (low risk): D-dimer, and a negative result ends the workup; positive D-dimer or Wells above 1: Doppler (compression) ultrasound of the symptomatic leg, repeated in 3 to 7 days if negative, with teaching on symptoms of pulmonary embolism. Thyroid nodule (AFP 2020): TSH and thyroid ultrasound with a neck node survey for every nodule; a radionuclide uptake scan only when TSH is low (a hot nodule rarely needs FNA). Hematuria: renal ultrasound or CT urography by risk group, as above.

Test accuracy and predictive values

Sensitivity is the share of people with the disease who test positive (true-positive rate); specificity is the share of people without it who test negative (true-negative rate). Both are properties of the test and stay roughly constant across populations. A highly sensitive test that is negative makes disease unlikely, which is why screening tests and rule-out tools such as D-dimer and the Ottawa rules favor sensitivity; a highly specific test that is positive makes disease likely, which is why confirmatory tests favor specificity. Moving a cutoff trades one for the other.

Predictive values answer the patient's question (do I have it?) and depend on prevalence. Merck's worked example uses a test with 71% sensitivity and 85% specificity in 1,000 women: at 30% prevalence the PPV is 67% and the NPV 87%; at 5% prevalence the PPV falls to 20% and the NPV rises to 98%. The same positive result therefore means far less in a low-risk screening population than in a symptomatic one. Likelihood ratios carry the test's strength to any pretest probability: LR+ is sensitivity ÷ (1 − specificity) and LR− is (1 − sensitivity) ÷ specificity; pretest odds × LR = post-test odds. In the example, LR+ is 4.73 and LR− 0.34, and at 30% pretest probability a positive test raises the probability to 67% while a negative test lowers it to 13%.
