Medications in older adults

  • Aging raises body fat and lowers body water: fat-soluble drugs (diazepam) last longer; water-soluble drugs (digoxin, aminoglycosides) reach higher levels.
  • Phase I liver metabolism slows; phase II (glucuronidation) is less affected. First-pass metabolism falls.
  • GFR falls with age while creatinine can stay 'normal' (less muscle): dose renally cleared drugs by estimated creatinine clearance.
  • Polypharmacy: regular use of 5 or more medications; fall risk rises with each added drug.
Safety Beers Criteria (AGS 2023) classes to avoid in most older adults: first-generation antihistamines and other strong anticholinergics, benzodiazepines and Z-drugs, all sulfonylureas, sliding-scale insulin alone, chronic NSAIDs, muscle relaxants, PPIs past 8 weeks without a clear reason, aspirin for primary prevention, antipsychotics for dementia behaviors, digoxin above 0.125 mg/day. Opioid plus benzodiazepine or gabapentinoid, and 3 or more CNS-active drugs, are combinations to avoid.

Elder abuse and advance care planning

  • Abuse types: physical, sexual, emotional, neglect, abandonment, financial exploitation. About 1 in 10 older adults living at home are abused.
  • Risk: isolation, functional or cognitive impairment, poor health, low income, prior abusive relationship.
  • USPSTF 2025: I statement for screening, which does not mean ignoring signs: interview the patient alone and evaluate any concern. Most states require reporting; in those states, report a reasonable suspicion (usually to Adult Protective Services, or law enforcement if in danger).
  • Advance directive (living will, health care power of attorney) is the patient's document; POLST is a portable medical order for serious illness or advanced frailty.
  • Medicare pays NPs for advance care planning (99497, 99498) with no visit limit.

The Annual Wellness Visit in detail

Medicare offers three different kinds of visit to a new beneficiary, and boards like to test which is which. The Initial Preventive Physical Exam (IPPE, the 'Welcome to Medicare' visit) is available once, within the first 12 months of Part B coverage, and reviews medical and social history and preventive services. The Annual Wellness Visit (AWV) builds a personalized prevention plan and is covered once every 12 months; the first one is billed as the initial AWV (G0438) and later ones as subsequent AWVs (G0439), and neither can be billed within 12 months of the IPPE. A routine head-to-toe physical is not a Medicare benefit at all, and the patient pays for it. The patient pays nothing for the IPPE or AWV when the clinician accepts assignment.

The initial AWV starts with a health risk assessment. CMS lists its minimum contents: demographics and self-rated health; psychosocial risks (depression, life satisfaction, stress, anger, loneliness or isolation, pain, fatigue); behavioral risks (tobacco, activity, nutrition and oral health, alcohol, sexual health, seat belts, home safety); and both basic ADLs and IADLs. The clinician then records medical and family history and current medications and supplements, lists the patient's other providers and suppliers, measures height, weight, BMI and blood pressure, assesses cognition, reviews depression risk with a recognized standardized screening tool (CMS names none; the PHQ-2, PHQ-9 and Geriatric Depression Scale are common choices), and reviews function and safety: ADLs, fall risk, hearing and home and community safety, including driving when it applies.

The visit ends with written output: a screening schedule for the next 5 to 10 years based on USPSTF and ACIP recommendations, a list of risk factors and conditions with the interventions recommended or under way, and personalized advice with referrals (falls prevention, nutrition, physical activity, tobacco cessation, weight loss, social engagement, cognition). Two more elements are required: a review of current opioid prescriptions (risk factors for opioid use disorder, pain severity, non-opioid options) and a review of substance use risk. Advance care planning is optional, at the patient's discretion. The subsequent AWV (G0439) updates all of this; the cognitive assessment is required at every AWV.

CMS does not require a particular cognitive test. The cognitive check can rest on direct observation and on concerns raised by the patient, family, friends or caregivers, and CMS suggests considering a brief cognitive test (it names none; the Mini-Cog is a common choice). The AWV can be furnished by a physician, a nurse practitioner, physician assistant or clinical nurse specialist, or by other health professionals under direct physician supervision, and it can be done by telehealth.

Functional assessment

The Katz Index scores six basic ADLs (bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence and feeding) as independent (1) or dependent (0); 6 is fully independent and 0 is highly dependent. The Lawton scale covers the instrumental ADLs that let a person live alone: using the telephone, traveling, shopping, preparing meals, housework, handyman work, laundry, managing medications and managing money. Each Lawton item is scored 3 (no help), 2 (some help) or 1 (unable), and the score is most useful for following one patient over time.

IADLs need planning, memory and judgment, so they are usually lost before the basic self-care ADLs. A patient who still bathes and dresses alone but has started missing bills, mixing up pill bottles or getting lost driving needs a cognitive evaluation. The distinction matters for diagnosis: mild cognitive impairment is measurable decline without loss of independence in daily function, while dementia (major neurocognitive disorder) is decline that interferes with daily function.

Falls: screening, assessment and prevention

More than 27% of adults 65 and older fall each year, about 10% of them report an injury, and 1-year mortality after a hip fracture may reach 20 to 30% (AFP 2024). The strongest risk factors in a 2023 meta-analysis were a prior fall, a balance disorder, fear of falling and dementia. CDC's STEADI program puts the AGS/BGS falls guideline into three steps: screen, assess, intervene.

Screen yearly and whenever a patient presents after a fall. STEADI offers either the 12-item Stay Independent brochure (at risk with a score of 4 or more) or three questions: has the patient fallen in the past year, does the patient feel unsteady standing or walking, and does the patient worry about falling. A yes to any question means the patient is at risk; for a fall, ask how many times and whether there was an injury. A patient who screens negative gets fall-prevention education, a vitamin D intake review and a referral to a community exercise program, and is rescreened next year.

Assessment looks for modifiable causes. Gait, strength and balance: the Timed Up and Go (the patient rises from an armchair, walks to a line 10 feet away at a normal pace, turns, walks back and sits; 12 seconds or more means fall risk; watch for shuffling, short strides, en bloc turning or steadying on walls), the 30-second chair stand (arms crossed, 17-inch chair; a below-average count for age and sex, or needing the arms, means risk) and the 4-stage balance test (feet together, semi-tandem, tandem, one foot; failing to hold the tandem stand for 10 seconds means increased risk). Orthostatic blood pressure: lying 5 minutes, then standing at 1 and 3 minutes; a systolic drop of 20 mm Hg or more, a diastolic drop of 10 mm Hg or more, or lightheadedness is abnormal. Then review medications, vision (Snellen), feet and footwear, home hazards, vitamin D intake and comorbidities such as depression and osteoporosis.

Each finding has a matching intervention: physical therapy or an evidence-based exercise program such as tai chi for gait and balance; stopping, switching or reducing fall-risk drugs; occupational therapy for home safety; for orthostatic hypotension, medication changes, hydration, foot pumps before standing and compression stockings; eye referral and cataract surgery when indicated, with advice on depth perception and single versus multifocal lenses; podiatry and shoe advice; and vitamin D for deficiency. Follow up in 30 to 90 days. The USPSTF (2024) grades exercise interventions B for community-dwelling adults 65 and older at increased risk and multifactorial programs C. Vitamin D supplementation to prevent falls in people who are not deficient has not reduced falls in a large trial, and the USPSTF is reviewing it separately.

Cognitive screening and evaluation

The USPSTF (2020, update in progress) gives screening community-dwelling adults 65 and older without symptoms an I statement. That is not a recommendation against looking: clinicians should stay alert to early signs, and evaluating a concern raised by the patient, family or clinician is not screening. The Medicare AWV still requires a cognitive assessment, so every Medicare patient who comes for an AWV is checked.

For the Mini-Cog, say three unrelated words and have the patient repeat them; ask for a clock with all the numbers and the hands set to 10 past 11 (move on if it is not done in 3 minutes); then ask for the three words. Each word recalled without cueing scores 1 point, and a normal clock (numbers in order and in roughly the right places, hands pointing to 11 and 2) scores 2; an abnormal or refused clock scores 0. A total below 3 of 5 is the validated cutoff for dementia screening, and below 4 can be used when more sensitivity is wanted. In the 2022 AFP review the Mini-Cog's sensitivity ranged from 76 to 100% and specificity from 27 to 85%, so a positive result calls for a fuller evaluation, not a diagnosis.

AFP 2025 suggests the Mini-Cog, the Memory Impairment Screen or an informant questionnaire to screen, then the MoCA, SLUMS or RUDAS when screening is positive or suspicion stays high. A MoCA score below 26 is the usual threshold for mild cognitive impairment; it catches most dementia but with poor specificity, and no dementia cutoff is established. The reversible-cause workup is CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel, TSH, vitamin B12 and folate, tailored to the patient, with noncontrast MRI (or CT when MRI is unavailable or contraindicated) to exclude secondary causes. Depression and delirium must be excluded. Refer to neurology for onset before age 60, severe behavioral symptoms or an unclear diagnosis.

Delirium

Delirium is an acute disturbance of attention, awareness and cognition that develops over hours to days and fluctuates. Hyperactive delirium (agitation, restlessness) can be mistaken for psychosis or dementia; hypoactive delirium (lethargy, slowing) can be mistaken for depression and is the type most often missed; mixed delirium has features of both. Predisposing factors include age 65 or older, dementia, sensory impairment, polypharmacy and poor function. Precipitants include infection, dehydration, hypoxia, pain, surgery, sleep loss, urinary retention or constipation, metabolic disturbance, withdrawal, and drugs, especially anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids and corticosteroids.

The Confusion Assessment Method has four features: (1) acute onset and fluctuating course, (2) inattention, (3) disorganized thinking, (4) altered level of consciousness. Delirium requires features 1 and 2 plus either 3 or 4; sensitivity is 94 to 100% and specificity 90 to 95%. Knowing the patient's baseline (ask the family) is what makes feature 1 answerable. In a patient with dementia, a sudden change over a day or two is delirium superimposed on dementia and needs a cause found.

Prevention is the most effective treatment. Multicomponent nonpharmacologic programs such as the Hospital Elder Life Program (sleep, orientation, mobility, glasses and hearing aids, hydration) reduce delirium and falls; drugs should not be used to prevent delirium in hospitalized patients. Management is to treat the cause, remove offending drugs, avoid physical restraints and unnecessary catheters, and keep the patient oriented and mobile. No drug is FDA-approved for delirium; an antipsychotic at the lowest dose for the shortest time is reserved for severe agitation with risk of harm after nondrug measures fail (AFP 2023 prefers a second-generation agent over haloperidol). Benzodiazepines worsen delirium and are reserved for alcohol or sedative withdrawal, seizures or end-of-life care. Inpatient mortality with delirium is 25 to 39% after adjusting for age, sex, comorbidity and dementia.

How aging changes drugs

Absorption changes little that matters, with two exceptions: calcium carbonate needs acid, so calcium citrate is better absorbed in patients on PPIs or with low gastric acid, and anticholinergics slow gastric emptying. Distribution changes more. Body fat rises and total body water falls, so lipophilic drugs such as diazepam spread into a larger volume and stay longer (half-lives of diazepam, flurazepam and chlordiazepoxide or their metabolites can reach 96 hours in older patients), while water-soluble drugs such as digoxin and the aminoglycosides reach higher concentrations. Lower albumin raises the free fraction of phenytoin and warfarin.

Hepatic clearance of many drugs falls by about 30 to 40%, with wide individual variation. Phase I reactions (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) slow more than phase II conjugation, which is why a phase II drug is preferred when there is a choice. First-pass metabolism falls about 1% a year after age 40, so nitrates, propranolol, phenobarbital, verapamil and nifedipine carry more risk of toxicity.

Renal function is the change with the most dosing consequences. GFR falls by about 8 mL/min/1.73 m² per decade after age 40, but serum creatinine often stays normal because older adults have less muscle. Use an estimated creatinine clearance (Cockcroft-Gault) to dose renally cleared drugs such as digoxin, the aminoglycosides, lithium, anticoagulants, metformin and gabapentin (drug labels now often dose by eGFR instead). The practical rule follows: start low, go slow, and check levels and kidney function.

Beers Criteria, polypharmacy and deprescribing

The AGS Beers Criteria (2023 update) list potentially inappropriate medications for adults 65 and older in outpatient, hospital and long-term care settings. They do not apply to hospice or end-of-life care. The AGS stresses that a listed drug is potentially, not definitely, inappropriate: the list supports shared decision-making and does not replace it. The criteria have five parts: drugs to avoid in most older adults; drugs to avoid with certain diseases or syndromes; drugs to use with caution; drug-drug interactions to avoid; and drugs to avoid or dose-reduce by kidney function.

Drugs to avoid in most older adults include first-generation antihistamines such as diphenhydramine and hydroxyzine and other strongly anticholinergic drugs (confusion, constipation, retention, falls); benzodiazepines and the Z-drug hypnotics (falls, fractures, delirium, accidents); all sulfonylureas as first- or second-line diabetes drugs and sliding-scale insulin without basal insulin (hypoglycemia); chronic NSAIDs (bleeding, kidney injury, heart failure); skeletal muscle relaxants; metoclopramide; mineral oil by mouth; megestrol; starting systemic estrogen (vaginal estrogen is still acceptable); PPIs beyond about 8 weeks without a clear indication; aspirin for primary prevention; warfarin as the first anticoagulant for AF or VTE and long-term rivaroxaban; and digoxin as first-line therapy for AF or heart failure, or above 0.125 mg/day.

Disease-specific cautions follow the patient's problems. With a history of falls or fractures, avoid anticholinergics, antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs), antiepileptics, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs and opioids unless safer choices are unavailable. With dementia or cognitive impairment, avoid anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs and chronic antipsychotics; antipsychotics for behavioral symptoms of dementia or delirium are used only when nonpharmacologic measures have failed or the patient threatens substantial harm, with periodic attempts to taper. With delirium or high risk of it, avoid anticholinergics, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, opioids, systemic corticosteroids and H2 blockers. With heart failure, avoid cilostazol, avoid nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers in reduced ejection fraction, and avoid NSAIDs, thiazolidinediones and dronedarone in symptomatic heart failure (caution when asymptomatic).

Use with caution: SGLT2 inhibitors (urogenital infections, euglycemic ketoacidosis), prasugrel and ticagrelor (bleeding, especially at 75 and older), long-term dabigatran, and drugs that can cause SIADH or low sodium (diuretics, SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, mirtazapine, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, antipsychotics, tramadol). Interactions to avoid include an opioid with a benzodiazepine or with a gabapentinoid, 3 or more CNS-active drugs together, 2 or more anticholinergics, and warfarin with amiodarone, ciprofloxacin, macrolides other than azithromycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole or SSRIs. Kidney-based rules include avoiding nitrofurantoin and NSAIDs below a creatinine clearance of 30 mL/min.

Polypharmacy is usually defined as regular use of 5 or more medications; patients taking more than four have a higher risk of injurious falls, and the risk rises with each additional drug. Watch for the prescribing cascade, in which a side effect is treated as a new disease. AFP 2019 frames deprescribing as a therapeutic intervention: gather every drug including over-the-counter products and supplements, match each to an indication, identify and prioritize drugs whose harms now outweigh their benefits given the patient's goals, function and life expectancy, agree a plan with the patient, and set a specific follow-up. Treat a new drug as a trial rather than a permanent addition, weigh benefit against risk at each refill, and review the plan at health maintenance visits. Drugs that cause withdrawal or rebound, such as benzodiazepines, are tapered rather than stopped. Explicit tools (Beers, STOPP/START) and implicit tools (Medication Appropriateness Index) help, but none has been shown superior for patient outcomes.

Frailty

Frailty is a syndrome of reduced reserve across several systems that affects 5 to 17% of older adults and raises the risk of falls, disability, hospitalization and death. The Fried phenotype scores five findings: unintentional weight loss (10 lb, or at least 5% of body weight, in the past year), self-reported exhaustion, weak grip strength, slow walking speed and low physical activity. Three or more is frail, one or two is prefrail, none is robust. The Rockwood frailty index instead counts deficits; a score of 0.25 or more suggests frailty. No laboratory test or imaging diagnoses frailty.

Routine screening is not currently recommended; AFP 2021 names comprehensive geriatric assessment as the most effective way to find frail or at-risk patients, using a validated tool, and lists higher-risk groups (for example smokers, socially isolated people, and those with diabetes, COPD, stroke or dementia). Management is individualized to the degree of frailty and the patient's goals. A multicomponent exercise program with resistance and balance training is the core (strength training 2 to 3 nonconsecutive days a week; balance training at least 2 hours a week, supervised at first); protein or nutritional supplements are added for weight loss or undernutrition; polypharmacy is addressed; causes of fatigue and weight loss (depression, anemia, hypotension, hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency) are treated. Vitamin D is given only for deficiency, hormone therapy is not recommended, and no drug is approved to treat frailty.

Urinary incontinence

Incontinence is common and often unreported, so ask. First look for transient causes, which are sudden in onset and reversible: for example infection, medications, delirium and constipation (the TOILETED mnemonic). Classify the chronic type. Stress incontinence is leakage with coughing, sneezing or exercise. Urge incontinence is urgency with frequency or nocturia and leaking on the way to the toilet. Mixed has both; treat the dominant symptom. Overflow (about 5% of chronic incontinence) comes from an underactive detrusor or outlet obstruction, with straining, a weak stream and a sense of incomplete emptying. Functional incontinence is a toileting barrier (cognition, mobility, frailty) with a normal bladder.

Evaluate with a history and a validated questionnaire (the 3 Incontinence Questions sorts stress from urge), a urinalysis in everyone, a 3-day voiding diary, a cough stress test for suspected stress incontinence and a postvoid residual (ultrasound preferred) when retention is possible, a referral is planned, or the patient takes drugs that affect voiding. Refer for pain, hematuria, significant prolapse, prior pelvic surgery or radiation, suspected fistula or a high residual.

Treatment is stepwise. Conservative care comes first for every type: sensible fluid intake (about 50 to 70 oz a day, less before bed, not severe restriction), less caffeine and carbonated drinks, weight loss, smoking cessation, and timed voiding. For stress incontinence, pelvic floor muscle training (three sets of 8 to 10 contractions held 6 to 8 seconds, three to four times a week, for at least 15 to 20 weeks), then a pessary or surgery. For urge incontinence, bladder training (lengthen the interval by 30 to 60 minutes until voiding every 3 to 4 hours), then medication with behavioral therapy: mirabegron (can raise blood pressure) or a selective antimuscarinic. Antimuscarinics are not first-line in older adults; selective agents (darifenacin, solifenacin) are preferred over nonselective ones (oxybutynin, tolterodine) to limit cognitive effects, and the Beers Criteria advise avoiding strong anticholinergics in dementia. Vaginal estrogen may help; systemic estrogen worsens incontinence. Overflow: stop drugs that impair bladder emptying and catheterize when the cause cannot be reversed (in men, see the GU page for BPH). Functional: scheduled and prompted toileting.

Pressure injury

More than 3 million pressure injuries are treated in the US each year; stages 1 and 2 are the most common. Risk rises with limited mobility, moisture from incontinence or drainage, poor nutrition, obesity, diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, neurologic disease or spinal cord injury, a prior pressure injury, and long nursing facility stays. Structured risk tools such as the Braden and Norton scales are widely used but have low accuracy, and no scale has been shown to reduce injuries on its own. Device-related injuries are staged with the same system, naming the device.

  • Stage 1: intact skin with nonblanchable redness (may look different in darker skin).
  • Stage 2: partial-thickness loss with exposed dermis; the bed is pink or red and moist, or an intact or ruptured serum blister. Fat is not visible. Do not use for moisture-associated or incontinence-associated skin damage.
  • Stage 3: full-thickness skin loss with fat visible; granulation tissue and rolled edges are common.
  • Stage 4: full-thickness loss with exposed or palpable fascia, muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage or bone.
  • Unstageable: full-thickness loss hidden by slough or eschar; it will be stage 3 or 4 once the base is seen.
  • Deep tissue pressure injury: persistent nonblanchable deep red, maroon or purple skin, or epidermal separation showing a dark wound bed or a blood-filled blister.
  • Device-related injuries are staged; mucosal injuries are not.

Prevention and treatment share the same pillars: offload pressure (routine repositioning at an interval set by clinical judgment, heels floated with a suspension device or a pillow under the legs, high-specification foam mattresses over standard foam), manage moisture with a pH-balanced cleanser (about 5.5, not alkaline soap) and a moisture barrier, and correct nutrition (for patients at risk or malnourished, 1.25 to 1.5 g/kg/day of protein and 30 to 35 kcal/kg/day). Choose dressings by the wound: hydrogel or hydrocolloid for a noninfected stage 2 with minimal drainage, foam for stage 2 or deeper with moderate to heavy exudate, calcium alginate for stage 3 or 4 with moderate exudate. Debride devitalized tissue, but leave fixed, firm, dry slough or eschar on the heel alone unless infection is strongly suspected. Suspect osteomyelitis when bone is visible or a wound fails to heal, and confirm with MRI or bone biopsy.

Elder abuse: recognize and report

CDC defines elder abuse as an intentional act or failure to act by a caregiver or other trusted person that harms or risks harm to someone 60 or older. The types are physical, sexual, and emotional or psychological abuse, neglect (failure to meet needs for food, shelter, hygiene or medical care), abandonment, and financial exploitation. About 1 in 10 older adults living at home experience abuse, and many cases are never reported. Risk factors in the older adult (USPSTF 2025) include social isolation, functional or cognitive impairment, poor physical health, low income and a history of an abusive relationship; in the caregiver (CDC), high stress, poor caregiving preparation, substance use, mental illness and financial or emotional dependence on the older adult.

The USPSTF (June 2025) found insufficient evidence to recommend for or against screening older or vulnerable adults for caregiver abuse (I statement), and that is not a reason to ignore signs. Clues worth acting on (AFP 2014) include an explanation that does not fit the injury, an unusual delay in seeking care, bruises in atypical places (face, lateral arms, back, abdomen, neck, inner upper arms) or larger than 5 cm, immersion burns, ligature marks, unexplained fractures; in a dependent patient, dehydration, malnutrition, pressure injuries that decline does not explain, poor hygiene, and missing medications or drug levels too low or too high; and a caregiver who keeps answering for the patient. Interviewing the patient alone, when possible, is essential.

Reporting laws vary by state, but most states require reporting, and AFP 2014 notes that clinicians in most states have a legal duty to recognize, report and refer. In those states, follow the statute; the threshold is suspicion, not proof, and the investigation belongs to the agency. Clinicians work with local social services and Adult Protective Services, call law enforcement when someone is in immediate danger, and for facility residents also involve the state agency; the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov, 1-800-677-1116) finds local services. One federal rule applies everywhere: under Section 1150B of the Social Security Act, staff and contractors of long-term care facilities that receive at least $10,000 in federal funds must report a reasonable suspicion of a crime against a resident to the state survey agency and law enforcement, within 2 hours if there is serious bodily injury and otherwise within 24 hours.

Advance care planning

Advance care planning is a voluntary conversation with the patient (and family or surrogate when appropriate) about what care the patient would want if unable to decide. It can lead to an advance directive, the patient's own document: a living will or instruction directive records treatment wishes, and a health care power of attorney or health care proxy names the person who decides when the patient cannot. Forms differ by state and are usually available from the state attorney general. Name the surrogate, record the wishes, and share them with family and other clinicians.

POLST is different. It is a portable medical order, completed with a clinician, for people who are seriously ill or have advanced frailty, and it tells emergency personnel what to do now. Its state names vary (MOLST, POST, MOST, LST and others). A POLST is optional and is meant only for the seriously ill or frail, so a healthy older adult is offered an advance directive, not a POLST.

Medicare pays physicians and NPs, PAs and CNSs for advance care planning with CPT 99497 (first 30 minutes) and 99498 (each additional 30 minutes); a discussion of 15 minutes or less is billed within an ordinary visit. There is no limit on how often it can be billed, but repeat billing should document a change in health or wishes. Documentation covers that the discussion was voluntary, the explanation of advance directives, who was present and the time spent. When done with the AWV, on the same day by the same clinician, the deductible and coinsurance are waived.

Exam pearls

Exam tip AWV: cognitive assessment required, physical exam not. TUG 12 seconds or more: fall risk. Mini-Cog 0 to 2: further evaluation. CAM: acute fluctuating onset and inattention, plus disorganized thinking or altered consciousness. New nighttime confusion after a sleep aid: stop the diphenhydramine, do not swap it for another antihistamine. Normal creatinine in a frail 85-year-old does not mean normal kidney function. Urge incontinence in an older adult with memory loss: bladder training, then mirabegron rather than oxybutynin. Dry, firm heel eschar without signs of infection: leave it. Suspected elder abuse: report suspicion where state law requires it; do not wait for proof.
