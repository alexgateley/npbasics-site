Try one

Genitourinary, Renal and Men's Health practice question

An 84-year-old woman who weighs 50 kg is starting rivaroxaban for newly diagnosed nonvalvular atrial fibrillation. Her serum creatinine is 1.0 mg/dL (reference 0.6 to 1.1 mg/dL), and the laboratory reports a CKD-EPI 2021 eGFR of 56 mL/min/1.73 m2. The rivaroxaban label sets its atrial fibrillation dose by Cockcroft-Gault creatinine clearance. Which value BEST describes her kidney function for choosing the dose?

  1. Creatinine clearance of about 39 mL/min
  2. Normal function, since the creatinine is within the reference range
  3. eGFR of 56 mL/min/1.73 m2 from the laboratory report
  4. Creatinine clearance of about 33 mL/min

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. This is the result when the 0.85 factor for women is left out, a common slip that overestimates clearance by about 15 percent.
  • B. A creatinine in the reference range can hide substantially reduced clearance in an older woman with low muscle mass, because creatinine production falls with age and lean mass.
  • C. Tempting because it is printed on the report, but CKD-EPI eGFR is indexed to body surface area and runs well above Cockcroft-Gault clearance in small, very old patients; here it would place her in the full-dose band.
  • D. Correct. (140 - 84) x 50 x 0.85 / (72 x 1.0) is about 33 mL/min, which places her in the reduced-dose band of a label written around Cockcroft-Gault.

Serum creatinine alone misleads in older adults because creatinine generation falls with muscle mass. Estimating equations correct for this, but they do not agree: CKD-EPI eGFR is normalized to 1.73 m2 of body surface area and is built to stage CKD, while many drug labels, including the direct oral anticoagulants, defined their dose bands using Cockcroft-Gault creatinine clearance with actual body weight. In a small, very old woman the two can differ enough to change the dose. When a label names Cockcroft-Gault, calculate it: subtract age from 140, multiply by weight in kilograms, multiply by 0.85 for women, and divide by 72 times serum creatinine. Recheck kidney function at least yearly on these drugs, and more often when clearance is near a threshold.

Sources: FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); KDIGO CKD Guideline (2024)

Uncomplicated cystitis: the evidence behind the choices

In a nonpregnant woman with dysuria, frequency or urgency and no vaginal discharge, the history is accurate enough to treat without a urinalysis or culture (AFP 2024). Send a culture when the infection recurs, when first-choice treatment fails, when an earlier isolate was resistant, when the presentation is atypical, and in women 65 or older.

The 2010 IDSA update ranked drugs by cure rate and by 'collateral damage', the resistance an antibiotic selects in bowel and vaginal flora. Nitrofurantoin (100 mg twice daily for 5 days) and fosfomycin (3 g once) cause little collateral damage; fosfomycin cures somewhat less often than the standard short courses. TMP-SMX (160/800 mg twice daily for 3 days) is appropriate only where local resistance is 20% or less, or when the organism is known to be susceptible. Pivmecillinam was approved in the US in April 2024 for women 18 and older; the US label dose is one 185 mg tablet three times daily for 3–7 days. AFP 2024 lists 400 mg three times daily for 3 days, which is the European regimen and not the US tablet strength. Fluoroquinolones work in 3 days but are kept for other infections. Beta-lactams such as cefpodoxime or amoxicillin-clavulanate, for 3–7 days, are used only when the others cannot be, and amoxicillin or ampicillin alone should not be used empirically.

The Macrobid label still gives 7 days, but the guideline course is 5. The label limits nitrofurantoin to acute cystitis: it does not reach kidney tissue, so it is wrong for pyelonephritis or any febrile UTI. It is contraindicated with anuria, oliguria or a creatinine clearance under 60 mL/min, at term and in labor (38–42 weeks), and in infants under 1 month because of hemolytic anemia. Long courses can cause pulmonary reactions, hepatitis and peripheral neuropathy.

Men with lower urinary tract symptoms should always get an antibiotic, guided by culture when possible; 7 days of trimethoprim, TMP-SMX or nitrofurantoin is as effective as longer courses in afebrile men (AFP 2024). Fever, perineal pain or a tender prostate changes the diagnosis to prostatitis.

Supportive care: fluids of at least 1.5 L a day, and acetaminophen or an NSAID for pain. A woman with cystitis and no sign of upper tract infection can be given a backup prescription to fill if symptoms have not improved in 48–72 hours. For recurrent UTI, AFP 2024 rates higher fluid intake, cranberry products and methenamine hippurate as effective prevention; antibiotic prophylaxis also works but brings adverse effects and resistance.

Pyelonephritis and complicated UTI

Fever, flank pain or other systemic signs mean the infection has spread beyond the bladder. Always send a urine culture and tailor therapy to it (IDSA 2010). The 2025 IDSA complicated UTI guideline folds pyelonephritis into complicated UTI and sorts empiric choice in four steps: how sick the patient is (sepsis or not), the patient's own resistance risk (avoid any drug a previous urine isolate resisted, and avoid a fluoroquinolone used in the past 12 months), allergies and interactions, and, for sepsis only, a recent local antibiogram.

For an outpatient who can take oral drugs, IDSA 2010 gives ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 7 days or levofloxacin 750 mg daily for 5 days when fluoroquinolone resistance is not known to exceed 10%. Where it may exceed 10%, give one long-acting parenteral dose first, such as ceftriaxone 1 g IV. TMP-SMX 160/800 mg twice daily works if the organism is known to be susceptible. Oral beta-lactams are less effective and need that initial parenteral dose.

Durations are shorter in the 2025 guideline: for a patient improving on effective therapy, 5–7 days of a fluoroquinolone or 7 days of another agent, counted from the first day of effective therapy, instead of 10–14 days. Bacteremic infection that is improving also gets 7 days. Febrile men with suspected acute bacterial prostatitis are the exception; they may need 10–14 days. Switch from IV to oral once the patient is improving, can take oral medication and an effective oral drug exists.

Safety Sepsis, inability to take oral drugs or failure to improve as an outpatient means parenteral therapy. An infected, obstructed kidney is a urologic emergency (AFP 2019 stone review).

Asymptomatic bacteriuria

Asymptomatic bacteriuria is 100,000 CFU/mL or more of one organism in a voided urine without urinary symptoms or signs, whether or not there is pyuria (IDSA 2019). In women it should be confirmed on two consecutive samples; one sample is enough in men. Pyuria alone is not a reason to treat.

Only two groups are screened and treated. Pregnant women are screened early in pregnancy and treated for 4–7 days, using the shortest effective course. Patients about to have an endoscopic urologic procedure that breaches the mucosa are screened, and a culture-directed 1–2 dose course is started 30–60 minutes before the procedure.

Everyone else with bacteriuria and no symptoms is left untreated: children, healthy pre- and postmenopausal women, older adults living at home or in long-term care, people with diabetes, spinal cord injury, short- or long-term catheters, kidney transplant recipients more than 1 month after surgery, other organ transplant recipients, and patients having nonurologic surgery. In an older, functionally or cognitively impaired patient with a fall or delirium and a positive culture but no urinary symptoms and no systemic signs, look for other causes and observe. If the same patient has fever or hypotension without a clear source, broad empiric coverage of urinary and other sources is appropriate.

Febrile UTI in infants and young children

UTI is found in up to 7% of febrile infants. The AAP 2011 guideline for children 2–24 months was retired in May 2021 because its risk assessment used race. Its replacement, published in Pediatrics in October 2026, covers children from 8 days to 5 years with low medical complexity and leaves race out. It asks for evaluation within 72 hours for a child 2 months to 5 years with fever and no source or with urinary symptoms, sooner for higher-risk children.

Collection: culture needs a catheterized or suprapubic specimen, or a midstream clean catch in a toilet-trained child. A bagged specimen has a high false-positive rate and should not be sent for culture (AFP 2020). The 2011 guideline required pyuria plus at least 50,000 CFU/mL of one uropathogen. News reports of the 2026 guideline describe lower thresholds (10,000 CFU/mL for catheter and 100,000 for clean-catch specimens) and removal of nitrites from the diagnostic algorithm; confirm these against the published table.

Treatment: oral antibiotics work as well as IV for pyelonephritis in children (Cochrane review cited by AFP 2020). The 2026 guideline, as summarized by the AAP and reported by Healio, favors a course of 7 days or fewer for most children, tailored to age, severity and response, with longer courses for infants, higher-risk children and those not improving. Infants 8–28 days start on parenteral antibiotics and switch to oral once improving; older children can often take oral therapy from the start. Choose by local resistance; AFP 2020 lists cefixime, cephalexin, cefuroxime and amoxicillin-clavulanate, and notes high resistance to amoxicillin and TMP-SMX in many communities. Nitrofurantoin is not used for febrile UTI.

Imaging and prevention under the 2011 standard (AFP 2020): kidney and bladder ultrasound for children under 24 months after a first febrile UTI; VCUG only when the ultrasound is abnormal or febrile UTI recurs; antibiotic prophylaxis used selectively; a medical review within 48 hours for any later febrile illness. The 2026 guideline's imaging statements could not be read for this page; its summaries say routine prophylaxis is not appropriate for lower-grade reflux and that referral and imaging decisions are shared with the family. Constipation and bladder-bowel dysfunction drive recurrence and should be looked for and treated (both guidelines).

CKD: definition, staging and when to refer

KDIGO 2024 defines CKD as an abnormality of kidney structure or function present for more than 3 months with implications for health. Either an eGFR under 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or a marker of damage qualifies: albuminuria (ACR 30 mg/g or more), urine sediment abnormalities, persistent hematuria, tubular electrolyte disorders, abnormal histology or imaging, or a kidney transplant. eGFR of 60 or more with no damage marker is not CKD. The threshold of 60 applies to older adults too, even without albuminuria. A single abnormal eGFR or ACR, especially soon after AKI, does not prove chronicity: the abnormality must persist beyond 3 months.

CKD is classified by Cause, GFR category and Albuminuria category (CGA). The GFR categories are G1 90 or more, G2 60–89, G3a 45–59, G3b 30–44, G4 15–29 and G5 under 15 (kidney failure). Albuminuria categories are A1 under 30 mg/g, A2 30–300 and A3 over 300. A raised ACR on a random sample is confirmed with a first-morning midstream sample. Risk of every outcome, from kidney failure to heart failure, rises with both a lower G and a higher A.

Creatinine-based eGFR is the starting test. Adding cystatin C (eGFRcr-cys) improves accuracy when creatinine may mislead, for example at extremes of muscle mass. Changes in eGFR within ±20%, and in albuminuria short of halving or doubling, can be random variation. The kidney failure risk equation estimates 5-year risk; a risk over 3–5% supports nephrology referral.

  • Refer adults (KDIGO Figure 48): eGFR under 30; a sustained fall in GFR over 20% (over 30% after starting an ACE inhibitor, ARB or SGLT2 inhibitor); ACR 300 mg/g or more with hematuria, or ACR over 700 mg/g; RBC casts or more than 20 RBC/HPF not otherwise explained.
  • Also refer for an uncertain or hereditary cause, recurrent extensive stones, hypertension refractory to 4 or more drugs, persistent potassium abnormalities, acidosis, anemia, bone disease or malnutrition.
  • Refer children with ACR 30 mg/g or more (or protein-creatinine ratio 200 mg/g or more) confirmed on a first-morning sample, persistent hematuria, any sustained fall in eGFR, hypertension, structural anomalies, known or suspected CKD, or recurrent UTI.

CKD: slowing progression and protecting the heart

KDIGO 2024 treats CKD with layers: lifestyle for everyone, then first-line drugs for most, then targeted drugs. Lifestyle: no tobacco, moderate-intensity activity, a plant-forward diet low in ultra-processed food, sodium under 2 g a day, and protein about 0.8 g/kg a day in G3–G5. Blood pressure: an SBP target under 120 mm Hg when tolerated, individualized for frailty, falls, limited life expectancy or postural symptoms.

Renin-angiotensin inhibitors: start an ACE inhibitor or ARB in diabetes with A2 or A3 albuminuria and in CKD without diabetes with A3 (strong recommendations), and consider it without diabetes at A2. Use the highest approved dose that is tolerated, because the trials used those doses. Never combine an ACE inhibitor, an ARB and a direct renin inhibitor. Check blood pressure, creatinine and potassium within 2–4 weeks of starting or raising the dose. A rise in creatinine of up to 30% is expected and is not a reason to stop. A larger rise calls for a search for volume depletion, NSAIDs or other causes of AKI, and renal artery stenosis. Hyperkalemia on an ACE inhibitor or ARB is usually managed with diet, diuretics, bicarbonate or a potassium binder before the dose is cut. Keep the drug even when eGFR falls below 30.

SGLT2 inhibitors: KDIGO recommends one for type 2 diabetes with CKD and eGFR 20 or more, for CKD with ACR 200 mg/g or more at eGFR 20 or more, and for CKD with heart failure regardless of albuminuria; it suggests one at eGFR 20–45 with lower ACR. Once started, it can continue below 20 until dialysis or transplant. The early dip in eGFR is expected and does not change monitoring. The dapagliflozin label gives 10 mg daily for CKD, does not recommend starting it for kidney or heart indications below eGFR 25, and does not recommend it for glucose lowering below 45. Warn about genital yeast infections, volume depletion, ketoacidosis even with near-normal glucose, and the rare Fournier gangrene, and hold it at least 3 days before surgery or prolonged fasting (label).

Type 2 diabetes with persistent albuminuria despite a maximal ACE inhibitor or ARB: KDIGO suggests adding the nonsteroidal MRA finerenone if eGFR is over 25 and potassium is normal, alongside the SGLT2 inhibitor. Start 10 mg if eGFR is 25–59 and 20 mg if it is 60 or more, check potassium at 1 month and then every 4 months, and hold it if potassium rises above 5.5. A long-acting GLP-1 receptor agonist is recommended when glucose targets are not met on metformin and an SGLT2 inhibitor. KDIGO also recommends a statin (or statin with ezetimibe) for adults 50 or older with eGFR under 60.

AKI in the office

KDIGO defines AKI as a rise in creatinine of 0.3 mg/dL or more within 48 hours, a rise to 1.5 times baseline known or presumed to have occurred within 7 days, or urine output under 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours. Stage 1 is 1.5–1.9 times baseline (or the 0.3 rise), stage 2 is 2.0–2.9 times, and stage 3 is 3 times baseline, creatinine 4.0 mg/dL or more, or the start of dialysis.

Sort the cause. Prerenal: volume loss, low blood pressure, low cardiac output, or drugs that cut renal perfusion (ACE inhibitors, ARBs and NSAIDs are the most common). Intrinsic: acute tubular necrosis is the most common; acute interstitial nephritis is usually a drug reaction (antibiotics, NSAIDs, increasingly proton pump inhibitors). Postrenal: obstruction, most often from BPH in older men. A FENa under 1% suggests a prerenal cause and over 2% an intrinsic one; FEUrea (under 35% prerenal, over 50% intrinsic) holds up better in a patient on diuretics. Muddy brown granular casts point to tubular injury, RBC casts and dysmorphic red cells to glomerular disease, and WBC casts to tubulointerstitial disease. Order a renal ultrasound when obstruction is possible (AFP 2019).

Management is supportive: correct volume, stop or hold nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and ARBs while volume-depleted, aminoglycosides, contrast where possible), and adjust doses of renally cleared drugs such as metformin, sulfonylureas, allopurinol, colchicine, digoxin, lithium, low-molecular-weight heparin, direct oral anticoagulants and most antibiotics. Diuretics do not treat AKI except to relieve fluid overload. Life-threatening findings (potassium over 6.5, pH under 7.2, pulmonary edema not responding to diuretics, uremic encephalopathy or pericarditis, anuria) need emergency care and possible dialysis.

Follow-up: if function has recovered to 90% or more of baseline, review at 3 months; if recovery is slower, at 3 weeks and 3 months, checking blood pressure, weight and creatinine each time (AFP 2019). Refer if eGFR stays under 60. KDIGO reminds clinicians to record a clear plan for restarting drugs stopped during the illness, because they are often never restarted.

Hematuria and proteinuria

Microscopic hematuria means 3 or more red cells per high-power field on microscopy. A dipstick positive for blood needs microscopy, since at least 20% of positive dipsticks show no red cells, and one properly examined sample is usually enough (AFP 2021). First look for a benign cause: UTI, menstruation, vaginal atrophy, prolapse, stones, BPH or urethral stricture. Treat it and repeat the urinalysis. Anticoagulation does not explain hematuria, and an anticoagulated patient gets the same workup.

Red cell casts, dysmorphic red cells, albuminuria, a rising creatinine or new hypertension suggest a glomerular source, which is a nephrology question rather than a urology one. KDIGO lists RBC casts, or more than 20 RBC/HPF not otherwise explained, and ACR 300 mg/g or more with hematuria among its reasons to refer.

Proteinuria is quantified with a spot albumin-creatinine (or protein-creatinine) ratio rather than a dipstick. A positive dipstick in a young person may be transient (fever, exercise) or orthostatic, which is benign and found in about 3–5% of adolescents and young adults; a split daytime and overnight collection shows it (AFP 2000). Persistent albuminuria of 30 mg/g or more for more than 3 months is CKD by KDIGO's definition. Nephrotic-range proteinuria is more than 3–3.5 g a day (a protein-creatinine ratio of 3–3.5 g/g) and, with edema, low albumin and high lipids, makes up nephrotic syndrome (AFP 2009); refer.

Kidney stones

Renal colic is sudden flank pain radiating to the groin with hematuria. Noncontrast CT of the abdomen and pelvis is the most accurate test; ultrasound performs acceptably as a first test and costs less. NSAIDs are first line for pain (for example ketorolac 30–60 mg IM) and work better than opioids with fewer adverse effects. Extra fluids do not ease colic or speed passage (AFP 2019).

About 86% of stones pass on their own: about 90% of stones 6 mm or smaller and 59% of larger ones. Medical expulsive therapy with an alpha blocker (tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily, or doxazosin 4 mg daily) is offered for distal ureteral stones of 5–10 mm. Recheck imaging within 14 days to follow the stone and look for hydronephrosis; with good pain control and no significant hydronephrosis a stone can be watched for about 4–6 weeks. Refer for removal if it is over 10 mm or hydronephrosis is significant.

  • Same-day urology or ED: suspected sepsis, UTI with obstruction, an obstructed single kidney, anuria or bilateral obstruction, pain not controlled by oral drugs.
  • Also send urgently: pregnancy or a late period (ectopic pregnancy), and age over 60 or vascular disease (a leaking aortic aneurysm can mimic colic).

Most stones (about two-thirds or more) are calcium, mostly calcium oxalate; struvite, uric acid and cystine stones are less common, and up to 75% of stones in pregnancy are calcium phosphate. Strain the urine and send the stone for analysis. A basic workup is creatinine, calcium and uric acid (PTH if calcium is high) with urinalysis; a 24-hour urine is for high-risk or recurrent stone formers.

Prevention: 2.5–3 L of fluid a day to pass 2–2.5 L of urine; normal dietary calcium (1.0–1.2 g a day, not restricted); sodium 4–5 g a day or less; animal protein 0.8–1.0 g/kg a day; limit colas. For recurrent calcium stones, a thiazide (hydrochlorothiazide 50 mg, chlorthalidone 25–50 mg or indapamide 2.5 mg daily) or potassium citrate; allopurinol for high uric acid. Check potassium before a thiazide or citrate, within 2 weeks and then yearly (AFP 2019).

BPH and lower urinary tract symptoms in men

Grade symptoms and track response with the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS); 20 or more is severe. The evaluation is a history, a digital rectal exam (reliable only for glands over 50 mL), and a urinalysis for glucose, protein, blood and infection. PSA is not needed to diagnose BPH; it is a screening decision (shared decision-making at 55–69). Check renal function only if kidney impairment is suspected (AFP 2023).

Self-care first: less fluid in the evening, less caffeine and alcohol, double voiding and bladder training. Watchful waiting suits men who are not much bothered (EAU 2026). Alpha blockers work within days and lower the IPSS by 2–4 points. Nonselective agents (doxazosin, terazosin) cause more orthostatic hypotension; selective agents (tamsulosin, alfuzosin) cause more ejaculatory dysfunction. Tell the ophthalmologist before cataract surgery because of intraoperative floppy iris syndrome. Tadalafil (5 mg daily is the dose licensed for LUTS) is about as effective and is the first choice when the man also has ED.

5-alpha reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride) shrink the gland and reduce retention and surgery, but take up to a year and work best in larger prostates: AFP 2023 uses 30 mL or more and EAU 2026 more than 40 mL as the marker of progression risk. Combined with an alpha blocker they beat either drug after more than a year. They lower PSA by up to 50%, so double the measured value when interpreting a PSA. Adverse effects are ED, low libido and ejaculatory problems.

Storage symptoms (urgency, frequency) that persist can be treated with an antimuscarinic, but not when the postvoid residual is over 150 mL (EAU 2026), and with caution in older men because of retention and cognitive effects. Evidence for mirabegron in men is mixed: AFP 2023 found it no better than placebo, while EAU 2026 recommends beta-3 agonists for storage symptoms. Saw palmetto does not help. For acute urinary retention, catheterize, start an alpha blocker, and wait 3 days before a trial without the catheter. Surgery is considered for failed medical therapy, retention, recurrent UTI, recurrent gross hematuria or kidney impairment from obstruction.

Urinary incontinence

Find the type from the history, a 3-day bladder diary and a urinalysis (AFP 2019). Stress incontinence (leakage with cough, sneeze or exercise) affects 25–45% of women over 30; a cough stress test with a full bladder (200–300 mL) that leaks at once supports it. Urge incontinence is sudden urgency with leakage on the way to the toilet. Mixed has both. Overflow (about 5% of chronic cases) comes from an underactive detrusor or outlet obstruction and shows as straining or incomplete emptying; anticholinergics, antihistamines, opioids and sedatives contribute. Functional incontinence is a barrier to reaching the toilet. Sudden, reversible incontinence lasting under 6 months is transient; AFP 2019 uses the TOILETED mnemonic to find the reversible cause.

Conservative care is first line for stress and urge incontinence. Pelvic floor muscle training: three sets of 8–10 contractions held 6–8 seconds, three or four times a week, for at least 15–20 weeks (number needed to treat 3). Bladder training lengthens the interval between voids by 30–60 minutes until the patient voids every 3–4 hours. Lose weight if overweight, stop smoking, cut caffeine and carbonated drinks, and avoid both excessive and severely restricted fluid.

Drugs: no drug is approved for stress incontinence, and systemic drug therapy is not recommended for it. For urge incontinence, mirabegron 25–50 mg daily (can raise blood pressure) or an antimuscarinic; selective agents such as solifenacin or darifenacin cause fewer cognitive effects than oxybutynin, and antimuscarinics are not first line in older adults under the Beers criteria. Vaginal estrogen may help urge symptoms and is often needed before a pessary is fitted; systemic estrogen worsens incontinence. Refractory cases go on to pessaries, slings or other surgery for stress incontinence, and onabotulinumtoxinA injections or neuromodulation for urge incontinence. Refer for pain, persistent hematuria, significant prolapse, prior pelvic surgery or radiation, a suspected fistula or a high residual.

Prostatitis

The NIH classification has four categories: I acute bacterial, II chronic bacterial, III chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) and IV asymptomatic inflammatory. Categories I and II make up about 10% of cases and III and IV about 90% (AFP 2024).

Acute bacterial prostatitis presents with fever, chills, dysuria or obstructive voiding and perineal, rectal or suprapubic pain; the prostate is tender, boggy and enlarged. Examine gently and do not massage the prostate, which can cause bacteremia. Send a midstream urine culture; E. coli causes more than half of cases. In sexually active men under 35, and older men with high-risk sexual behavior, also test and treat for gonorrhea and chlamydia (AFP 2016). Do not check a PSA during the infection; it rises with inflammation. Mild cases are treated with ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin or TMP-SMX; AFP 2024 asks for at least 14 days and AFP 2016 for about 4 weeks in severe infection. Admit for sepsis, failed outpatient therapy, inability to take oral drugs, urinary retention or resistance risk. Fever beyond 36 hours on treatment suggests an abscess and needs imaging.

Chronic bacterial prostatitis is recurrent UTI with the same organism, often with perineal or low back pain between episodes; treat with a fluoroquinolone for 4 weeks, or TMP-SMX, doxycycline or fosfomycin for 6 weeks. CP/CPPS is pelvic pain for months without infection, a diagnosis of exclusion scored with the NIH Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index; it is treated with multimodal, nonantibiotic care. Category IV is an incidental finding during an infertility or PSA workup and needs no treatment.

Erectile dysfunction

ED shares risk factors with vascular disease: diabetes (about 3 times the risk), metabolic syndrome, hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, inactivity and smoking. It typically appears 2–5 years before coronary artery disease, so a man with ED needs a cardiovascular risk assessment (AFP 2016). Check A1C or fasting glucose and lipids; check a morning total testosterone when libido is low, the testes are small or a PDE5 inhibitor fails. Review drugs, especially SSRIs and SNRIs (bupropion and mirtazapine are less likely to cause ED), and screen for depression and relationship or performance anxiety.

Lifestyle change and control of diabetes, blood pressure and lipids come first. PDE5 inhibitors are first-line drug therapy and are similar in effect: sildenafil (taken 30 minutes before, half-life 3–5 hours), vardenafil, avanafil (works in 15 minutes) and tadalafil (half-life 17.5 hours, also 2.5–5 mg daily). A fatty meal delays sildenafil and vardenafil. Common effects are headache, flushing and dyspepsia. PDE5 inhibitors are contraindicated with any nitrate because of severe hypotension; a man on regular nitrates can use a vacuum device instead.

Second line: intracavernosal alprostadil (more effective than the intraurethral pellet; avoid with anticoagulants or bleeding and clotting disorders) or a vacuum device, with the ring left on no more than 30 minutes. Third line: a penile prosthesis. Testosterone is not a treatment for ED when the level is normal. An erection lasting 4 hours or more is priapism and needs emergency urologic care.

Prostate cancer screening

USPSTF (2018, update in progress): for men 55–69, PSA screening is an individual decision after a discussion of benefits and harms (grade C), and men who do not ask for it are not screened. For men 70 and older, the USPSTF recommends against PSA screening (grade D). Black men and men with several first-degree relatives with prostate cancer have higher risk; the USPSTF found too little evidence for a separate recommendation and asks clinicians to tell them about their risk.

The discussion covers the harms. About 1 in 6 men screened at least once in the European trial had a false-positive result; about 1% of biopsies lead to hospitalization; 20–50% of screen-detected cancers are overdiagnosed, more so with age. After radical prostatectomy about 1 in 5 men have long-term incontinence needing pads and about 2 in 3 have long-term ED; more than half have long-term ED after radiation. Active surveillance (regular PSA, exams and repeat biopsies, with treatment if the cancer changes) is a standard option for low-risk localized cancer.

Acute scrotum and scrotal masses

Testicular torsion peaks in newborns and around puberty. It presents with sudden, severe, one-sided pain, often with nausea and vomiting and sometimes after exercise or minor trauma; the testis may sit high with a horizontal lie, and the cremasteric reflex is usually absent (a present reflex makes torsion less likely but does not exclude it). Torsion is a clinical diagnosis: color Doppler ultrasound (sensitivity about 89%, specificity about 99%) helps when the picture is unclear but must not delay surgery. Salvage is 90–100% within 6 hours, about 50% after 12 hours and under 10% after 24 hours (AFP 2013). If surgery cannot happen at once, manual detorsion rotates the testis from medial to lateral ('opening a book'); surgery still follows, with fixation of both testes because the bell-clapper anomaly is bilateral in up to 80%.

Torsion of the appendix testis can mimic cord torsion, sometimes with a visible blue dot, and is managed supportively. Epididymitis develops gradually with dysuria and a normal cremasteric reflex. In sexually active men 14–35 chlamydia and gonorrhea are the usual causes: ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once (1 g if 150 kg or more) plus doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 10 days. Men who practice insertive anal sex get ceftriaxone plus levofloxacin 500 mg daily for 10 days to also cover enteric organisms, and levofloxacin alone is used when an enteric organism alone is likely. Treat partners from the previous 60 days (AFP 2022, CDC regimens).

Every scrotal mass gets an ultrasound with Doppler first. Testicular cancer is the most common solid tumor in men 15–34 and usually presents as a firm, painless intratesticular nodule; draw AFP, beta-hCG and LDH and refer urgently (treatment starts with orchiectomy). The USPSTF recommends against screening asymptomatic men. A varicocele is a 'bag of worms' that grows when the patient stands or bears down, usually on the left; an isolated right-sided varicocele calls for imaging for a retroperitoneal mass. Hydroceles transilluminate and are usually painless; infant hydroceles often resolve within the first year. Spermatoceles are benign epididymal cysts, more common after vasectomy.

Hyperkalemia

Hyperkalemia is a potassium above 5.0 mEq/L (AFP 2023). When a result does not fit the patient, think of pseudohyperkalemia from hemolysis, a tight tourniquet, fist clenching, or very high white cell or platelet counts, and repeat it. Real causes are reduced excretion (CKD, AKI, type 4 RTA, low effective volume), drugs (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone and other potassium-sparing diuretics, TMP-SMX, NSAIDs, heparin, beta blockers, calcineurin inhibitors, digoxin), shifts out of cells (acidosis, hyperglycemia, tissue breakdown) and intake (salt substitutes, mainly with kidney impairment).

ECG changes roughly follow the level: peaked T waves, then a long PR, wide QRS, loss of P waves and bradycardia, then a sine wave. A wide QRS, bradycardia and junctional rhythm best predict serious events. The ECG is insensitive, so a normal tracing does not make a high level safe. KDIGO's table: potassium 6.0–6.4 in a patient who is unwell or has AKI is assessed and treated in hospital, and an unexpected result in a well patient is repeated within 24 hours; 6.5 or more needs immediate assessment with an ECG.

Emergency treatment (AFP 2023): IV calcium gluconate 1,000–2,000 mg over 2–5 minutes stabilizes the heart within minutes but does not lower potassium; repeat after 5 minutes if ECG changes persist. Then shift potassium into cells with regular insulin 10 units IV plus 25 g of glucose (more if glucose is under 100 mg/dL; monitor glucose for at least 3 hours) and nebulized albuterol 10–20 mg. Bicarbonate is only for metabolic acidosis. Remove potassium with a loop diuretic if the patient makes urine, a binder, or dialysis.

Outpatient: review drugs and diet (salt substitutes), and in advanced CKD limit potassium intake. Patiromer (8.4 g once daily to start, titrated; take other oral drugs at least 3 hours before or after; constipation, low magnesium) and sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (10 g three times daily for up to 48 hours, then a once-daily maintenance dose; edema) are preferred to sodium polystyrene sulfonate, which carries a risk of intestinal necrosis. KDIGO advises managing potassium so that an ACE inhibitor or ARB can continue, rather than stopping it first.

Hyponatremia

Hyponatremia is sodium under 135 mEq/L: mild 130–134, moderate 125–129, severe under 125. Chronic means present 48 hours or more or of unknown duration, which is most outpatients. It is linked to falls, fractures, gait instability and higher mortality in older adults (AFP 2023).

Work it up in order. Serum osmolality first: normal or high osmolality points to pseudohyponatremia (high lipids or protein) or hyperglycemia. Then urine osmolality, urine sodium and volume status. Hypovolemic: diuretics (thiazides especially), vomiting or diarrhea, adrenal insufficiency; urine sodium is over 20 with diuretics and low with GI losses. Euvolemic: SIADH (drugs include SSRIs, carbamazepine, opioids and tricyclics), hypothyroidism, cortisol deficiency, primary polydipsia, and beer potomania and other low-solute diets. Hypervolemic: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome and advanced CKD.

SIADH is a diagnosis of exclusion: low serum osmolality, urine osmolality over 100 mOsm/kg, euvolemia, urine sodium over 20 mEq/L, normal thyroid, adrenal and kidney function, and no diuretic. Treat chronic, mild cases by stopping the cause and restricting free water (about 500 mL to 1 L a day); salt tablets or oral urea are second steps. Vaptans start in hospital and are not combined with fluid restriction. Thiazide-induced hyponatremia is treated by stopping the thiazide.

Safety Correct chronic hyponatremia slowly: no more than 8 mEq/L in 24 hours when the risk of osmotic demyelination is high (sodium under 105, alcohol use, malnutrition, low potassium), and no more than 10–12 mEq/L in 24 hours otherwise. Severe symptoms with sodium under 125 are a hospital emergency treated with 3% saline boluses aiming for a 4–6 mEq/L rise.

Renal dosing principles

Consider kidney function whenever a drug is cleared by the kidneys. For most patients a creatinine-based eGFR is good enough for dosing; use combined creatinine and cystatin C, or a measured GFR, when a narrow therapeutic range makes accuracy matter or when creatinine misleads, and use eGFR not indexed to body surface area at extremes of weight. Kidney function that is changing (AKI, recovery) is not a steady state, and doses based on it need frequent review (KDIGO 2024). Package inserts usually state thresholds as creatinine clearance (Cockcroft-Gault), so check which measure a label uses.

  • Metformin (label): do not start at eGFR 30–45; reassess benefit if eGFR falls below 45 on therapy; contraindicated under 30; check eGFR at least yearly. Stop at or before iodinated contrast at eGFR 30–60 and restart after 48 hours if function is stable.
  • Nitrofurantoin (label): contraindicated at CrCl under 60 mL/min.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors: kidney and heart benefit persists at low eGFR even though glucose lowering fades (dapagliflozin: no glycemic use under 45, no new start for CKD under 25; KDIGO continues it below 20 once started).
  • ACE inhibitors and ARBs: continue below eGFR 30 (KDIGO); expect a creatinine rise up to 30%.
  • Sick-day rule: KDIGO suggests considering planned pauses of metformin, ACE inhibitors, ARBs and SGLT2 inhibitors in the 48–72 hours before elective surgery or while an acute illness is managed, with a documented restart plan.
  • Avoid NSAIDs in CKD and AKI, limit over-the-counter and herbal products, and review the whole list at every transition of care.
