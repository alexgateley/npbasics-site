Try one

Neurology Primary Care practice question

A 42-year-old woman with episodic migraine without aura returns 3 months after starting topiramate for prevention, titrated to 50 mg twice daily over the first month. Before treatment she had 10 migraine days a month; her diary now shows 4, and she uses sumatriptan on 3 of those days with good relief. She has mild tingling in her fingertips that she says does not bother her. She has a levonorgestrel IUD. BP is 118/74 mm Hg. Which plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Stop topiramate because of the paresthesia
  2. Add a CGRP monoclonal antibody injection monthly
  3. Continue the current dose
  4. Increase topiramate to 100 mg twice daily

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Paresthesia is the most common topiramate side effect and is often mild and stable; she finds it tolerable, so stopping an effective preventive is not warranted.
  • B. A CGRP monoclonal antibody is a preventive option when other preventives fail or are not tolerated. Adding a second preventive to one that already works is not indicated.
  • C. Correct. A preventive has worked when monthly migraine days fall by at least half; she went from 10 to 4. Once control has lasted 6 to 12 months, a gradual taper can be tried.
  • D. Tempting because 100 mg twice daily is the labeled target, but she has already responded well at a lower dose, and a higher dose adds cognitive and paresthesia side effects without a clear need.

The American Headache Society defines a successful preventive response as at least a 50% reduction in migraine days, judged after an adequate trial of about 2 to 3 months at a target or tolerated dose. This patient went from 10 to 4 migraine days a month, so the drug is working, and because she uses acute treatment on fewer than 10 days a month she is below the medication-overuse threshold for triptans. Her mild paresthesia is a common, often transient effect of carbonic anhydrase inhibition, which means it is not a reason to stop. The plan is to keep the effective dose and, after 6 to 12 months of stable control, consider a gradual taper. Her IUD avoids the interaction of higher topiramate doses with estrogen-containing contraception, and she should still know the drug is teratogenic. At each visit, review the diary, acute medication days and side effects such as cognitive slowing, kidney stones, weight loss and acute vision change.

Sources: American Headache Society Consensus Statement: Integrating New Migraine Treatments (2021); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

TIA and stroke

  • BE FAST: Balance, Eyes, Face droop, Arm drift, Speech, Time to call 911. Note when symptoms began (last known well).
  • Active symptoms: call EMS, not a car ride. Clot-busting drugs have an hours-long window (TNKase label: within 3 hours of onset; ESO guideline: alteplase up to 4.5 hours).
  • TIA that has resolved: same-day emergency evaluation. Stroke risk is highest in the first days (median time to stroke 24 hours).
  • Workup: noncontrast CT, ECG, vascular imaging of the neck; rhythm monitoring if no cause is found.
  • Symptomatic carotid stenosis of 50–99%: refer for revascularization within 2 weeks.
Exam tip Higher-risk TIA or minor stroke: aspirin plus clopidogrel for 21 days, then one antiplatelet. Atrial fibrillation: anticoagulate, a DOAC over warfarin. Add a statin and treat blood pressure.

First seizure

  • Get a witness account. Tongue biting, head turning, limb jerking and incontinence favor seizure; chest pain, palpitations and presyncope favor syncope.
  • Look for a provoking cause (about 40%): glucose, sodium, drugs or withdrawal, alcohol, stroke, infection, head injury.
  • Unprovoked: EEG (ideally within 24 hours) and epilepsy-protocol MRI. Immediate CT for focal seizure, fever, head trauma, anticoagulation, cancer or a deficit.
  • Epilepsy: two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, or one with at least a 60% risk of another.
  • Starting medicine after one seizure cuts the absolute 2-year recurrence risk by about 35% but does not change remission after 3 years: decide together.
Safety Counsel on safety the same day: no driving until the state's seizure-free interval is met (12 months in some states), and no swimming or bathing alone, ladders or heights.

Febrile seizures

  • Age 6 months to 5 years, fever of 38°C (100.4°F) or more, no CNS infection.
  • Simple: generalized, under 15 minutes, once in 24 hours. Complex: focal, 15 minutes or more, or repeated within 24 hours.
  • Well child, simple seizure: find the fever source. No routine labs, EEG or imaging.
  • Lumbar puncture: signs of meningitis; consider at 6–12 months if Hib or pneumococcal doses are missing or unknown, or after antibiotics.
  • Recurrence about a third; epilepsy about 2.4% after simple seizures. Antipyretics and daily anticonvulsants do not prevent them.

Parkinson disease

  • Clinical diagnosis: bradykinesia with rest tremor or rigidity, asymmetric onset, gradual course, clear levodopa response.
  • Early clues: REM sleep behavior disorder, loss of smell, constipation, depression.
  • Doubt the diagnosis with rapid progression, early symmetric signs, poor levodopa response; rule out drug-induced parkinsonism.
  • Treat when symptoms affect function. Carbidopa-levodopa (preferred over 65 or with marked impairment), a dopamine agonist, or an MAO-B inhibitor (younger, mild).
  • Give levodopa 30–45 minutes before high-protein meals; never stop it abruptly.
Safety Dopamine agonists: ask about impulse control problems (gambling, shopping) and sleep attacks. Avoid anticholinergics in older adults.

Dementia

  • Mild cognitive impairment: decline without loss of daily function. Dementia: decline that interferes with function.
  • Screen at-risk patients with the Mini-Cog or Memory Impairment Screen; if abnormal, use the MoCA, SLUMS or RUDAS. USPSTF: I statement for screening adults 65+ without symptoms.
  • Rule out depression, delirium and drugs. Labs: CBC, CMP, TSH, B12, folate. Imaging: noncontrast MRI (CT if MRI not possible).
  • Refer to neurology if onset before 60, severe behavior change or unclear diagnosis.
  • Donepezil: 5 mg at bedtime, 10 mg after 4–6 weeks. Small benefit; reassess periodically.
Safety Cholinesterase inhibitors slow the heart: bradycardia, heart block and syncope can occur. They also raise GI bleeding risk with NSAIDs. Antipsychotics are not first-line for behavior symptoms (higher mortality).

Bell palsy

  • Sudden one-sided weakness that includes the forehead; the rest of the exam is normal.
  • Forehead spared, other deficits, or both sides: think central lesion.
  • Prednisone 50–60 mg daily for 5 days, then a 5-day taper, started within 72 hours. Adding valacyclovir or acyclovir is optional; never an antiviral alone.
  • Eye protection: lubricating drops or gel, taping the lid at night.
  • No routine labs or imaging. Reassess or refer if recovery is incomplete at 3 months.

Peripheral neuropathy

  • First labs: CBC, CMP, fasting glucose, B12, TSH, serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation.
  • Refer for nerve studies if onset is acute, asymmetric, motor- or autonomic-predominant, or rapidly progressive, or if symptoms persist with a normal workup.
  • Acute or rapidly progressive, motor-predominant or proximal weakness: think Guillain-Barré; refer urgently.
  • Diabetes: 10-g monofilament plus 128-Hz tuning fork.
  • Neuropathic pain: gabapentin, pregabalin, duloxetine, venlafaxine, or a tricyclic; topical lidocaine or capsaicin as add-ons; opioids last.

Multiple sclerosis clues

  • Young adult, women twice as often: optic neuritis (one-eye vision loss, pain with eye movement), Lhermitte sign, numbness, weakness, bladder symptoms, fatigue, heat sensitivity.
  • Symptoms that build over days and remit point to relapsing-remitting MS (about 85%).
  • MRI of brain and spine and neurology referral; diagnosis by the McDonald criteria (lesions disseminated in space and time).
  • Relapse: methylprednisolone 1,000 mg daily for 3 days, IV or oral. Start disease-modifying therapy early in active MS.

Concussion and return to play

  • Remove from play at once; return only with a health care provider's clearance.
  • Relative rest for about 2 days (less screen time); light activity in the first 24–48 hours is fine if symptoms barely rise.
  • CDC's 6 steps: back to regular activities such as school, light aerobic, moderate, heavy non-contact, full-contact practice, competition. At least 24 hours per step.
  • Symptoms return: stop, rest, restart at the previous step.
  • Symptoms over 4 weeks: refer to a concussion specialist.
Safety ED now: worsening headache, repeated vomiting, seizure, growing drowsiness or confusion, slurred speech, weakness, unequal pupils.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Evaluating headache

The first task with any headache is to decide whether it is primary (migraine, tension-type, cluster) or secondary to something else. VA/DoD 2023 builds the history around frequency, onset, aura, location, triggers, autonomic and visual symptoms, the doses and days of every acute medicine used each month, sleep, caffeine, alcohol, mental health and head or neck trauma. Its examination includes blood pressure, a neurologic exam with fundoscopy, the neck, the jaw joint, pericranial muscles and the temporal arteries (tenderness, a cord-like artery or an absent pulse).

The SNOOP4E red flags in VA/DoD 2023 are: Systemic symptoms or conditions (fever, weight change, cancer, infection, giant cell arteritis, pregnancy or postpartum, immunocompromise including HIV); Neurologic symptoms or signs (confusion, altered alertness, personality change, diplopia, pulsatile tinnitus, focal signs, meningismus, seizures, ptosis, proptosis, pain with eye movement); sudden Onset (thunderclap, maximal within minutes, first or worst ever); Older onset (50 or older); Pattern change; Precipitated by Valsalva (cough, straining); Postural aggravation; Papilledema; and Exertion. Red flags call for emergent evaluation when they point to a dangerous cause.

Thunderclap headache is subarachnoid hemorrhage until shown otherwise. In the AFP 2024 summary of the US aneurysmal SAH guideline, the Ottawa SAH rule can rule out SAH clinically in an alert patient 15 or older with a sudden nontraumatic headache peaking within an hour, but only if none of its features is present: age 40 or older, neck pain or stiffness, witnessed loss of consciousness, onset during exertion, thunderclap onset (instant peak) or limited neck flexion. Anyone who does not clear the rule needs a noncontrast head CT; within 6 hours of onset it is 99% sensitive and can rule out aneurysmal SAH. After 6 hours a negative CT is followed by a lumbar puncture, and a positive CT by CT angiography. Sentinel headaches precede up to 43% of these bleeds, so a sudden severe headache that settled is still an emergency.

The American Headache Society Choosing Wisely list advises against neuroimaging for stable headaches that meet migraine criteria, and against CT for headache when MRI is available, except in emergency settings. So when a nonacute red flag points to a structural cause, MRI is the study; CT is the emergency test for suspected bleeding. A new headache after 50 with jaw claudication, scalp or temporal artery tenderness or visual symptoms raises giant cell arteritis, which can blind a patient and needs same-day evaluation.

Migraine: acute treatment

AFP 2025 stratifies acute treatment by severity. NSAIDs and acetaminophen are first-line for mild to moderate attacks; triptans are first-line for moderate to severe attacks, and a different triptan can be tried if the first fails. VA/DoD 2023 strongly recommends eletriptan, frovatriptan, rizatriptan, oral or subcutaneous sumatriptan, sumatriptan with naproxen, or oral or nasal zolmitriptan, and also strongly recommends aspirin-acetaminophen-caffeine. Acetaminophen, aspirin, ibuprofen and naproxen alone are suggested (weak).

Triptans are contraindicated with known or suspected coronary artery or cerebrovascular disease, because they can provoke ischemia, and VA/DoD adds no triptan with an ergot or in hemiplegic or basilar migraine. De novo coronary vasospasm is very rare. In pregnancy, nonpharmacologic measures come first; if a prescription is needed, VA/DoD notes emerging evidence that triptans are as safe as and more effective than butalbital compounds and can be used in select cases with close observation.

Gepants (rimegepant, ubrogepant) block the CGRP receptor without constricting vessels. AFP 2025 places gepants second-line, for nonpregnant adults who did not respond to more than one oral triptan or cannot take triptans; cost limits their use. VA/DoD 2023 suggests them (weak). Lasmiditan, the only ditan, is being withdrawn and is unavailable after May 31, 2026. Antiemetics and ergots are second-line or refractory options. Opioids and butalbital compounds should be avoided except in refractory cases.

Medication overuse headache (ICHD-3, as VA/DoD tabulates it) is headache on 15 or more days a month in a patient with a preexisting headache disorder, with regular overuse for more than 3 months: triptans, ergots, opioids or combination analgesics on 10 or more days a month, or simple analgesics (aspirin, NSAIDs, acetaminophen) on 15 or more days. VA/DoD notes lower thresholds in population studies for opioids (about 8 days) and butalbital (about 5 days), and counts use for any reason. In the cohort VA/DoD cites, baseline headache frequency was the strongest risk factor (7 to 14 headache days a month against none: odds ratio 19.4). Over-the-counter combinations with caffeine count as combination analgesics.

Migraine: prevention

AFP 2019 lists the indications for prevention: 4 or more headaches a month or at least 8 headache days a month; debilitating attacks despite acute treatment; intolerance of or contraindication to acute drugs; medication overuse; patient preference; and certain subtypes such as hemiplegic migraine or frequent aura. AFP 2025 refers to the American Headache Society criteria for the same decision.

AFP 2019 advises judging a preventive after about 2 months at a target dose (full effect can take up to 6 months), defines success as a 50% fall in attacks or headache days, shorter attacks or a better response to acute drugs, and suggests a slow taper after 6 to 12 months of good control. VA/DoD suggests against gabapentin for episodic migraine. Exercise, CBT, biofeedback, mindfulness and relaxation training help (AFP 2025, SORT B), and VA/DoD suggests physical therapy and aerobic or strength exercise. Oral magnesium is suggested (VA/DoD), which finds the evidence for coenzyme Q10, feverfew, melatonin and riboflavin insufficient; AFP 2025 counts feverfew, magnesium and melatonin as effective and generally well tolerated.

Exam tip Choosing the preventive by comorbidity is the usual board logic: a beta-blocker for coexisting hypertension (not asthma), topiramate when weight loss is welcome (not kidney stones or pregnancy), amitriptyline for poor sleep or coexisting tension-type headache in a younger patient, venlafaxine with depression.

Tension-type and cluster headache

Tension-type headache is the common bilateral, pressing headache without the nausea, photophobia and activity sensitivity of migraine. VA/DoD 2023 suggests ibuprofen 400 mg or acetaminophen 1,000 mg for acute attacks, amitriptyline to prevent chronic tension-type headache, and physical therapy and exercise; it suggests against botulinum toxin. The overuse limits for analgesics apply here too.

Cluster headache is a trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia: strictly one-sided orbital or temporal pain with same-side tearing, conjunctival redness, nasal congestion or rhinorrhea and ptosis, in attacks that recur over weeks (a bout). Unlike a migraine patient, who lies still, the patient paces. The AHS 2016 guideline gives Level A evidence to three acute treatments: subcutaneous sumatriptan, nasal zolmitriptan and high-flow oxygen. VA/DoD suggests subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg or nasal zolmitriptan 10 mg, and normobaric oxygen; in the key crossover trial, 100% oxygen at 12 L/min by face mask for 15 minutes gave pain freedom or adequate relief at 15 minutes in 78% of attacks, against 20% with air.

Prevention is usually specialist-led. Verapamil is the traditional first choice, but VA/DoD 2023 found insufficient evidence for or against it. VA/DoD suggests galcanezumab for episodic (not chronic) cluster headache and noninvasive vagus nerve stimulation for acute episodic attacks; AHS 2016 rates suboccipital steroid injection as its only Level A preventive.

TIA and stroke recognition

CDC teaches the public BE FAST: Balance loss or dizziness, Eyes (trouble seeing), Face drooping on smiling, Arm drifting down when both are raised, Speech slurred or strange, Time to call 911. Other sudden symptoms are numbness or weakness on one side, confusion or trouble understanding, and a severe headache with no known cause. Patients should call an ambulance rather than drive, and note when symptoms began, because the most effective treatments depend on how early the stroke is recognized.

The time window matters to the ED, not to the office decision. The US TNKase label (2025) says to start treatment for acute ischemic stroke as soon as possible and within 3 hours of symptom onset; ESO 2021 supports alteplase within 4.5 hours of onset. Either way, a patient with active deficits goes by EMS, now, with the time last known well recorded.

A TIA is a transient episode of focal neurologic loss without infarction on imaging (the tissue-based definition), as opposed to the older 'resolved within 24 hours'. In Perry 2022 (CMAJ), 90-day stroke risk after TIA or minor stroke has fallen from about 10% to as low as 1% with rapid, organized care, and the risk is front-loaded: median time to the next stroke is 24 hours. Sudden unilateral weakness, aphasia or dysarthria strongly suggest TIA; isolated vertigo, loss of consciousness, bilateral symptoms and confusion are more common in mimics (migraine aura, seizure, syncope, peripheral vertigo, somatization). About half of initial TIA diagnoses turn out to be mimics, so the workup still matters.

  • Immediate ECG and noncontrast CT head for everyone.
  • CT angiography or carotid Doppler to find extracranial stenosis over 50%, urgently in medium- and high-risk patients.
  • Holter or loop monitoring for up to 14 days when no cause is found, to catch atrial fibrillation; outpatient echocardiography.
  • Symptomatic stenosis of 50–99% treated within 2 weeks: absolute stroke risk falls by 17% (number needed to treat 6). The benefit is gone after 3 months.
  • Medium- and high-risk TIA or minor stroke: aspirin 81 mg plus clopidogrel 75 mg daily for 21 days (load aspirin 160 mg and clopidogrel 300 or 600 mg), then a single antiplatelet.
  • Atrial fibrillation: anticoagulation lowers recurrent stroke by about two-thirds; a DOAC is preferred unless contraindicated (for example creatinine clearance under 30 mL/min).
  • A statin for noncardioembolic events, blood pressure treatment, and diabetes control.

First seizure: workup, treatment and counsel

AFP 2022 starts with the witness, because the patient often cannot describe the event. Tongue biting, head turning, limb jerking and urinary incontinence best predict a seizure; a prodrome such as déjà vu and confusion or amnesia afterward also favor it. Chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations and presyncope point elsewhere. Psychogenic nonepileptic seizures are diagnosed by video-EEG during an event and can coexist with epilepsy. Prolactin helps little: it rises after syncope as well as after seizures, although a normal level can support PNES.

About 40% of first seizures are provoked. Stroke and CNS infection are the most common causes; others are metabolic derangement (AFP lists glucose under 36 or over 450 mg/dL and sodium under 110 mEq/L), drug toxicity, withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives, and head injury. Labs (CBC, metabolic panel, toxicology when suspected) look for these. After an unprovoked seizure, AFP advises an EEG, ideally within 24 hours (about half are abnormal; up to 10% of people with epilepsy have a normal first EEG, so a sleep-deprived or ambulatory EEG follows a normal one), and an epilepsy-protocol MRI. Imaging is abnormal in up to 30%. Immediate CT is for focal seizure, fever, persistent headache, a deficit, head trauma, anticoagulation, cancer or immunocompromise. Lumbar puncture is for fever, stiff neck, immunocompromise or altered mental status after imaging.

Epilepsy is diagnosed after two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, or after one when the risk of another over 10 years is at least 60% or an epilepsy syndrome is present. After a first unprovoked seizure, about a third of adults have another within a year and nearly half within 2 years (AFP 2022); the AAN/AES 2015 guideline gives 21% to 45% over the first 2 years. A prior brain insult, an epileptiform EEG, an imaging abnormality and a seizure during sleep raise the risk. Starting an antiseizure medicine at once lowers the absolute risk of recurrence over 2 years by about 35% (AFP 2022) but may not improve quality of life, and by 3 to 5 years recurrence is the same; AAN/AES puts adverse effects at 7% to 31%, mostly mild and reversible. The decision is shared, and AAN/AES advises individualizing it.

Driving rules are set by each state. AFP 2022 notes that some states require 12 months free of seizures and others leave the decision to the clinician; patients on medicine are more likely to be driving at 2 years. Tell the patient not to drive until the state's interval is met, document the advice, and, in states that require clinicians to report, follow that rule. Safety advice: avoid ladders and sharp tools, and have someone present for bathing and swimming.

Febrile seizures

A febrile seizure occurs between about 6 months and 5 years of age with a temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or more and no CNS infection (AFP 2019). Simple seizures are generalized, last under 15 minutes and do not recur within 24 hours; complex seizures are focal, last 15 minutes or more, or recur within 24 hours. The AAP 2011 guideline tells clinicians to focus on the cause of the fever. A simple febrile seizure does not usually need EEG, blood tests or neuroimaging. Lumbar puncture is done when meningitis is a clinical concern, and is an option for infants 6 to 12 months old whose Hib or pneumococcal immunizations are incomplete or unknown, and for children pretreated with antibiotics.

About a third of children have another febrile seizure. The risk factors are age under 18 months, fever for under an hour before the seizure, a first-degree relative with febrile seizures, and a temperature under 40°C (104°F); with none of them, 2-year recurrence is about 14%, and with all four about 75%. Epilepsy follows in about 2.4% after simple and 6% to 8% after complex febrile seizures. Antipyretics do not prevent recurrence (SORT A), and continuous or intermittent anticonvulsants are not started after a first febrile seizure (SORT B); intermittent diazepam reduces recurrence but causes adverse effects in up to 30%.

Teach parents seizure first aid (CDC): keep the child from injury, do not hold the child down or put anything in the mouth, turn the child gently onto the side, and time the seizure; call 911 if it lasts more than 5 minutes. A seizure longer than 5 minutes is unlikely to stop on its own and is treated with a benzodiazepine (AFP 2019). Simple febrile seizures do not affect intelligence, school progress or behavior.

Parkinson disease

AFP 2020 makes Parkinson disease a clinical diagnosis built on bradykinesia (tested with rapid alternating movements), rigidity (usually cogwheel, on the side of the tremor) and a unilateral, distal 'pill-rolling' rest tremor. Asymmetric onset, gradual progression and a clear response to levodopa support it. Shuffling gait, stooped posture, turning en bloc, freezing and postural instability come later. REM sleep behavior disorder, depression, constipation, fatigue and loss of smell can precede the motor signs by years; orthostatic hypotension, urinary symptoms, dementia and psychosis appear later.

Common mimics are essential tremor (action tremor of the whole hand, often familial), progressive supranuclear palsy, Lewy body dementia and drug-induced parkinsonism, which in practice usually follows dopamine blockers such as antipsychotics, metoclopramide or prochlorperazine and is typically symmetric. Rapid progression, early symmetric involvement and a poor levodopa response should make the clinician doubt the diagnosis.

Carbidopa-levodopa starts at the lowest immediate-release dose 3 times daily, ideally on an empty stomach and 30 to 45 minutes before high-protein meals. It should not be withheld for long periods (risk of akathisia and a neuroleptic malignant-like syndrome). Review nonmotor symptoms at every visit: depression (SNRIs have the best data), psychosis (quetiapine or pimavanserin, started low and titrated slowly; clozapine when those fail or are not tolerated), orthostatic hypotension (nonpharmacologic steps and fewer antihypertensives first, then midodrine or fludrocortisone), constipation and dementia (cholinesterase inhibitors; rivastigmine has the most data). Psychosis is the strongest risk factor for nursing home placement, and dementia affects about 40% of patients. Interprofessional care (physical, occupational and speech therapy) improves function.

Dementia: screening, workup and drugs

AFP 2025 uses DSM-5: dementia is a major neurocognitive disorder, decline in at least one of six cognitive domains that interferes with daily function; mild cognitive impairment is decline without that functional impact. Age is the strongest risk factor. For patients at risk or with concerns, it recommends a brief validated screen (Mini-Cog or Memory Impairment Screen, or an informant questionnaire), then a more detailed tool (MoCA, SLUMS or RUDAS) if the screen is abnormal or suspicion persists. The USPSTF (2020) gives an I statement for screening community-dwelling adults 65 and older without signs or symptoms; the MMSE was the most studied tool (pooled sensitivity 0.89, specificity 0.90 for dementia), and screening tests are not diagnostic.

Depression and delirium must be separated from dementia because both can reverse, and a medication review looks for anticholinergic and sedating drugs. Initial labs are a CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel, TSH, folate and B12, tailored to risk. Structural imaging, preferably noncontrast MRI (CT if MRI is unavailable or contraindicated), rules out secondary causes. Routine blood or CSF biomarkers are not recommended in mild cognitive impairment. Refer to neurology for onset before 60, severe behavioral disturbance or an unclear diagnosis.

Classic clues to the type: Alzheimer disease starts with short-term memory loss and progresses gradually; Lewy body dementia brings fluctuating attention, early visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM sleep behavior disorder and marked sensitivity to antipsychotics; frontotemporal dementia starts earlier with behavior or language change rather than memory loss; vascular dementia follows strokes and declines in steps.

Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) give minor improvements in cognition and daily function (donepezil, AFP 2024, SORT A). The Aricept label starts 5 mg once daily in the evening and allows 10 mg after 4 to 6 weeks; 23 mg is for moderate to severe disease after at least 3 months at 10 mg; in its trial it beat 10 mg on a cognition score but not on the global rating, and AFP 2024 calls its benefit not significantly greater. Label warnings: bradycardia, heart block and syncope even without known conduction disease; nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and weight loss; peptic ulcer and GI bleeding, especially with NSAIDs; bladder outflow obstruction; seizures; caution in asthma or COPD; exaggerated succinylcholine effect under anesthesia. Insomnia and abnormal dreams are listed adverse reactions. Memantine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is the other symptomatic drug class. The American Geriatrics Society Choosing Wisely list (quoted in AFP 2024) advises against prescribing cholinesterase inhibitors without periodic assessment of cognitive benefit and GI adverse effects.

Anti-amyloid antibodies are specialist drugs. The Leqembi (lecanemab) label limits starting treatment to mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia with confirmed amyloid pathology; it carries a boxed warning for amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (brain edema and microhemorrhage, sometimes fatal, with intracerebral hemorrhage over 1 cm reported), higher risk in ApoE ε4 homozygotes, so ApoE ε4 testing comes before treatment, and caution with thrombolytics because ARIA can mimic stroke. It needs a recent baseline brain MRI and MRIs after 1, 2, 3 and 6 months of treatment. It starts at 10 mg/kg IV every 2 weeks or 500 mg by weekly subcutaneous autoinjector; after 18 months it can step down to a maintenance regimen (10 mg/kg IV every 4 weeks or 360 mg subcutaneous weekly). AFP 2024 found no meaningful benefit on patient-centered outcomes.

Safety For agitation, start with nonpharmacologic approaches (music, validation, routine). Antipsychotics raise mortality in dementia and are not first-line for behavioral symptoms (American Geriatrics Society Choosing Wisely, quoted in AFP 2024); brexpiprazole is FDA-approved for agitation in Alzheimer dementia.

Bell palsy

Bell palsy is acute, idiopathic, unilateral lower motor neuron facial weakness: the whole side, forehead included, with an otherwise normal neurologic exam (AFP 2023). Incidence is 20 to 30 per 100,000 a year, highest at ages 15 to 45; diabetes, hypertension, immunosuppression, respiratory infection and pregnancy are risk factors. A patient who can wrinkle the forehead and close the eye tightly on the weak side needs evaluation for a central (upper motor neuron) lesion such as stroke. Bilateral weakness, abnormal eye movements, hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, limb or bulbar weakness, gradual onset, recurrence on the same side (tumor) or no improvement over 3 months also point elsewhere. Lyme disease is a systemic cause that labs can identify where it is endemic.

AAO-HNS 2013 makes oral steroids within 72 hours of onset a strong recommendation for patients 16 and older. AFP 2023 gives prednisone 50 to 60 mg daily for 5 days followed by a 5-day taper (number needed to treat 10 for full recovery). An antiviral may be added (valacyclovir 1 g three times daily for 7 days or acyclovir 400 mg five times daily for 10 days), which reduces synkinesis (number needed to treat 12), but an antiviral alone does not help and is not recommended. Patients who cannot close the eye need lubricating drops or gel and taping at night; keratitis needs an eye specialist.

AAO-HNS advises against routine labs, routine imaging and electrodiagnostic testing in incomplete paralysis, and asks for reassessment or referral to a facial nerve specialist for new neurologic findings, eye symptoms, or incomplete recovery 3 months after onset. More than two-thirds of patients recover completely, 66% to 85% within 3 weeks; synkinesis affects 26% at a year, and recurrence is 6.5%. Up to 90% of children under 14 and of pregnant patients recover completely; a pediatric trial found no benefit from prednisolone, and pregnant patients are usually treated like other adults, early. MRI is recommended for recurrent palsy, and physical therapy for severe (House-Brackmann V or VI) or persistent weakness.

Peripheral neuropathy

Distal symmetric polyneuropathy (stocking-glove numbness, tingling or burning) affects 1% to 7% of adults, more after 50; diabetes is the most common identifiable cause, and 25% to 46% of cases stay idiopathic (AFP 2020). The initial labs are a CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel, fasting glucose, vitamin B12, TSH and serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation; the highest-yield results are a high glucose (11%), an abnormal serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation (9%) and low B12 (3.6%). Add tests by suspicion: A1C or glucose tolerance, alcohol history, folate, thiamine, HIV, a hepatic panel, syphilis serology, Lyme antibodies, ANA, and a medication review (chemotherapy, amiodarone, metronidazole, isoniazid and others).

Red flags change the plan. Speech, visual or cranial nerve findings, ataxia or bladder or bowel incontinence suggest a CNS lesion. Asymmetric or dermatomal symptoms suggest radiculopathy or compression. Acute or rapidly progressive, motor-predominant or proximal weakness suggests Guillain-Barré syndrome, CIDP or vasculitis; acute, subacute, severe or progressive symptoms call for more urgent referral, and CSF analysis is part of the GBS and CIDP workup. Pure motor or autonomic findings without sensory change also need referral. Electrodiagnostic studies are not routine (they changed management in 2 of 368 patients with symmetric distal neuropathy in one study) and are ordered for worrisome features or persistent symptoms with a normal workup. Imaging is not ordered routinely for peripheral neuropathy.

In diabetes, a 10-g monofilament combined with a 128-Hz tuning fork is more accurate than either alone. Treat the cause, teach foot care and footwear, and treat neuropathic pain with a gabapentinoid (gabapentin, pregabalin) or an antidepressant (duloxetine, venlafaxine, amitriptyline, nortriptyline); topical lidocaine or capsaicin can be added, and opioids are a last resort. Tricyclics carry anticholinergic effects to which older adults are particularly sensitive (amitriptyline label).

Multiple sclerosis

MS is an immune-mediated demyelinating disease of the CNS and the most common cause of nontraumatic neurologic disability in young adults; women are affected twice as often, and prevalence rises with distance from the equator (AFP 2022). Smoking and prior infectious mononucleosis are risk factors. Presenting symptoms include sensory disturbance, weakness, gait and coordination problems, optic neuritis (one-eye vision loss with pain on eye movement), Lhermitte sign (an electric sensation down the spine on neck flexion), bladder, bowel and sexual dysfunction, diplopia, vertigo, fatigue and heat sensitivity. A relapse evolves over days and then partly or fully resolves.

About 85% of patients start with relapsing-remitting MS and 15% with primary progressive MS. After a clinically isolated syndrome, about 80% with an abnormal MRI and 20% with a normal MRI go on to MS within 20 years. AFP 2022 uses the 2017 McDonald criteria (dissemination of lesions in space and time, with MRI of the brain and spine and CSF oligoclonal bands as support). The 2024 revision (Lancet Neurology, October 2025) gives one approach for relapsing and progressive courses at any age and adds the optic nerve as a fifth anatomical location; the diagnosis remains a neurologist's. Doubt the diagnosis with a family history of other neurologic disease, abrupt or transient symptoms under 24 hours, progressive ataxia or other organ involvement; mimics include B12 deficiency, Lyme disease, syphilis, HIV, lupus, sarcoidosis and neuromyelitis optica.

Relapses are treated with methylprednisolone 1,000 mg daily for 3 days; oral and IV routes work equally well, and plasmapheresis is a rescue. Disease-modifying therapy should start in active MS (SORT A), chosen with neurology by disease activity, safety and preference, and is generally lifelong. Every smoke-free year is linked to less disability, so smoking cessation is part of treatment. Relapses tend to be less frequent during pregnancy.

Concussion

A sport-related concussion causes a temporary loss of normal brain function without abnormal neuroimaging. Remove the athlete from play at once for loss of consciousness, seizure, balance problems, confusion, behavior change or amnesia (AFP 2024, summarizing the 2022 Amsterdam consensus). The SCAT6 is for ages 13 and older and the Child SCAT6 for ages 8 to 12; they are most accurate within 72 hours, take at least 10 minutes and should be repeated, because symptoms evolve. Any CDC danger sign means the ED now: a headache that worsens and does not go away, repeated vomiting, seizure, inability to recognize people or places, growing confusion or drowsiness, slurred speech, weakness, numbness or poor coordination, or unequal pupils; in infants, inconsolable crying or refusal to feed.

Strict rest does not speed recovery. Relative rest, with less screen time and limited activity, is advised for the first 2 days, and light physical activity can start in the first 24 to 48 hours if it does not significantly worsen symptoms; progression continues as long as any symptom increase is mild and brief (no more than 2 points on a 10-point scale). Students may need temporary school accommodations, and return to school comes before return to sport.

CDC's return-to-sport progression has 6 steps: back to regular activities such as school, light aerobic activity (5 to 10 minutes of walking, biking or light jogging, no weights), moderate activity, heavy non-contact activity, full-contact practice, then competition. Each step usually takes at least 24 hours, and the athlete advances only without new symptoms; if symptoms return, stop, contact the provider, and after rest restart at the previous step. Return happens only with a provider's approval. Refer to a concussion specialist for severe symptoms, symptoms beyond 4 weeks, multiple concussions or risk factors for slow recovery.

Dizziness and vertigo

AFP 2023 sorts dizziness by timing and trigger rather than by the patient's word for it. Episodic triggered dizziness lasts seconds to minutes and is provoked by position (BPPV, orthostatic hypotension: a fall of at least 20 systolic or 10 diastolic within 3 minutes of standing). Episodic spontaneous dizziness lasts minutes to hours (Meniere disease, vestibular migraine, vertebrobasilar TIA, arrhythmia, hypoglycemia, panic). The acute vestibular syndrome is continuous for days (vestibular neuritis, or a posterior circulation stroke in 10% to 20% of spontaneous cases).

HINTS is for the patient with continuous, spontaneous vertigo and nystagmus at the time of the exam. A corrective saccade on head impulse is reassuring (peripheral); a normal head impulse in a vertiginous patient is a central sign. Unidirectional horizontal nystagmus suppressed by fixation is peripheral; vertical, torsional or direction-changing nystagmus is central. Vertical skew on cover testing is central. AFP adds that new hearing asymmetry on finger rub (HINTS plus) suggests stroke even when the rest looks peripheral, and significant gait ataxia raises the probability of a central lesion. In trained hands HINTS is 97% sensitive and 96% specific for central causes. Imaging, especially CT, has limits: AFP says the evidence does not support neuroimaging as the only tool for ruling out stroke.

BPPV is diagnosed when the Dix-Hallpike maneuver (upright to supine, head turned 45 degrees to one side and neck extended 20 degrees, affected ear down) provokes vertigo with upbeating, torsional nystagmus (posterior canal); if it gives horizontal or no nystagmus with a typical history, a supine roll test looks for lateral canal BPPV (AAO-HNS 2017). Treat with a canalith repositioning maneuver such as the Epley, with no postural restrictions afterward. Do not order imaging or vestibular testing for typical BPPV, do not routinely give vestibular suppressants such as antihistamines (meclizine) or benzodiazepines (AFP notes these can block central compensation), and reassess within a month. BPPV peaks between 50 and 70.

Vestibular neuritis often follows a viral illness: days of continuous vertigo with horizontal nystagmus and no hearing loss; treatment is reassurance, short-term symptom relief and vestibular rehabilitation, and AFP 2023 finds no proven role for steroids or antivirals. Meniere disease brings spells of 20 minutes to 12 hours with fluctuating hearing loss, tinnitus and aural fullness; get an audiogram. Vestibular migraine needs at least 5 episodes of 5 minutes to 72 hours in a person with migraine and is treated as migraine.

Restless legs syndrome

RLS is an urge to move the legs, usually with unpleasant sensations, that starts or worsens at rest and in the evening and is relieved by movement. The AASM 2025 guideline asks clinicians first to address exacerbating factors: alcohol, caffeine, drugs such as sedating antihistamines, serotonergic antidepressants and dopamine blockers, and untreated obstructive sleep apnea. Check iron studies (ferritin and transferrin saturation) in the morning, with no iron supplements or iron-rich food for 24 hours.

  • Iron: in adults, oral or IV iron if ferritin is 75 ng/mL or less or transferrin saturation is under 20%; IV iron only if ferritin is 75 to 100 ng/mL. In children, iron if ferritin is under 50 ng/mL. IV ferric carboxymaltose is a strong recommendation; ferrous sulfate, ferumoxytol and low-molecular-weight iron dextran are conditional.
  • Strong recommendations for gabapentin enacarbil, gabapentin and pregabalin.
  • Conditional recommendations against levodopa, pramipexole, ropinirole and rotigotine as standard first-line treatment, because of augmentation (symptoms that start earlier, intensify and spread on treatment); they remain an option for someone who values short-term relief. Strong recommendation against cabergoline.
  • Conditional options: dipyridamole, extended-release oxycodone and other opioids for refractory symptoms, and bilateral high-frequency peroneal nerve stimulation.
  • End-stage kidney disease: gabapentin, IV iron sucrose (ferritin under 200 ng/mL and saturation under 20%) and vitamin C.
  • Pregnancy: RLS is common; weigh each treatment's pregnancy safety.

Trigeminal neuralgia

Trigeminal neuralgia causes paroxysms of brief, electric, one-sided pain in one or more trigeminal divisions (most often V2 and V3), triggered by light touch, talking, chewing or brushing teeth. The EAN 2019 guideline separates primary disease (classical, with neurovascular compression, or idiopathic) from secondary disease (for example MS or a tumor). Because no clinical feature reliably excludes a secondary cause, it advises MRI with high-resolution sequences for every patient.

Carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine is the first choice for long-term treatment; lamotrigine, gabapentin, pregabalin, baclofen, botulinum toxin and phenytoin are alternatives or add-ons. Surgery is offered when pain is not controlled or drugs are not tolerated; microvascular decompression is the first-line operation for classical trigeminal neuralgia.

The Tegretol label indicates carbamazepine for the pain of true trigeminal neuralgia, not as a simple analgesic. It starts at 100 mg twice daily, increases by up to 200 mg a day only as needed for pain, usually holds at 400 to 800 mg daily, and does not exceed 1,200 mg; try to lower the dose or stop at least every 3 months. Boxed warnings: Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis strongly linked to HLA-B*1502, so screen patients with ancestry from at-risk populations (much of Southeast and South Asia) before starting; and aplastic anemia and agranulocytosis, so get a baseline CBC with platelets. Also watch for hyponatremia (SIADH, worse in older adults and with diuretics), liver injury and fetal harm. Carbamazepine induces CYP3A4 and other enzymes, lowering levels of many drugs, and hormonal contraceptives can fail.
