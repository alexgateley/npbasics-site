Try one

Professional Practice for the FNP practice question

A 70-year-old Medicare patient returns for hypertension follow-up under a plan of care his physician set 3 months ago. The nurse practitioner sees him while the physician is in the office suite. He also reports new right knee pain and swelling; the NP examines the knee, diagnoses an osteoarthritis flare, orders a radiograph and starts topical diclofenac. How should the visit be billed?

  1. Hypertension care incident-to the physician, and knee care under the NP
  2. As a split or shared visit under the physician who set the plan
  3. Under the NP's own billing number, at 85% of the fee schedule
  4. Incident-to the physician at the full fee schedule, since the physician is in the suite

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. One office visit is billed as one E/M service by one practitioner; it cannot be divided between two billing numbers by problem.
  • B. Split or shared billing applies in hospitals and other facility settings; CMS does not allow it for office visits, where incident-to rules apply instead.
  • C. Correct. Because the NP initiated care for a new problem, the service is the NP's own and is paid at 85% of the physician fee schedule.
  • D. Direct supervision is met, but incident-to also requires the NP to follow a physician-established plan; a new problem the NP evaluates and treats breaks that requirement.

Medicare incident-to billing (paid at 100% of the physician fee schedule) requires that the physician performed the initial service and set the plan of care, remains actively involved, and directly supervises in the office, and that the NP carries out that plan. When an NP evaluates and manages a new problem, or changes the plan, the visit no longer qualifies and must be billed under the NP's own National Provider Identifier, paid at 85%. Billing it incident-to would be an overpayment. Split or shared visits apply to facility settings, not physician offices.

Sources: CMS Medicare Benefit Policy Manual, Ch. 15 (NP and incident-to services); CMS MLN Booklet: Evaluation and Management Services (May 2026)

Consent, capacity and reporting

  • Informed consent: diagnosis, the proposed intervention and its purpose, risks, benefits and burdens of each option including no treatment; document the discussion.
  • Capacity has four parts: understand, appreciate how it applies to them, reason, and communicate a choice. It is decision-specific and judged by the clinician; competence is decided by a court.
  • No capacity: the named agent decides, then a surrogate by state law; use substituted judgment, or best interests if wishes are unknown.
  • Emergency: treat without consent when delay would harm and no surrogate is available; inform them as soon as possible.
  • Child abuse: report on reasonable suspicion, not proof; good-faith reporters are immune; the physician-patient privilege is usually no defense.
  • Competent adult with intimate partner violence: report only where state law requires it or the patient agrees.

The Consensus Model and what the ANCC exam asks

The APRN Consensus Model, released in 2008, is a framework for regulating APRNs the same way in every state. It defines the APRN as a registered nurse educated at the graduate level in a role and a population focus, and it ties four regulatory pieces together: licensure, accreditation, certification and education (often shortened to LACE). The four roles are the certified nurse practitioner, the clinical nurse specialist, the certified nurse-midwife and the certified registered nurse anesthetist. The six population foci are family/individual across the lifespan, adult-gerontology (primary or acute care), pediatrics (primary or acute care), neonatal, women's health/gender-related, and psychiatric-mental health. The model also calls for APRNs to practice and prescribe without a required physician agreement, and for an APRN compact to ease practice across states; NCSBN notes that some states have not adopted every element.

Because the four pieces must line up, an FNP's program, national exam and state license are all in the family/individual focus. A board item that describes a pediatric NP seeing a 70-year-old, or an adult-gerontology NP seeing an infant, is testing whether the clinician is outside the population focus, not outside the state's practice category. ANCC's FNP eligibility follows the same logic: an FNP degree or certificate from a program accredited by CCNE, ACEN or NLN CNEA, at least 500 faculty-supervised clinical hours, and graduate courses in advanced pathophysiology, health assessment and pharmacology.

The ANCC outline effective October 30, 2026 has 150 scored items: Assessment 26, Diagnosis 25, Planning 29, Implementation 50 and Evaluation 20. Implementation lists legal and ethical implications (HIPAA, confidentiality, accessibility, technology), regulatory guidelines (reportable diseases, abuse reporting), APRN scope and standards, responsible use of information technology (voice recognition, artificial intelligence, the electronic record) and comprehensive documentation. Evaluation lists ethical and legal principles such as justice, consent, guardianship and bioethics. Planning includes research appraisal. Each of those has a section below.

Practice authority, collaboration and Medicare enrollment

Scope of practice is set by each state, so the exam frames it in AANP's three categories rather than by state. In full practice states, state law lets NPs evaluate, diagnose, order and interpret tests, and start and manage treatment, including prescribing, under the board of nursing alone. In reduced practice states, at least one of those elements is limited by law, typically by a career-long collaborative agreement with another provider or by limiting the settings where NPs may practice. In restricted practice states, at least one element is restricted by a requirement for career-long supervision, delegation or team management by another provider. When a question asks what an NP may do 'in a state that requires a collaborative agreement', the answer is whatever that agreement and the state's law allow.

Medicare has its own floor. To bill Medicare as an NP, a clinician must be an RN authorized by the state to practice as an NP and certified by a recognized national certifying body (CMS's list names ANCC and the American Academy of Nurse Practitioners, whose certification board is AANPCB); those enrolling for the first time since January 1, 2003 also need a master's degree in nursing or a DNP. Medicare covers what the state allows, in collaboration with a physician as state law defines it. Where the state sets no collaboration rule, CMS accepts the NP's own documentation of scope and of the physician relationships used for problems outside it. Services Medicare excludes, such as routine physical checkups, stay excluded even when they are within the NP's scope.

Credentialing and privileging are separate steps. Credentialing verifies the license, certification, education, DEA registration and history; privileging is the facility's decision about which services the NP may perform there. Hospital medical staff may include nonphysician practitioners where state law and the governing body allow, under bylaws that set the criteria for privileges and require periodic appraisal. Every covered provider obtains a National Provider Identifier: a 10-digit number with a check digit that carries no information about the provider. Changes to the NPI record are reported within 30 days, the NPI goes on the Medicare enrollment (CMS-855 or PECOS) and every claim, and a claim without a required NPI is rejected. NPs may also opt out of Medicare and contract privately with beneficiaries, for 2-year periods.

Prescriptive authority and controlled substances

Federal law calls NPs 'mid-level practitioners': clinicians other than physicians, dentists, veterinarians and podiatrists who may dispense controlled substances when the state where they practice allows it. The order is fixed. State authority comes first (some states limit NPs to certain schedules or require a collaborative agreement for them), and the DEA registration follows and can cover only what the state allows. A separate registration is needed for each principal place of practice, so an NP who prescribes in two states holds a registration in each. Registrations run 36 months. Since June 27, 2023, every practitioner registering or renewing attests once to at least 8 hours of training on opioid and other substance use disorders; graduates of an advanced practice nursing program within the past 5 years whose curriculum included at least 8 hours on it meet the requirement, and past DATA-waiver training counts. The same law ended the separate buprenorphine waiver, and federal law no longer caps the number of patients treated for opioid use disorder.

Schedules follow abuse potential and accepted medical use. Schedule I drugs have high abuse potential and no accepted medical use (heroin, and marijuana in general). A final order effective April 28, 2026 moved marijuana, marijuana extracts and plant-derived THC into Schedule III when they are in an FDA-approved drug product or covered by a state medical marijuana license; all other marijuana stays in Schedule I, and DEA's November 2026 list shows both entries. Schedule II drugs have high abuse potential, an accepted use, and can cause severe dependence: oxycodone, hydrocodone, fentanyl, methadone, codeine as a single agent, cocaine, amphetamine, methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine. Schedule III drugs (buprenorphine, testosterone and other anabolic steroids, ketamine, butalbital with aspirin, codeine combinations with no more than 90 mg per dosage unit) have less abuse potential. Schedule IV includes benzodiazepines (alprazolam, lorazepam, clonazepam, diazepam), zolpidem, zaleplon, suvorexant, tramadol, carisoprodol, phenobarbital, phentermine and modafinil. Schedule V is the lowest: pregabalin, lacosamide, brivaracetam, cenobamate, codeine cough preparations up to 200 mg per 100 mL, and diphenoxylate with atropine.

Schedule II prescriptions cannot be refilled. A prescriber may issue several Schedule II prescriptions at once for up to a 90-day supply in total, each marked with the earliest date it may be filled, if the state allows it and the risk of diversion is acceptable. In an emergency a pharmacist may dispense a Schedule II drug on an oral order limited to the emergency period, and the prescriber must deliver a written or electronic prescription marked for emergency dispensing within 7 days. A Schedule II prescription may be partially filled at the request of the patient or prescriber, with the rest filled within 30 days of the date written. Schedule III and IV prescriptions may be refilled up to 5 times within 6 months of the date issued. Every controlled substance prescription must be for a legitimate medical purpose by a practitioner acting in the usual course of professional practice; a prescription outside that is not a valid prescription.

Prescription drug monitoring programs are state databases of controlled substance prescriptions filled for a patient. The CDC 2022 opioid guideline recommends checking the PDMP before starting opioid therapy for acute, subacute or chronic pain and at least every 3 months during ongoing therapy, ideally before every opioid prescription. State law may add its own required checks; the exam answer is to check it whenever a controlled substance is started or a patient's story does not fit.

Telemedicine prescribing of controlled substances is a separate question from Medicare telehealth payment. The Ryan Haight Act generally requires an in-person evaluation before a controlled substance is prescribed over the internet. DEA and HHS have extended the COVID-era flexibilities that waive that requirement four times; the fourth extension runs through December 31, 2026, while a proposed special registration rule (January 2025) is still pending. Separately, a final rule in effect since December 31, 2025 lets a DEA-registered practitioner prescribe an FDA-approved Schedule III-V medication for opioid use disorder (buprenorphine) by telemedicine, audio-only included, for an initial supply of up to 6 months after checking the PDMP of the patient's state; further prescriptions need another form of telemedicine the law allows or an in-person evaluation. Check DEA's current rule before prescribing a controlled substance to a patient you have never seen in person, because the flexibilities end on a set date unless extended again.

HIPAA, confidentiality and minors

The minimum necessary standard asks covered entities to limit each use, disclosure or request of protected health information to what its purpose needs. It does not apply to disclosures to another provider for treatment, to the patient, under the patient's authorization, to HHS for compliance work, or where law requires the disclosure. So an NP may send a full record to a consultant, but front-desk staff should see only what scheduling and billing require.

Family and friends involved in a patient's care or payment may be told what is directly relevant to that role. If the patient is present and able to decide, get agreement, give a chance to object, or reasonably infer from the situation that the patient does not object (a spouse invited into the room). If the patient is absent, incapacitated or in an emergency, use professional judgment about the patient's best interests and share only what is relevant. Someone else may pick up a prescription. A patient may see and get a copy of the record; the practice acts within 30 days, with one 30-day extension if it explains the delay in writing, and may charge only a reasonable, cost-based fee. Psychotherapy notes and material compiled for a lawsuit are excluded. A breach of unsecured information is reported to affected patients without unreasonable delay and within 60 days of discovery; a breach affecting 500 or more people is reported to HHS at the same time, and smaller breaches are logged and reported within 60 days after the end of the calendar year. Records of a patient who has died stay protected for 50 years.

For minors, HIPAA follows state consent law. A parent is usually the minor's personal representative, but not for a service the minor consented to when no other consent is required by law, a service the minor may lawfully obtain without a parent (states commonly allow this for some reproductive, sexually transmitted infection, substance use or mental health care, with details that vary), or a service where the parent agreed to confidentiality between the minor and the clinician. Whether the parent can then see those records depends on state law; where law is silent, the clinician uses professional judgment. A clinician may also decline to treat someone as the personal representative when there is reason to believe that person is abusing or endangering the patient. An emancipated minor is treated as an adult.

Some disclosures need no authorization: reporting diseases to public health authorities, reporting child abuse or neglect to the authorized agency, disclosures required by law, and disclosures made in good faith to prevent a serious and imminent threat to a person or the public, to someone able to prevent it. State law decides whether a clinician must warn or may warn; HIPAA only permits the disclosure.

Consent, capacity, surrogates and mandatory reporting

Informed consent is a process, not a form. The clinician confirms the patient can understand the information and decide voluntarily, then explains the diagnosis if known, the nature and purpose of the recommended intervention, and the risks, burdens and expected benefits of every option, including no treatment, in the way the patient prefers. The discussion and the decision are documented, with any signed form placed in the record. In an emergency, when the patient cannot take part and no surrogate is available, treatment may begin without consent, and the patient or surrogate is told as soon as possible.

Decision-making capacity is a clinical judgment any treating clinician can make, and it is specific to the decision: the same patient may be able to consent to a new medication and not to high-risk surgery. The patient must be able to understand the benefits, risks and alternatives, appreciate how they apply to their own situation, reason through the choice, and communicate it. Cognitive screens help only at the extremes (in the AFP review, an MMSE below 20 makes incapacity more likely and 25 or above makes it unlikely); the Aid to Capacity Evaluation is the structured tool the review rates best. Document the questions asked, the patient's answers and the reasoning. Competence is a legal status decided by a court, for example when a guardian is appointed.

When the patient lacks capacity, involve the patient as far as possible and turn to the person the patient named (a health care power of attorney), and if no one was named, a family member or other close person in the order state law sets. The surrogate uses substituted judgment, deciding as the patient would have from known wishes and values; when those are unknown, the surrogate decides in the patient's best interests. An ethics consultation is the next step when there is no surrogate, when disputes cannot be settled, or when a surrogate's choice conflicts with the patient's known wishes.

Federal law (CAPTA) requires every state to name who must report suspected child abuse and neglect and to give good-faith reporters immunity from civil and criminal liability. In the 2023 federal summary, health care workers were named mandated reporters in 46 states, and about 17 states required every person to report. The reporter must report the facts that raised suspicion; proving abuse is the agency's job. Most states deny the physician-patient privilege as a reason not to report. For adults, many states require health professionals to report suspected abuse, neglect or exploitation of older or vulnerable adults. For a competent adult experiencing intimate partner violence, HIPAA allows a report when a law requires it, when the patient agrees, or in narrow circumstances a statute authorizes, and the patient is told a report was made unless that would put them at risk. Reportable disease lists are set by each state health department.

Malpractice, documentation and liability

Malpractice is negligence by a professional. The plaintiff must prove four things: a duty (a clinician-patient relationship existed), a breach (care fell below what a reasonably prudent clinician in the same role would provide, usually shown by expert testimony), causation (the harm would not have happened but for the breach, and it was a foreseeable result), and damages (actual harm). A bad outcome without a breach is not malpractice, and a breach that caused no harm is not either. Res ipsa loquitur lets a plaintiff infer negligence when the injury does not normally happen without negligence and the thing that caused it was in the defendant's control, as with an instrument left in a wound.

Any malpractice payment made for an individual practitioner's benefit, whatever the amount, is reported to the National Practitioner Data Bank, and the regulations say a settlement does not create a presumption that malpractice occurred. Lack of informed consent is a separate claim: the care itself may have been competent, but a material risk or alternative was not disclosed.

The record is the clinician's best defense. Document the history and examination that are medically appropriate, the assessment and reasoning, the plan, patient education, the patient's refusal of advice and the risks explained, and follow-up. Corrections are made openly as dated, signed late entries or amendments, never by altering or deleting the original. On a Medicare Part B service, the NP may review and verify (sign and date) notes made by students or other team members rather than rewriting them. When the visit level is chosen by time, document the total time on that date.

E/M coding: MDM or time

Since 2021, office and outpatient visits (99202-99205 for new patients, 99212-99215 for established patients) are chosen by medical decision making or by total time on the date of the encounter, whichever better reflects the work. The history and examination must be medically appropriate but no longer set the level, and staff or the patient may record parts of the history for the clinician to review.

MDM has three elements, and two of the three must be met or exceeded for a level: the number and complexity of problems addressed at the visit, the amount and complexity of data reviewed and analyzed, and the risk of patient management. In broad terms: straightforward is one self-limited or minor problem; low is, for example, one stable chronic illness or one acute uncomplicated illness; moderate is, for example, a chronic illness that is worsening or causing treatment side effects, two or more stable chronic illnesses, or an undiagnosed new problem with uncertain prognosis, and prescription drug management is a moderate-risk example; high is a severe exacerbation or a problem that threatens life or bodily function, with high-risk management such as drug therapy needing intensive monitoring for toxicity or a decision about hospitalization. Comorbidities count only when they are addressed or make the visit more complex.

Time is the clinician's own total time on the visit date, face to face and not: preparing for the visit and reviewing tests, obtaining or reviewing history, the face-to-face visit, counseling the patient, family or caregiver, ordering, and documenting. It excludes clinical staff and scribe time, travel, work on another date, and time spent on separately billed services such as an ECG interpretation or a procedure. Minimum times are 15, 30, 45 and 60 minutes for 99202-99205 and 10, 20, 30 and 40 minutes for 99212-99215. For longer visits, Medicare uses G2212 for each 15 minutes beyond its range for the top code (a first unit at 69 minutes total with 99215 and 89 minutes with 99205); other payers use CPT 99417.

G2211 is a Medicare add-on to an office or home visit that recognizes the work of being the continuing focal point for a patient's care, as in primary care, or of ongoing care for a single serious or complex condition. Medicare does not pay it when the visit carries modifier 25, except when the visit is on the same day as an annual wellness visit, vaccine administration or another Part B preventive service.

Medicare billing: the NP rate, incident to and split or shared visits

When an NP bills Medicare under the NP's own NPI, Medicare pays 80% of the lesser of the actual charge or 85% of the physician fee schedule amount; the patient owes the usual 20% coinsurance. NPs accept assignment for these services.

Incident-to billing lets a service by the NP be billed under the physician's NPI and paid as the physician's service, but only in the office or clinic (not hospitals or covered SNF stays) and only when all the rules are met. The physician personally saw the patient and started the course of treatment, and continues to see the patient often enough to show active management. The NP's service is part of that plan. The physician provides direct supervision: present in the office suite and immediately available, though not necessarily in the room. From January 1, 2026, CMS allows that presence by real-time audio and video (not audio-only) for most incident-to services. A new problem has no physician-started plan, so that visit, and an established patient's new complaint, is billed under the NP's own number at 85%.

Split or shared visits apply only in facility settings where incident-to is not allowed: hospital inpatient, observation, outpatient and emergency department visits, and SNF visits. Office visits cannot be split or shared. When a physician and an NP in the same group both see the patient, the one who performed the substantive portion bills: more than half of the combined total time, or a substantive part of the MDM (for critical care, time only). The claim carries modifier FS, and the record names both clinicians and is signed and dated by the one who bills.

Telehealth

Licensure follows the patient: the NP must meet the requirements of the state where the patient is located during the visit. The Nurse Licensure Compact covers RN and LPN licenses only; a separate APRN Compact has been written but is not yet in effect, so an NP needs an APRN license in each state where patients are seen. HIPAA applies to telehealth as to any visit.

For Medicare, the CMS FAQ (October 2026) says beneficiaries can receive telehealth anywhere in the US, including at home, through December 31, 2027, and audio-only services at home are also allowed through that date. From January 1, 2028, nonbehavioral telehealth again generally requires a rural medical facility as the originating site. Behavioral health telehealth already has no geographic or site restrictions and allows audio-only; for patients who start mental health care at home by telehealth after 2027, an in-person visit within 6 months before the first telehealth visit and then every 12 months is required. Telehealth to the home is coded with place of service 10 and paid at the nonfacility rate. Commercial and Medicaid rules vary by plan and state.

Responsible use of AI, voice recognition and the EHR

No federal rule tells a clinician exactly how to use an AI scribe or chatbot, so the exam tests principles drawn from rules that do exist; the practice points here are a synthesis. The signer owns the note: dictated, voice-recognized and AI-drafted text is read, corrected and edited before signing, because transcription errors (a wrong drug, dose or laterality) become the legal record. A vendor that receives protected health information for the practice, such as an ambient scribe service, is a business associate and needs a business associate agreement; pasting identifiable patient data into a public tool without one is a disclosure HIPAA does not permit. Minimum necessary applies to what is shared. Tell patients when a recording tool is in use and follow any state consent-to-record law.

Decision support carries its own duties. Under the ACA Section 1557 rule (45 CFR 92.210), covered entities must make reasonable efforts to identify the patient care decision support tools they use whose inputs include race, color, national origin, sex, age or disability, and to reduce the risk of discrimination from each one. ONC's HTI-1 rule (2023) required certified EHRs to show 'model card' source information about predictive algorithms so users could judge whether they were fair, valid, effective and safe; an October 2026 deregulatory rule removes those model-card requirements from December 28, 2026, so the clinician cannot count on the EHR to explain how a tool was built or tested. A risk score or AI suggestion supports the NP's judgment; it does not replace it, and acting on it without checking it against the patient is not a defense.

EHR habits that create risk: copying a previous note forward so outdated findings appear as today's, templated examination text for systems not examined, alert fatigue that hides a real interaction warning, and charting in the wrong patient's record. Each makes the record inaccurate, can support a higher bill than the work done, and undermines the record as a defense.

The ANA Code of Ethics (2025) has ten provisions. In brief: respect for the dignity and worth of every person; the primary commitment is to the patient, family, group, community or population; protect the rights, health and safety of those in care; accountability and responsibility for one's own practice; duties to oneself, including safety and competence; improving the ethical environment of the workplace; advancing the profession through research, standards and policy; collaborating with others to meet shared health goals; working through professional organizations for justice and against health disparities; and joining global efforts for human and environmental health. Ethical questions with no clear answer go to an ethics consultation or committee.

Quality improvement

The Model for Improvement asks three questions. What are we trying to accomplish? (a time-bound, measurable aim for a defined population). How will we know a change is an improvement? (measures). What change can we make that will result in improvement? Changes are then tested in Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles: plan the test and predict the result, do it on a small scale (one clinician, one day, a few patients), study the data against the prediction, and act by adopting, adapting or abandoning the change before the next cycle. Small, fast cycles under varied conditions come before rolling a change out to the whole practice.

Example: aim to raise colorectal cancer screening among eligible patients in one panel over 6 months. Measure the share of eligible patients with a current screen each month. First cycle: one NP has the medical assistant flag overdue patients at check-in for one week, compares completion with the week before, then keeps, adjusts or drops the step.

Reading the evidence

Study designs are usually ranked by how well they control bias: systematic reviews and meta-analyses of good randomized trials, then individual randomized trials, then cohort studies, then case-control studies, then case series, with expert opinion and mechanism-based reasoning at the bottom. The SORT system used by American Family Physician grades individual studies as level 1 (good-quality patient-oriented evidence), 2 (limited-quality patient-oriented evidence) or 3 (other evidence), and recommendations as A (consistent, good-quality patient-oriented evidence), B (inconsistent or limited-quality patient-oriented evidence) or C (consensus, usual practice, opinion, disease-oriented evidence or case series). Patient-oriented outcomes are those patients care about, such as death, symptoms, function and quality of life; disease-oriented outcomes are surrogates such as blood pressure, glucose or plaque thickness, and a recommendation that rests only on them is rated C, because lowering a surrogate does not always help patients.

Absolute risk reduction (ARR) is the event rate in the control group minus the event rate in the treated group. Relative risk reduction (RRR) is the ARR divided by the control event rate. The number needed to treat (NNT) is 1 divided by the ARR, rounded up, and it only has meaning with its time frame and outcome. Worked example (illustrative numbers): events in 10% of controls and 6% of treated patients over 5 years give an ARR of 4%, an RRR of 40% and an NNT of 25 for 5 years. The same 40% relative reduction in a population with a 1% baseline risk gives an ARR of 0.4% and an NNT of 250, which is why relative measures alone overstate benefit for low-risk patients. NNTs from different trials should not be compared directly, because baseline risk, outcomes and follow-up differ. The number needed to harm is calculated the same way from the absolute increase in an adverse event.
