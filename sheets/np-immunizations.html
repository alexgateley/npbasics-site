Where the schedule stands (November 2026)

CDC still posts the 2025 schedules (addendum July 2, 2025). A January 5, 2026 CDC memo cut the all-children list to 11 diseases, but a federal court stayed it on March 16, 2026, along with every vote the newly appointed ACIP had taken. CDC says the July 2025 recommendations remain in effect for the 2026-27 flu and COVID-19 season while the appeal is pending.

Safety Stayed, not in force: hepatitis B birth dose by shared decision (Dec 2025), COVID-19 by shared decision for everyone (Sept 2025), thimerosal-free flu vaccine only (June 2025), and the January 2026 schedule memo. AAP and ACOG publish their own 2026 schedules; check CDC before each season.

Adolescents (7-18 years)

  • Tdap: 1 dose at 11-12 y; any time for 13-18 y who missed it, regardless of the interval since the last tetanus dose.
  • HPV: routine at 11-12 y, can start at 9. Start at 9-14 y: 2 doses 6-12 months apart (minimum 5 months). Start at ≥15 y or immunocompromised: 3 doses at 0, 1-2 and 6 months.
  • MenACWY: 11-12 y and a booster at 16 y. First dose at 13-15 y: booster at 16-18 y. First dose at 16-18 y: 1 dose.
  • MenB: shared decision at 16-23 y (16-18 preferred), 2 doses ≥6 months apart, same brand. MenABCWY can be used when both are due at one visit.
  • Catch-up anything missed: HepB, IPV, MMR, varicella, HepA, influenza.

Catch-up rules

  • Never restart a series, however long the gap; give the next dose.
  • Use minimum ages and intervals. Doses up to 4 days early count; 5 or more days early must be repeated.
  • The 4-day grace period never applies to the 28 days between two live injected or nasal vaccines.
  • Healthy children need no more Hib once a dose was given at ≥15 months, and no more PCV once a dose was given at ≥24 months.
  • Age 7-18 with incomplete DTaP: Tdap as the first catch-up dose, then Td or Tdap.
  • Varicella catch-up interval: 3 months under 13 years, 4 weeks at 13 and older. MMR: 2 doses ≥4 weeks apart.

Live vaccines and spacing

  • Live: MMR, MMRV, varicella, LAIV, rotavirus, yellow fever, oral typhoid. Zoster (RZV) is not live.
  • Two live injected or nasal vaccines: same day or ≥4 weeks apart. If less, repeat the second one ≥4 weeks later.
  • Oral live vaccines and any inactivated vaccine: any interval.
  • TB test with MMR: same day, or wait 4 weeks after MMR (applies to TST and IGRA).
  • Antibody products delay MMR and varicella by up to 11 months, depending on the product; inactivated vaccines, LAIV and rotavirus are not affected.
  • No live vaccines with severe immunodeficiency, chemotherapy (wait ≥3 months after), prednisone ≥20 mg/day for ≥14 days (wait ≥1 month after stopping), or anti-TNF therapy (wait 3 months after a dose).

The 2025-2026 changes in order

In May 2025 an HHS directive removed the routine COVID-19 recommendation for healthy children and for pregnant women; CDC folded it into the 2025 schedule by the July 2, 2025 addendum. In June 2025 the Secretary replaced the ACIP membership. The reconstituted committee then voted for thimerosal-free influenza vaccine (June 2025), COVID-19 vaccination by shared clinical decision-making for everyone (September 2025), and shared decision-making on the hepatitis B birth dose for infants of mothers who test HBsAg negative (December 2025). On January 5, 2026 the acting CDC director signed a memo that kept measles, mumps, rubella, polio, pertussis, tetanus, diphtheria, Hib, pneumococcal disease, HPV and varicella on the all-children list and moved other vaccines to high-risk or shared-decision categories, with insurance coverage kept for all three categories.

On March 16, 2026 a federal district court in Massachusetts stayed the January memo, the appointments of 13 ACIP members, and all votes they had taken. The government appealed. CDC's 2026-27 COVID-19 and influenza guidance says the July 2025 schedule recommendations remain in effect because of the legal uncertainty. For a board candidate the safe answer is the established schedule: hepatitis B at birth, routine influenza from 6 months, and the full childhood series.

Professional societies publish their own schedules. AAP's 2026 schedule keeps the hepatitis B birth dose and the 1-2 and 6-18 month doses, and its 2026-27 COVID-19 policy recommends vaccination for all children 6-23 months, a dose for children 2-18 years at increased risk, and a dose for other children whose parents want it. ACOG's 2026 maternal schedule recommends influenza, COVID-19 and Tdap in every pregnancy and maternal RSV vaccine in the first eligible pregnancy. AAP notes that a shared-decision recommendation still gives a payment pathway, so insurers should cover the dose.

A 4-dose series is acceptable when a combination vaccine containing hepatitis B follows the monovalent birth dose. Only monovalent hepatitis B vaccine is used before 6 weeks of age.

RSV protection for infants

Every infant should be protected in the first RSV season by one route: maternal Abrysvo at 32 weeks 0 days through 36 weeks 6 days of gestation, or an infant monoclonal antibody. If the mother was vaccinated at least 14 days before delivery, most infants need no antibody. If she was not, if her status is unknown, if she was vaccinated in an earlier pregnancy only, or if delivery came less than 14 days after the dose, the infant gets nirsevimab within the first week of life (ideally before discharge) when born October-March, or just before the season when born April-September.

Clesrovimab (Enflonsia) was licensed in June 2025 as a second infant antibody for the first season, with no preference between the two in the ACIP report. The CDC notes page still names only nirsevimab. The clesrovimab recommendation covers infants under 8 months; the CDC notes give nirsevimab for the second season, for children 8-19 months with certain high-risk conditions or who are American Indian or Alaska Native.

Exam tip Maternal RSV vaccine is given once. In a later pregnancy, do not repeat it: protect the infant with an antibody. ACOG's September 2026 addendum extends the maternal window to March 1 for an expected longer season.

Pneumococcal, zoster and RSV in older adults

Pneumococcal: every adult 50 and older, and adults 19-49 with a risk condition (see the CDC notes for the list), who has not had a conjugate vaccine gets one dose of PCV15, PCV20 or PCV21. After PCV20 or PCV21 nothing more is needed. After PCV15, give PPSV23 at least 1 year later, or as soon as 8 weeks later for immunocompromise, cochlear implant or CSF leak. Earlier PCV13 or PPSV23 histories follow CDC's pneumococcal app.

Zoster: recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV, Shingrix) is not live, so it is safe in immunocompromise and recommended from age 19 in that group, including HIV at any CD4 count. Two doses 2-6 months apart (minimum 4 weeks). Give it after a past shingles episode or prior Zostavax; defer during an active episode. No ACIP recommendation in pregnancy.

RSV: one lifetime dose for everyone 75 and older and for adults 50-74 at increased risk of severe disease (Arexvy, Abrysvo or mResvia). No booster is recommended. Late summer to early fall is best, but it may be given any time.

Influenza and COVID-19 details

  • All 2026-27 influenza vaccines are trivalent. No product is preferred under 65; at 65 and older, high-dose, recombinant or adjuvanted vaccine is preferred.
  • Best timing is September or October. Avoid July-August for most adults, especially 65+, except that third-trimester patients may be vaccinated early to protect the newborn.
  • Egg allergy: any age-appropriate influenza vaccine, egg-based or not.
  • LAIV (ages 2-49) is contraindicated in pregnancy, immunocompromise, asplenia, cochlear implant, CSF leak, children 2-4 with asthma or wheeze in the past 12 months, children on aspirin, close contacts of people needing a protected environment, and after recent influenza antivirals (oseltamivir or zanamivir 48 hours, peramivir 5 days, baloxavir 17 days).
  • COVID-19, 2026-27: one dose for all adults; children 6 months-17 years with moderate or severe immunocompromise are vaccinated routinely, and other children by shared decision. Myocarditis or pericarditis within 3 weeks of a COVID-19 dose is a precaution.

MMR and varicella in adults

Evidence of measles immunity: birth before 1957 (not for health care personnel), documented doses, or lab evidence. One dose for most adults without evidence; two doses at least 4 weeks apart for postsecondary students, international travelers, close contacts of immunocompromised people and health care personnel. A woman of childbearing age without rubella immunity gets one dose, but not during pregnancy; screen in pregnancy and vaccinate after delivery.

HIV: MMR and varicella are contraindicated with CD4 below 15% or below 200 cells/mm³. With CD4 at least 15% and at least 200 (for 6 months, for MMR), give MMR as 2 doses 4 weeks apart; varicella may be considered as 2 doses 3 months apart. Varicella evidence: US birth before 1980 (except health care personnel and pregnant women), 2 documented doses, a health care provider-verified history of varicella or zoster, or lab evidence.

After MMR or varicella, a patient who could become pregnant should use careful contraception for 1 month. Stop acyclovir, valacyclovir or famciclovir 24 hours before varicella vaccine and avoid them for 14 days after. Aspirin or salicylate use is also a varicella precaution.

Antibody products and live vaccines

Immune globulin and blood products can blunt the response to MMR and varicella. The wait depends on the product and dose: about 3 months after HBIG or tetanus immune globulin, 6 months after packed red cells, and up to 11 months after high-dose IGIV for Kawasaki disease. If an antibody product is given within 14 days after MMR or varicella, repeat the vaccine after the interval unless serology shows immunity. Inactivated vaccines, LAIV, rotavirus and oral typhoid are not affected and can be given at any time around an antibody product.

Immunosuppression: after immunosuppressive chemotherapy, live vaccines can be given at least 3 months after treatment ends. Withhold live and non-live vaccines for at least 6 months after anti-B-cell therapy such as rituximab, since the response is poor (RZV is the exception, at 1 month). Give needed vaccines at least 2 weeks before starting a biologic such as an anti-TNF agent, and hold live vaccines for 3 months after a dose. High-dose steroids mean prednisone 20 mg/day or more, or 2 mg/kg/day or more in a child over 10 kg, for 14 days or longer; defer live vaccines until at least 1 month after stopping. Inhaled, topical, injected, short-course and physiologic-replacement steroids are not a reason to withhold live vaccines.

Tetanus, pertussis and wound care

  • Unvaccinated adult: Tdap, then Td or Tdap at least 4 weeks later, then a third dose 6-12 months later; boosters every 10 years.
  • Clean minor wound after 3 or more prior doses: booster if more than 10 years since the last. Any other wound: booster if more than 5 years.
  • Tdap can be given at any interval after a prior tetanus-containing vaccine.
  • Arthus reaction after a prior tetanus or diphtheria vaccine: wait at least 10 years after the last tetanus dose.
  • Progressive or unstable neurologic disorder or uncontrolled seizures: hold Tdap until the condition is stable.

Exam pearls

  • Mild URI, otitis or low fever: vaccinate today. Moderate or severe illness: defer.
  • Pregnant mother at home is not a reason to delay MMR, varicella or rotavirus for the child. Household contacts of an immunocompromised person get every routine vaccine; wash hands after diapers for 1 month after rotavirus, and avoid contact if a varicella vaccine rash appears.
  • Preterm infants are vaccinated by chronological age; only the hepatitis B birth dose changes for infants under 2,000 g.
  • Rotavirus has hard age limits: start by 14 weeks 6 days, finish by 8 months.
  • HPV started at 15 or older, or in immunocompromise: 3 doses. Started at 9-14: 2 doses.
  • Missed the 28-day window between MMR and varicella given on different days: repeat the second vaccine.
  • PreHevbrio and LAIV are the vaccines to avoid in pregnancy that candidates forget; MMR and varicella are the classic ones.
Exam tip Because the federal schedule is in court, boards are likely to test the long-standing rules on this page, not the stayed 2025-2026 changes.
