Health Screening by Age

USPSTF A and B screening for adults by age and sex, start and stop ages, intervals and exceptions, the I and D statements boards ask about, and Bright Futures in brief

Checked against current sources November 2026

What the grades mean

GradeMeaningIn practice
AHigh certainty of substantial net benefitOffer or provide it
BModerate net benefit (high certainty), or moderate to substantial (moderate certainty)Offer or provide it
CAt least moderate certainty that the net benefit is smallOffer to selected patients after shared decision-making

Preconception and pregnancy in brief

  • Folic acid 0.4-0.8 mg daily for anyone who plans to or could become pregnant, from at least 1 month before conception through the first 2-3 months (A).
  • First visit: HIV, syphilis and HBsAg (A); Rh(D) type and antibody (A); hepatitis C as part of the adult screen; urine culture for asymptomatic bacteriuria (B).
  • Rh(D)-negative and unsensitized: repeat antibody test at 24-28 weeks (B). Gestational diabetes at 24 weeks or later (B). BP at every visit for hypertensive disorders (B).
  • Low-dose aspirin 81 mg daily after 12 weeks if at high risk for preeclampsia (prior preeclampsia, multifetal gestation, chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes, kidney or autoimmune disease) (B).
  • Detail on prenatal care belongs on the NP women's health and prenatal page.

Children and adolescents in brief

  • Bright Futures (AAP) sets the well-child schedule; see the NP pediatrics page for development, growth and vaccines.
  • Caregiver depression screening at 1, 2, 4 and 6 months; developmental screening at 9, 18 and 30 months; autism screening at 18 and 24 months.
  • Blood pressure yearly from age 3; hemoglobin at 12 months; lead by risk, or universally at 12 and 24 months where Medicaid or local prevalence requires it.
  • Lipids once at 9-11 and once at 17-21; depression and suicide risk yearly from 12; HIV at least once at 15-21.
  • USPSTF B: vision at least once at 3-5; fluoride varnish from first tooth eruption in children under 5, and oral fluoride from 6 months if the water is fluoride-deficient; intensive behavioral treatment (26+ contact hours) for BMI at the 95th percentile or above from age 6; tobacco-use prevention in school-aged children; sun-protection counseling for fair-skinned people 6 months to 24 years.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Reading a recommendation the way the boards do

Every USPSTF statement applies to people without signs or symptoms of the condition, seen in primary care. A patient with rectal bleeding, a breast lump or a cough with weight loss needs a diagnostic workup, not a screening test. When a stem gives a symptom, the screening schedule is usually the distractor.

The letter grade carries two judgments: how large the net benefit is and how sure the Task Force is about it. A and B mean the service should be offered. A and B grades are also the ones the Task Force describes as relevant to the Affordable Care Act's preventive coverage rules. C means the benefit for the whole group is small, so it is offered to some people after a discussion. D and I are different: D is evidence that the service does not help or does harm; I is a gap in the evidence.

Exam tip When two age ranges meet, read the grade at the edge. Colorectal: 45 is B, 50 is A, 76 is C, 86 is stop. Prostate: 55-69 is C, 70 is D. Breast: 40-74 is B, 75 is I.

Cancer screening details and exceptions

  • Cervical, stopping after 65 (only if not otherwise at high risk): adequate prior screening means three negative cytology results in a row or two negative cotests in a row in the 10 years before stopping, with the latest in the last 5 years. Do not restart for a new partner. After treated or regressed CIN 2 or worse, keep screening for at least 20 years, even past 65.
  • Cervical, hysterectomy: stop only if the cervix was removed and there was no CIN 2, CIN 3 or cancer. Confirm with the operative report or exam.
  • Cervical, the 2024 draft: primary HPV testing every 5 years becomes the preferred test at 30-65, collected by a clinician or by the patient; cytology every 3 years and cotesting every 5 years stay acceptable. The 2018 statement is still the final one.
  • Breast: the 2024 statement starts at 40 and uses a 2-year interval, which had a better balance of benefit and harm than yearly screening. It covers women and others assigned female at birth, including those with a first-degree relative with breast cancer or dense breasts, but not BRCA carriers, those with chest radiation at a young age or those with prior breast cancer.
  • Colorectal: any of the listed tests counts. A positive stool test or CT colonography needs a colonoscopy to complete screening. At 76-85 the people most likely to benefit are those never screened.
  • Lung: a pack-year is a pack a day for a year. Offer cessation support to anyone still smoking, and use shared decision-making before the first scan.
  • BRCA: use a brief familial risk tool (for example the Ontario, Manchester, Pedigree Assessment or 7-question family history tools). General breast cancer risk models such as the Gail-based tool are not built to find BRCA risk.
  • Breast cancer risk-reducing medication: offer tamoxifen, raloxifene or an aromatase inhibitor to women 35 or older at increased risk and low risk of side effects (B); not for women without increased risk (D). Raloxifene is for postmenopausal women only.

Cardiometabolic details

  • Blood pressure: measure in the office, seated after 5 minutes of rest, then confirm with ambulatory or home monitoring before diagnosing and treating. The USPSTF evidence used a home threshold of 135/85 and notes that organizations' office cutoffs range from 130/80 to 140/90.
  • Diabetes: fasting glucose, A1c or a 75 g 2-hour glucose tolerance test. Diabetes is fasting 126 or more, A1c 6.5% or more or 2-hour 200 or more, confirmed by repeat testing; prediabetes is fasting 100-125, A1c 5.7-6.4% or 2-hour 140-199. Consider starting before 35 in higher-risk groups (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian American, Black, Hispanic or Latino, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander) and with a family history of diabetes, prior gestational diabetes or PCOS. Refer prediabetes to a lifestyle program or consider metformin (off-label for this use).
  • Statins: the risk factors that qualify are dyslipidemia, diabetes, hypertension or smoking. Moderate intensity is suggested. The statement does not cover LDL above 190 mg/dL or familial hypercholesterolemia; those patients are outside this risk-based approach.
  • Aspirin for primary prevention: 40-59 with a 10-year risk of 10% or more is C; starting it at 60 or older is D.
  • Osteoporosis: risk factors in younger postmenopausal women include low body weight, a parent with a hip fracture, current smoking and heavy alcohol use. Screen with central DXA (hip or spine). No repeat interval is set; repeating within 4-8 years did not improve fracture prediction in the cohorts reviewed.

Infection screening details

  • HIV: repeat screening is reasonable for men who have sex with men, people who inject drugs, those with a new partner since the last test, and in high-prevalence settings. CDC advises at least yearly testing with ongoing risk and every 3-6 months for some sexually active men who have sex with men, using an opt-out approach.
  • Hepatitis C: anti-HCV antibody first; a positive result is confirmed with HCV RNA. Screen once in most adults, periodically with ongoing risk such as injection drug use.
  • Hepatitis B: risk groups include people born where prevalence is 2% or higher, US-born people not vaccinated as infants whose parents came from regions with 8% or higher, people with HIV, people who inject drugs, men who have sex with men, and household contacts or sex partners of people with HBV. CDC adds periodic retesting while risk continues. CDC's triple panel is HBsAg, anti-HBs and total anti-HBc.
  • Chlamydia and gonorrhea in women 25+: increased risk means a new partner or more than one partner, a partner with other partners or with an STI, a prior or current STI, inconsistent condoms outside a monogamous relationship, exchanging sex for money or drugs, or a history of incarceration.
  • Syphilis: the traditional order is a nontreponemal test (RPR or VDRL) confirmed by a treponemal test; the reverse sequence starts with a treponemal immunoassay. Men who have sex with men and people with HIV may need screening at least yearly, or every 3-6 months at high risk. In pregnancy, screen at the first visit; the USPSTF makes no call on repeat screening. As the USPSTF page reports, CDC advises rescreening at 28 weeks and at delivery for those at high risk, and ACOG advises rescreening everyone in the third trimester and at birth.
  • Latent TB: IGRA is often the better choice after BCG vaccination or when the patient may not return for a skin test reading at 48-72 hours.

Mental health, substance use and safety details

  • Depression: a PHQ-9 of 10 or more is the usual positive cutoff. A positive screen needs a diagnostic interview, a severity assessment and a plan for care. Screening for suicide risk alone is an I statement, but a patient who screens positive for depression is still asked about suicidal thoughts as part of that assessment.
  • Anxiety: GAD-2 of 3 or more and GAD-7 of 10 or more were the cutoffs studied. A screen does not make the diagnosis.
  • Alcohol: the single question asks about heavy-drinking days, 5 or more drinks for men and 4 or more for women and adults over 65.
  • Drugs: screening means asking questions, not urine testing, and does not apply where results would be punitive or care is not available. Tools named include the NIDA Quick Screen, ASSIST, TAPS and, in pregnancy, the Prenatal Risk Overview.
  • Tobacco: the medications named are nicotine replacement, bupropion SR and varenicline. E-cigarettes for quitting are an I statement; steer patients to proven methods.
  • Intimate partner violence: tools include HARK, HITS and WAST. Brief counseling and a referral list alone did not help; positive screens need referral to multicomponent, ongoing support.
  • Falls: identify increased risk by asking about prior falls and problems with mobility, gait or balance; then prescribe exercise (gait, balance and functional training).

Children and adolescents: what to know beyond the list

The Bright Futures/AAP periodicity schedule (updated February 2025) separates universal screens from risk assessments. Universal screens on the schedule include newborn blood and hearing screening, bilirubin and pulse oximetry for critical congenital heart disease before discharge, the developmental, autism and caregiver depression screens, yearly blood pressure from 3, hearing by audiometry once at 11-14, 15-17 and 18-21, depression and suicide risk from 12, HIV at least once between 15 and 21 and hepatitis C at least once from 18. Tobacco, alcohol and drug use is a risk assessment from 11 (the CRAFFT tool is suggested). Fluoride varnish is applied every 3-6 months once teeth erupt.

Where the AAP and the USPSTF differ, the USPSTF gives an I statement and Bright Futures still recommends the service: universal lipid screening and blood pressure screening in children are the two examples on this page. Boards that ask 'per Bright Futures' expect the AAP schedule; boards that ask 'per the USPSTF' expect the I statement.

Exam tip The schedule asks that adolescent screening for depression and suicide risk, and for HIV, be done with every effort to keep the teen's answers confidential.

Exam pearls

  • Pap at 19 with a new partner: no (D under 21, whatever the sexual history).
  • Woman 66 with three normal Paps in 10 years, last 2 years ago: stop.
  • Man 70, quit 20 years ago, 40 pack-years: no lung CT (quit more than 15 years).
  • Man 68 who smoked 10 years in his twenties: one-time AAA ultrasound.
  • Woman 58, BMI 19, smokes: use a risk tool, then DXA if risk is increased.
  • Man 48 at average risk: colorectal screening (B from 45).
  • Adult 45 with BMI 24 who is Asian American: diabetes screening qualifies (BMI 23 or more).
