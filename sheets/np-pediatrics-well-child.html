Pediatrics: Growth, Development and Well-Child Visits

Bright Futures visits and screens, CDC milestones, primitive reflexes, growth charts and BMI, vital signs, HEADSS and confidentiality, safe sleep, car seats and weight-based dosing

Bright Futures: what happens at which visit

Visits: newborn, 3–5 days, by 1 month, then 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 24 and 30 months, then every year from 3 to 21. Surveillance, history, exam and anticipatory guidance happen at every visit.

Adolescents: confidentiality and HEADSS

  • Spend part of every adolescent visit alone with the teen, and explain at the start what stays private and what you would have to share (abuse reporting, serious risk of harm).
  • HEADSS (versions vary): Home, Education/employment, Eating, Activities, Drugs, Sexuality, Suicide/depression, Safety. Start with the least sensitive topics.
  • Bright Futures asks for depression, HIV and hepatitis B screening with every effort to keep it confidential.
  • Federal Title X clinics may not require parental consent or notify parents when a minor seeks family planning care. Other minor-consent rights come from state law.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

How the Bright Futures schedule works

The Bright Futures/AAP Recommendations for Preventive Pediatric Health Care (the periodicity schedule) is updated each year; the current version reflects changes approved in December 2024 and was published in February 2025 with no changes to clinical guidance. It assumes a healthy child with nurturing caregivers who is growing and developing well; children with concerns need extra visits. A child who enters care late, or misses an item, is brought up to date at the earliest visit.

The grid uses two marks. A solid mark means the service is done for every child at that visit. An open mark means a risk assessment, with the test done only if the assessment is positive. A shaded bar means the service can be done at any visit within the range. This distinction is the usual trap: lipids, for example, are universal only in the 9–11 and 17–21 windows and risk-based at 2, 4, 6 and 8 years and 12 to 16; hemoglobin is universal only at 12 months.

Newborn period: a prenatal visit is offered to first-time and high-risk parents. The newborn gets the state blood-spot panel (the Recommended Uniform Screening Panel sets the federal recommendation; each state's law sets its own panel), a bilirubin check, CCHD pulse oximetry and a hearing screen. Babies discharged before 48 hours are seen within 48 to 72 hours of discharge, and every newborn has a visit at 3 to 5 days that looks at feeding and jaundice. Hepatitis B infection risk is assessed at the newborn visit.

Mental health: behavioral and social-emotional screening is listed at every visit, family-centered, and may include caregiver mental health and social determinants such as poverty and racism. Maternal (perinatal) depression screening is done at the 1, 2, 4 and 6 month visits; the infant's clinician screens the parent. From 11, a risk assessment for tobacco, alcohol and drugs is done yearly, and the schedule names the CRAFFT tool; if opioid use is a concern it suggests prescribing naloxone. Depression and suicide risk screening is yearly from 12. The USPSTF grades depression screening at 12 to 18 years B, and gives an I statement for depression screening at 11 and younger and for suicide risk screening; it names the PHQ-9 and the PHQ-A.

Other adolescent items: STI risk assessment from 11 (Red Book rules); HIV screening at least once between 15 and 21, yearly or more often if at risk; hepatitis C at least once from 18; a sudden cardiac arrest and death risk assessment from 11; and cervical cytology starting at 21, with pelvic exams before 21 only for specific indications. Oral health: from 6 months, check for a dental home and refer if there is none; apply fluoride varnish to primary teeth from eruption, every 3 to 6 months in the office or dental clinic by caries risk; consider fluoride supplements if the water supply is low in fluoride; recommend fluoride toothpaste in an age-appropriate amount.

Newborn screens: CCHD and hearing

Critical congenital heart disease (CCHD) screening uses pulse oximetry on the right hand and a foot, done at 24 hours of age or later (as late as possible if the baby goes home sooner), with the baby alert and off supplemental oxygen. CDC's algorithm (Oster 2025) passes the baby when saturation is 95% or higher in both the right hand and the foot and the hand-foot difference is 3% or less. The screen fails with any reading below 90%, or with saturation below 95% in the right hand or foot, or a difference of more than 3%, on two measurements taken 1 hour apart. A failed screen needs an evaluation for causes of hypoxemia, usually an echocardiogram, and a prompt call to the pediatric clinician. A pass does not rule out every CCHD.

Hearing follows the 1-3-6 rule: screen no later than 1 month, complete a full audiologic evaluation by 3 months if the screen is not passed, and enroll in early intervention before 6 months. At the first visits after birth, confirm that the newborn screen was done and act on the result. After infancy, Bright Futures gives objective hearing screening at 4, 5, 6, 8 and 10 years and, in adolescents, audiometry that includes the high frequencies of 6,000 and 8,000 Hz once in each of the 11–14, 15–17 and 18–21 windows, because noise-induced loss starts at high frequencies.

Development: surveillance, screening and autism

Surveillance and screening are different jobs. Surveillance is done at every visit: ask about the parents' concerns, take a developmental history, observe the child and check milestones. Screening is giving a standardized, validated tool at 9, 18 and 30 months, or whenever surveillance raises a concern. AAP's 2020 report adds special attention at the 4 to 5 year visit before school entry. A concern on surveillance is followed by a standardized screen or a direct referral to early intervention and specialty care; the clinician does not wait for the next scheduled screen.

In 2022 CDC and AAP revised the milestone checklists. A milestone now has to be something at least 75% of children achieve by that visit age, and checklists for 15 and 30 months were added, so there is a checklist for every well-child visit from 2 months to 5 years. About a third of the retained milestones moved, two-thirds of those to older ages. The practical effect for the boards: a child who has not reached a listed milestone is already behind most peers, so the answer is to screen or refer, not to recheck in a few months.

Autism: AAP recommends a standardized autism screen at 18 and 24 months, alongside surveillance, because autism can be diagnosed from 18 months and early intervention helps. The usual tool is the M-CHAT-R/F, a parent questionnaire followed by a structured follow-up interview for positive answers; a positive result after the interview leads to a full diagnostic evaluation and early-intervention referral without waiting for the diagnosis. Its positive predictive value in two large US studies of children aged 16 to 30 months was about 50%. The USPSTF (2016, update in progress) gives an I statement for screening children whose parents and clinicians have raised no concern; boards written to Bright Futures key 18 and 24 months.

  • Language: babbling by 9 months, a few words by 15 to 18 months, two-word phrases by 24 months and speech understood most of the time by 3 years. Any language delay starts with a formal hearing evaluation.
  • Hand preference: a firm preference before about 18 months is a reason to examine the other arm for weakness (standard teaching; the CDC checklists do not list it).
  • Social: social smile by 2 months, stranger wariness and response to name around 9 months, pointing to show something by 18 months. Absent pointing, poor eye contact or loss of words calls for an autism screen.

Primitive reflexes in more detail

Primitive reflexes are brainstem and spinal patterns present at birth that fade as the cortex matures and voluntary movement takes over; the palmar grasp goes as the infant starts to use the hands, the plantar grasp as the infant starts to stand. A reflex is abnormal when it is absent or weak while it should be present, exaggerated, asymmetric, or still present after the age it normally disappears. Preterm infants follow the same timetable by corrected age.

Moro: with the infant supine, let the head drop back slightly with support; the arms abduct and extend with the fingers spread, then come together. Futagi's review finds it in all infants through 12 weeks and usually gone by 6 months; Iowa's table gives 3 to 4 months, normal to 6. Absence in the newborn suggests birth injury, asphyxia, intracranial hemorrhage, infection, brain malformation or generalized weakness. An exaggerated Moro is common in neonatal opioid withdrawal. Asymmetry usually means a local problem on the weak side, such as brachial plexus injury or a clavicle fracture, although one prospective study of 22 infants with clavicle fractures found no asymmetry, so the finding is not sensitive.

Grasp reflexes: pressing into the palm closes the fingers; pressing the sole behind the toes flexes them. The palmar grasp is present in all infants for 3 months and gone by about 6; the plantar grasp is present for 6 months and gone by about 12 (Iowa allows to 15). An absent or asymmetric palmar grasp points to a nerve, plexus or cord problem. A weak or absent plantar grasp in early infancy is associated with spastic cerebral palsy, and unusually strong or prolonged retention with athetoid cerebral palsy and intellectual disability. Futagi regards a Moro still present after 6 months as abnormal.

Others: the asymmetric tonic neck reflex (turn the head and the arm on that side extends, the other flexes, the 'fencing' posture) is gone by about 6 months; persistence past 6 months is abnormal. Rooting fades by about 4 months, Galant by about 4 months, stepping by 2 to 3 months. An upgoing toe on plantar stimulation (Babinski) is normal until about 1 to 2 years. The parachute response, arms thrown out when the infant is tipped forward, appears at 8 to 9 months and stays for life; its absence late in the first year, or an asymmetric response, is a concern.

Growth charts, measurement and BMI

CDC and AAP recommend the WHO growth standards for all US children from birth to 2 years, however they are fed, and the CDC charts from 2 to 20 years. The WHO charts describe growth under optimal conditions in a mostly breastfed population. On them, CDC uses the 2nd and 98th percentiles (really the 2.3rd and 97.7th, 2 standard deviations) as the cut-offs: weight-for-length below the 2nd is low, above the 98th is high, and length-for-age below the 2nd is short stature. BMI is not used under 2.

Measure length lying down until 2 and standing height after; standing height reads about 0.8 cm shorter. Together with the change of reference, this can move a child's percentile at 24 months, so a drop at the switch is not by itself a growth problem; follow the trend. Formula-fed infants gain faster after about 3 months than breastfed infants and may plot high on the WHO chart.

Expected growth (Merck): newborns lose 5% to 8% of birth weight in the first days and regain it within 2 weeks; weight doubles by 5 months and triples by 12; length rises about 25 cm in the first year; head circumference grows about 1 cm a month in the first year; from 1 to 10 years children grow about 7.6 cm a year. Most infants have 6 teeth at 12 months and all 20 primary teeth by 2½ years.

BMI-for-age (CDC, ages 2 to 19): under the 5th percentile is underweight, the 5th to under the 85th healthy, the 85th to under the 95th overweight, and the 95th or higher obesity. Severe obesity is a BMI at 120% of the 95th percentile or more, or 35 kg/m² or more; AAP splits it into class 2 (120% to under 140%, or 35 to under 40) and class 3 (140% or more, or 40 or more). Because the standard charts flatten at the top, CDC's 2022 extended BMI charts add curves above the 97th percentile for following children with high BMI.

Vital signs and blood pressure

Fleming's 2011 systematic review pooled heart rate data on more than 143,000 healthy children and respiratory rate data on about 3,900. Median heart rate rises from 127 at birth to a peak of 145 at about 1 month, then falls to 113 by 2 years and keeps falling through adolescence. Median respiratory rate falls 40% in the first 2 years, from 44 to 26, and continues down more slowly. The review found that the ranges in common life-support courses often disagree with the measured centiles, so a rate just outside a memorized range in a calm, well child needs context, while a rate that keeps rising matters at any age.

Fever is a rectal (core) temperature of 38.0 °C (100.4 °F) or higher. Treat fever to make the child comfortable; use one antipyretic at a time, since alternating products invites dosing errors.

Blood pressure (AAP 2017): measure at every preventive visit from 3 years, and before 3 only in children with specific risk conditions. Auscultation is the reference method, seated after 5 minutes of rest, on the right arm with a correctly sized cuff (bladder length about 80% to 100% of the mid-arm circumference). The guideline's norms come from normal-weight children, and it uses a simplified screening table to flag readings that need a closer look against the full percentile tables.

The adolescent visit

Teens disclose more when they trust that what they say stays private. The Society for Adolescent Health and Medicine (2025) calls confidentiality an essential part of adolescent and young adult care and asks clinicians to support parents' involvement while protecting private time. In practice: see the teen and parent together, then the teen alone; tell both at the start what is confidential and what is not; and encourage the teen to involve a parent where that is safe. Limits are set by law and safety: suspected abuse must be reported under state law, and a serious risk of harm to self or others is shared with the people who need to act.

Federal law protects one setting everywhere: a Title X family planning project may not require a parent's or guardian's consent to serve a minor and may not notify a parent before or after a minor seeks care; it encourages family participation where practical. Title X projects must also keep client information confidential and warn clients if information could reach a policyholder who is not the client, the usual leak through an insurance explanation of benefits. Beyond that, which services a minor can consent to (STI care, contraception, mental health, substance use) depends on state law, so the answer for a given state is 'in states that allow it'. Patient portals and shared records can expose sensitive notes, so know how your system handles adolescent records.

HEADSS is a structured psychosocial interview that moves from less sensitive to more sensitive topics. Versions differ: Tan 2025 lists home, education, eating, activities, drugs, sexual activity, sleep, mood and suicidality; the longer HEEADSSS adds safety (seat belts, helmets, weapons, violence, online risks). Start with home and school, normalize the questions ('I ask all my patients about...'), and record what is protective as well as what is risky.

  • Home: who lives there, relationships, safety at home.
  • Education/employment: grades, attendance, bullying, plans.
  • Eating: body image, dieting, binging or purging.
  • Activities: friends, sport, screen and social media use.
  • Drugs: tobacco and vaping, alcohol, cannabis, other drugs (CRAFFT from 11).
  • Sexuality: orientation, activity, consent, contraception, STI risk.
  • Suicide/depression: mood, self-harm, suicidal thoughts (yearly screen from 12).
  • Safety: seat belts, helmets, weapons, violence, driving.

Safe sleep and car seats in more detail

About 3,500 US infants die each year of sleep-related causes, and the rate has barely moved since 2000. The AAP 2022 policy, which Safe to Sleep follows, explains SIDS with a triple-risk model (a vulnerable infant, a critical developmental period and an outside stressor such as an unsafe sleep space) and targets the stressor. Place the baby on the back for every sleep until 1 year, including preterm infants and infants with reflux; once the baby rolls both ways unaided, leave them in the position they choose. Use a firm, flat, level surface that meets federal safety standards with only a fitted sheet, and no bumpers, pillows, blankets, positioners, wedges or weighted products. Car seats, strollers, carriers and slings are not for routine sleep; move a sleeping baby to a flat sleep space as soon as possible.

Room-share without bed-sharing for at least the first 6 months. Bed-sharing risk is highest on a sofa or armchair, with an adult who is impaired or smokes, and in infants under 4 months or born preterm. Protective factors are human milk feeding (exclusively for about the first 6 months), a pacifier at sleep once breastfeeding is established (no need to put it back if it falls out; never attached to clothing, toys or blankets), routine vaccines, and a smoke-, vape-, alcohol- and drug-free pregnancy and home. Avoid overheating: no hats indoors, a wearable blanket instead of loose bedding. Swaddling stops when the baby starts to roll, usually around 3 months. Home cardiorespiratory monitors do not prevent SIDS. Supervised tummy time while awake builds strength and prevents flat spots.

Car seats: every step down is a loss of protection, so each stage lasts as long as the seat allows. CDC (2026): rear-facing in the back seat until the seat's height or weight limit, which for most children is age 2 to 4; a rear-facing seat never goes in the front seat. Then a forward-facing seat with harness and top tether until its limit, to at least age 5. Then a belt-positioning booster until the adult belt fits, usually at 9 to 12: the lap belt low on the upper thighs and the shoulder belt across the middle of the chest and shoulder, not the neck. Children ride in the back seat until 13. The AAP 2018 policy states its minimums differently (harness through at least 4 years, booster through at least 8), but the principle is the same. Motor vehicle crashes are a leading cause of death from age 4.

Weight-based dosing principles

  1. Use a current weight in kilograms (pounds ÷ 2.2). OTC labels dose by weight when it is known and by age only when it is not.
  2. Read the order as per dose or per day. Amoxicillin is written per day (mg/kg/day, divided); acetaminophen and ibuprofen per dose (mg/kg/dose).
  3. Cap at the adult dose. The amoxicillin label switches to adult dosing above 40 kg; the IV acetaminophen label gives adult dosing at 50 kg and over.
  4. Convert to volume from the concentration on the bottle, and give an oral syringe or the marked cup that comes with the product, never a household spoon.
  5. Count every source: combination cold products often contain acetaminophen, and the daily maximum includes them all.

Worked example: a 22 lb child weighs 10 kg. Ibuprofen 10 mg/kg is 100 mg, which is 5 mL of the 100 mg/5 mL suspension. Acetaminophen 15 mg/kg is 150 mg, about 4.7 mL of the 160 mg/5 mL suspension.

Acetaminophen: oral doses of 10 to 15 mg/kg every 4 to 6 hours are the doses studied against ibuprofen in febrile children; the OTC label allows no more than 5 doses in 24 hours and tells parents to ask a clinician before giving it under 2 years. The IV label sets the daily ceiling at 75 mg/kg for ages 2 to 12, 60 mg/kg for 29 days to 2 years and 50 mg/kg for neonates, counting all routes. Exceeding the daily maximum can cause liver failure. The children's liquid is 160 mg/5 mL.

Ibuprofen: the prescription label (dosing written for 6 months up to 2 years) gives 5 mg/kg for fever when the temperature is below 102.5 °F and 10 mg/kg at 102.5 °F or above, with fever relief lasting 6 to 8 hours, 10 mg/kg every 6 to 8 hours for pain, and a maximum of 40 mg/kg a day; safety under 6 months is not established. The OTC children's suspension is 100 mg/5 mL, every 6 to 8 hours, no more than 4 times a day, and 'ask a doctor' under 2 years. In the label's trials, 10 mg/kg still held fever down at 8 hours, while fever tended to return by 6 hours after 5 mg/kg, and acetaminophen 10 to 15 mg/kg acted like the 5 mg/kg dose. As with any NSAID, the label warns of stomach and intestinal bleeding, ulceration and perforation.

Antibiotics: the amoxicillin label for children 3 months and older under 40 kg gives 25 mg/kg/day every 12 hours (or 20 mg/kg/day every 8 hours) for mild to moderate ear, nose, throat, skin and urinary infections, and 45 mg/kg/day every 12 hours (or 40 mg/kg/day every 8 hours) for severe infections and lower respiratory infections; under 3 months the ceiling is 30 mg/kg/day every 12 hours because the kidneys are immature. Acute otitis media is treated with a higher dose, 80 to 90 mg/kg/day in two doses, from the AAP guideline (see the HEENT page). Group A strep needs at least 10 days to prevent rheumatic fever.

  • Aspirin: avoid in children and teens, especially with influenza or varicella, because of Reye syndrome.
  • Codeine and tramadol: contraindicated under 12, and under 18 after tonsillectomy or adenoidectomy; ultra-rapid CYP2D6 metabolizers have died of respiratory depression.
  • OTC cough and cold medicines: FDA advises against them under 2 because of serious, potentially life-threatening effects; manufacturers label them 'do not use' under 4.
  • Adult products: do not give a child an adult formulation, and do not reuse a dosing device from another medicine.

Exam pearls

Exam tip 9-month-old not sitting alone: screen now. 18-month-old with no pointing and 2 words: M-CHAT-R/F and hearing test, refer to early intervention. Moro present at 7 months: abnormal. Upgoing toe at 10 months: normal. 20-month-old: plot on the WHO chart, no BMI. BMI at the 96th percentile in a 10-year-old: obesity. 14-year-old at 132/82: stage 1. Teen asks whether what they say is private: yes, except where safety or the law requires sharing. 4-month-old rolling to the stomach: still place on the back. 3-year-old asking to face forward: stay rear-facing to the seat's limit. Ibuprofen for a 4-month-old: no; acetaminophen by weight.
