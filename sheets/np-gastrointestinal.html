Try one

Gastrointestinal and Liver Primary Care practice question

A 67-year-old man has had 2 months of new epigastric discomfort and early fullness after meals. He has no dysphagia, vomiting, weight loss, melena or family history of gastric cancer. He takes lisinopril and no NSAIDs or aspirin. Abdominal exam is benign, and a CBC is normal. Which is the MOST appropriate diagnostic step?

  1. Right upper quadrant ultrasound for gallstones
  2. Stool antigen test for Helicobacter pylori, treating if positive
  3. Empiric proton pump inhibitor for 8 weeks
  4. Upper endoscopy

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Biliary colic causes episodic, intense right upper quadrant pain lasting hours, not continuous postprandial fullness; gallstones are a common incidental finding that would not explain dyspepsia.
  • B. Test-and-treat for H. pylori is the recommended first step for dyspepsia in patients younger than 60, but his age changes the approach.
  • C. Empiric PPI therapy is an option for younger patients who test negative for H. pylori, but it would delay investigation in a patient whose age calls for endoscopy.
  • D. Correct. The ACG and CAG guideline recommends endoscopy for patients aged 60 or older with dyspepsia, even without alarm features, to exclude gastric cancer.

The 2017 ACG and Canadian Association of Gastroenterology dyspepsia guideline uses age 60 as the decision point. Patients 60 or older should have upper endoscopy to look for upper gastrointestinal neoplasia, because cancer risk rises with age. Patients younger than 60 do not need endoscopy for alarm features alone; they are offered H. pylori test-and-treat, followed by PPI therapy, then tricyclic antidepressant or prokinetic therapy if symptoms persist. Gastric biopsies taken at endoscopy can also test for H. pylori, which should be treated if found. Functional dyspepsia is diagnosed only after this work-up is unrevealing.

Sources: ACG and CAG Dyspepsia Guideline (2017); ACG Helicobacter pylori Guideline (2024)

Peptic ulcer and NSAIDs

  • Ulcer treatment: PPI for at least 8 weeks; treat H. pylori if present; stop or cut the NSAID.
  • NSAID ulcer risk factors: older age, prior ulcer, aspirin or another NSAID, anticoagulant, corticosteroid.
  • Must stay on an NSAID: moderate GI risk (1–2 factors), NSAID plus a PPI or misoprostol; high GI risk (more than 2, or a prior complicated ulcer), avoid NSAIDs or use a COX-2 inhibitor plus a PPI. Test and treat H. pylori (eradication halves ulcer risk).
  • High cardiovascular risk: naproxen plus a PPI or misoprostol; high cardiovascular and high GI risk: avoid NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors.
Exam tip Duodenal ulcer pain eases with food and wakes the patient 2–3 hours after meals or at night; gastric ulcer pain worsens with eating.

Celiac disease (ACG 2023)

  • Test with TTG-IgA plus total IgA while the patient is still eating gluten.
  • IgA deficient: IgG-based test (DGP-IgG or TTG-IgG).
  • Confirm with endoscopy: 1–2 biopsies from the duodenal bulb and at least 4 from the distal duodenum. Some children can skip biopsy (TTG-IgA over 10 times normal plus positive EMA on a second sample).
  • Test first-degree relatives; test IBS with diarrhea. No mass screening.
  • Treatment: lifelong strict gluten-free diet (wheat, barley, rye); gluten-free oats are fine; pneumococcal vaccine.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

GERD: what the 2022 ACG guideline changed

For typical heartburn and regurgitation without alarm features, an 8-week once-daily PPI trial is both the treatment and the diagnostic test (strong recommendation). Delayed-release PPIs block only pumps that are actively secreting, so the dose goes 30–60 minutes before a meal; bedtime dosing controls acid less well. If the symptoms respond, the guideline asks clinicians to try stopping the PPI. If they do not respond, or return when the drug is stopped, the next step is endoscopy, ideally 2–4 weeks off the PPI so that erosive disease is not masked. Endoscopy is the first test, not a PPI trial, for dysphagia, weight loss, GI bleeding or several Barrett's risk factors.

Lifestyle advice with evidence behind it is narrow: weight loss for overweight patients (strong), and conditional suggestions to avoid meals in the 2–3 hours before bed, raise the head of the bed for night symptoms, stop tobacco and avoid personal trigger foods. Coffee, citrus and spicy food can provoke reflux in laboratory studies, but there is little evidence that avoiding them helps, so advice is limited to each patient's own triggers.

Maintenance: patients without erosive esophagitis or Barrett's whose symptoms resolve should try to come off, using on-demand dosing if symptoms recur. LA grade C or D esophagitis relapses in almost everyone who stops, so those patients stay on a PPI indefinitely or have antireflux surgery. Long-term users take the lowest effective dose. Adding baclofen, a prokinetic or sucralfate to a PPI that is not working is not recommended; sucralfate is reserved for pregnancy.

Extraesophageal symptoms (chronic cough, hoarseness, throat clearing, asthma): look for other causes first. With no heartburn or regurgitation, the guideline wants reflux testing before a PPI. When typical symptoms are also present, a twice-daily PPI trial for 8–12 weeks is reasonable before further testing. Laryngoscopy findings alone do not make the diagnosis.

Dyspepsia and H. pylori in more depth

ACG/CAG 2017 recommends endoscopy for new dyspepsia at 60 or older and, under 60, a noninvasive H. pylori test with treatment if positive. Patients who test negative or do not improve get a PPI trial, then a tricyclic antidepressant, then a prokinetic. The joint guideline adds that alarm features in a patient under 60 should prompt a case-by-case decision rather than automatic endoscopy, and that a younger patient at higher gastric cancer risk (raised in a high-incidence country, family history) can be offered endoscopy sooner; the 2024 ACG H. pylori guideline suggests 50 as the threshold for such patients. AFP 2023 lists the alarm features and recommends endoscopy for them at any age. Normal endoscopy with persistent symptoms is functional dyspepsia; infected patients are still treated.

About 30–40% of people in North America carry H. pylori, usually acquired in childhood. It causes peptic ulcer and gastric adenocarcinoma and is linked to MALT lymphoma, iron deficiency and ITP. ACG 2024 widened testing to adult household members of a person who tests positive by a nonserologic test, people with gastric premalignant changes (atrophy, intestinal metaplasia), and people at higher gastric cancer risk by family history, ethnicity or immigration from a high-incidence region.

Clarithromycin and levofloxacin resistance have risen sharply in North America, and clarithromycin triple therapy cures only about 30% of clarithromycin-resistant infections. ACG 2024 therefore recommends against clarithromycin triple therapy unless susceptibility testing shows the strain is sensitive. Optimized bismuth quadruple therapy for 14 days is the first-line choice when susceptibility is unknown: a PPI twice daily, bismuth subcitrate 120–300 mg or subsalicylate 300–524 mg four times daily, tetracycline 500 mg four times daily, and metronidazole 500 mg three or four times daily. It is also the regimen after a failed clarithromycin course. Its weak point is adherence: four drugs, up to four times a day.

For patients without a penicillin allergy, two 14-day alternatives are suggested: rifabutin triple therapy (an FDA-approved fixed-dose omeprazole, amoxicillin and rifabutin product taken as 4 capsules three times daily) and vonoprazan dual therapy (vonoprazan 20 mg twice daily with amoxicillin 1,000 mg three times daily). Vonoprazan is a potassium-competitive acid blocker. After a failed optimized bismuth course, rifabutin triple therapy is the empiric choice. Patients labeled penicillin-allergic can be referred for allergy testing, which may open these options.

Everyone treated needs proof of cure: a urea breath test, stool antigen or biopsy at least 4 weeks after the antibiotics, with PPIs (and probably vonoprazan) stopped for 2 weeks beforehand; an H2 blocker or antacid can bridge symptoms. Serology stays positive after cure and is not used for diagnosis of active infection or follow-up.

Ulcers, NSAIDs and long-term PPIs

About 1 in 12 people in the United States has peptic ulcer disease. H. pylori and long-term NSAIDs are the two main causes, and they multiply each other's risk: in a meta-analysis the combination raised the risk of a bleeding ulcer more than sixfold. Risk rises with more than one NSAID and with longer use, and aspirin carries a risk similar to other NSAIDs; aspirin plus clopidogrel is higher still. Ulcers heal with a PPI for at least 8 weeks, with healing rates close to double those of H2 blockers.

Prevention when the NSAID must continue (ACG and Canadian recommendations summarized in AFP 2023): risk factors are older age, previous ulcer, and use of aspirin, another NSAID, an anticoagulant or a corticosteroid. Low GI risk (none) needs no protection. Moderate GI risk (1–2 factors): NSAID plus a PPI or misoprostol. High GI risk (more than 2, or a previous complicated ulcer): avoid NSAIDs if possible, otherwise a COX-2 inhibitor plus a PPI. When cardiovascular risk is high, ACG advises naproxen plus a PPI or misoprostol (the Canadian guideline accepts naproxen alone at low GI risk); with high risk on both sides, avoid NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors. Eradicating H. pylori in NSAID users halves the chance of an ulcer and is more effective than a maintenance PPI alone.

Long-term PPI use has been associated with fractures, chronic kidney disease, C. difficile infection in hospitalized patients, low magnesium, calcium and vitamin B12, dementia and a small rise in gastric cancer, though the evidence for most of these is observational and weak. The FDA discourages pairing omeprazole with clopidogrel because omeprazole inhibits CYP2C19, which activates clopidogrel; pantoprazole is the usual choice when a patient on clopidogrel needs gastroprotection. Deprescribing (lower dose, stop, or use as needed) is reasonable once symptoms have resolved and there is no ongoing indication.

IBS: positive diagnosis and treatment by subtype

The ACG 2021 guideline asks clinicians to diagnose IBS positively from the history and examination instead of by exhausting tests. Rome IV requires recurrent abdominal pain on average at least 1 day a week over the last 3 months, with two or more of: pain related to defecation, a change in stool frequency, or a change in stool form; symptoms should have begun at least 6 months before diagnosis. Bloating is common but not required. Subtype by the share of abnormal stools on the Bristol scale, off drugs that change bowel habit: IBS-C when more than 25% are hard (types 1–2) and fewer than 25% loose, IBS-D the reverse, IBS-M when both exceed 25%.

Testing is limited. With diarrhea symptoms, check celiac serology (TTG-IgA with total IgA) and, if there are no alarm features, fecal calprotectin or lactoferrin and CRP to screen for IBD. Routine stool pathogen testing is not recommended. Colonoscopy is not routine under 45 without warning signs: hematochezia, melena, unintended weight loss, onset at an older age, or a family history of IBD, colon cancer or other significant GI disease.

Treatment: a limited low-FODMAP trial, soluble fiber (psyllium) rather than insoluble fiber, peppermint oil, tricyclic antidepressants for global symptoms, and gut-directed psychotherapy. The guideline advises against probiotics, the antispasmodics available in the United States, and fecal transplant. For IBS-C, the secretagogues lead: guanylate cyclase activators (linaclotide, plecanatide) and the chloride channel activator lubiprostone (FDA-approved for women, 8 mcg twice daily); polyethylene glycol softens stool but did not improve global IBS-C symptoms. For IBS-D: rifaximin, alosetron for women with severe symptoms who have failed conventional treatment, and eluxadoline (a mixed opioid receptor drug).

IBD in primary care

Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis are relapsing immune-mediated inflammation of the gut. Crohn disease can involve any segment, most often the small bowel, with patchy, transmural inflammation that causes strictures, fistulas, abscesses and perianal disease; granulomas are found in 20% of cases or fewer. Ulcerative colitis is confined to the colon, always involves the rectum and extends proximally without skip areas; inflammation is mucosal, and gross rectal bleeding is the rule. Current smoking worsens Crohn disease but lowers the risk of ulcerative colitis, and NSAIDs can trigger flares of either.

Extraintestinal disease is common: peripheral arthritis, episcleritis, aphthous ulcers and erythema nodosum track bowel activity, while ankylosing spondylitis, uveitis, pyoderma gangrenosum and primary sclerosing cholangitis can run their own course. Primary sclerosing cholangitis is strongly tied to colitis and should prompt a search for IBD. Health maintenance the primary care NP owns: yearly influenza vaccine, pneumococcal vaccine for those over 50 or on immune-modifying therapy, recombinant zoster vaccine for those 50 and older or younger patients on immune-modifying therapy, cervical screening every 3 years for those not on immunosuppression and more often (at first yearly) for those who are, and surveillance colonoscopy starting 8 years after symptom onset (at once, then yearly, with primary sclerosing cholangitis), every 2 to 5 years at low risk and yearly at high risk, on the interval GI sets.

Acute diarrhea, STEC and C. difficile

Most acute diarrhea in the United States is viral, self-limited and needs only fluids. Oral rehydration is preferred; intravenous fluid is for shock, sepsis or altered mental status. Feeding an ordinary age-appropriate diet early shortens illness; no special refeeding diet is supported. Blood, high fever, significant pain or symptoms past 3 days point to an invasive bacterial cause (Salmonella, Campylobacter, Shigella, Shiga toxin-producing E. coli, C. difficile).

Stool studies (multiplex PCR preferred over culture) are for bloody diarrhea, fever, severe pain, sepsis, duration of 7 days or more, immunocompromise, and food handlers, child-care and health care workers or nursing home residents; a separate culture is needed when an outbreak is suspected. Fecal leukocytes, lactoferrin, calprotectin and occult blood are not useful in acute infectious diarrhea. Endoscopy is for diarrhea lasting over a month with nondiagnostic tests, or earlier in the immunocompromised.

Symptom relief: loperamide (antimotility) and bismuth subsalicylate (antisecretory) are the most useful agents, and loperamide with simethicone works better than either alone. Avoid loperamide in bloody or suspected inflammatory diarrhea because of toxic megacolon and prolonged illness. Empiric antibiotics are for moderate to severe travelers' diarrhea, bloody stool with fever, sepsis or immunocompromise; a single dose usually suffices. Azithromycin 1,000 mg once is the preferred choice for febrile or bloody illness or where fluoroquinolone-resistant Campylobacter is common; a fluoroquinolone is the alternative.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (O157 and others) often causes bloody diarrhea with little fever. CDC warns that antibiotics may increase the risk of hemolytic uremic syndrome and that antimotility drugs can add toxic megacolon, HUS and neurologic complications. Monitor hemoglobin, platelets, electrolytes and kidney function; in children with STEC O157, intravenous fluids in the first 4 days of diarrhea may lower the risk of renal failure.

C. difficile: risk rises with antibiotics (especially fluoroquinolones, later-generation cephalosporins, clindamycin and carbapenems), age 65 and older, a hospital or nursing home stay in the last 3 months, prior infection and immunocompromise. Test only a patient with 3 or more unformed stools in 24 hours not explained by laxatives; AFP adds recent hospitalization of more than 3 days or antibiotics in the past 12 weeks as triggers. Colonization is common, so do not test formed stool or repeat the test after symptoms resolve. IDSA/SHEA 2021 prefers fidaxomicin 200 mg twice daily for 10 days for a first episode, with oral vancomycin 125 mg four times daily for 10 days as the alternative; fulminant disease gets vancomycin 500 mg four times daily by mouth or tube and hospital care. For recurrence: fidaxomicin, or tapered and pulsed vancomycin; after multiple recurrences, fecal microbiota transplant. IDSA/SHEA 2021 also suggests adding bezlotoxumab for a recurrence within 6 months (caution in heart failure); confirm the product is still supplied before planning on it. Stop the inciting antibiotic when possible. Alcohol hand rub does not kill spores, so gloves and gowns and contact precautions matter.

Constipation drugs and how to use them

The 2023 AGA-ACG guideline covers drug therapy for chronic idiopathic constipation in adults. Strong recommendations: polyethylene glycol, bisacodyl or sodium picosulfate (as short-term daily use for 4 weeks or less, or as rescue), and, for patients who do not respond to over-the-counter agents, linaclotide, plecanatide and prucalopride. Conditional recommendations: fiber supplements, senna, magnesium oxide, and, after over-the-counter failure, lubiprostone and lactulose. Read together, the recommendations support starting with fiber or PEG, using a stimulant for rescue, and moving to a prescription secretagogue or prucalopride when over-the-counter agents fail. Refractory constipation or symptoms of a pelvic floor disorder warrant anorectal physiology testing, which identifies patients who could be treated with biofeedback (ACG IBS 2021).

Colorectal cancer screening choices

USPSTF 2021 recommends screening for all adults 50 to 75 (A) and 45 to 49 (B), and selective screening at 76 to 85 based on health, prior screening and preference (C); it does not recommend screening after 85. It does not rank the tests; the choice is made with the patient, and the benefit depends on keeping to the interval and completing follow-up. Stool options are FIT or high-sensitivity guaiac every year and stool DNA-FIT every 1 to 3 years; visual options are CT colonography every 5 years, flexible sigmoidoscopy every 5 years (or every 10 years with yearly FIT), and colonoscopy every 10 years. A positive result on any test other than colonoscopy needs a follow-up colonoscopy. The recommendation covers asymptomatic adults at average risk: it excludes people with prior colorectal cancer or adenomatous polyps, IBD, or a known hereditary syndrome such as Lynch syndrome. A general family history of colorectal cancer does not remove anyone from screening at 45.

Hepatitis B and C: screening and reading results

CDC (2023) recommends hepatitis B screening for all adults 18 and older at least once with a triple panel: HBsAg, anti-HBs and total anti-HBc. HBsAg means current infection. Total anti-HBc means past or present infection; IgM anti-HBc marks acute infection, though it can also appear in a flare of chronic disease. Anti-HBs means immunity: alone after a documented series it is vaccine immunity (above 10 mIU/mL counts as immune), and with anti-HBc it is resolved infection. A resolved infection still carries a risk of reactivation under immunosuppression or chemotherapy. Isolated anti-HBc may be resolved infection with waned antibody, occult infection, maternal antibody in an infant, a false positive or a mutant strain, so it needs follow-up. Acute infection is the first 6 months after exposure; about 90% of infected infants and about 30% of children infected at 1 to 5 years become chronically infected, against 5% or fewer of adults. Chronic infection is referred to a clinician experienced in hepatitis B (ACG 2017). CDC recommends hepatitis B vaccine for nearly every adult, universally from 19 to 59.

CDC (2020) recommends hepatitis C screening for all adults 18 and older at least once, for every pregnant patient in each pregnancy, and periodically for ongoing risk such as injection drug use with shared equipment or hemodialysis. Start with an HCV antibody and reflex to HCV RNA. Antibody negative: no infection, unless exposure was recent. Antibody positive with RNA detected: current infection; counsel and link to curative treatment. Antibody positive with RNA not detected: no current infection (cleared or treated infection, or a false-positive antibody). After a possible exposure in the past 6 months, test RNA directly, because antibody takes 8 to 11 weeks to appear.

MASLD, FIB-4 and abnormal liver enzymes

In 2023 a multisociety Delphi process renamed NAFLD metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD): hepatic steatosis plus at least 1 of 5 cardiometabolic risk factors. NASH became MASH. Steatosis with metabolic risk and moderate alcohol intake (140–350 g a week for women, 210–420 g for men) is MetALD; steatosis with no metabolic factor and no other cause is cryptogenic. Steatosis affects about 2 in 3 people with type 2 diabetes and raises their risk of MASH, cirrhosis and liver cancer (ADA 2025 consensus).

AASLD 2023 makes FIB-4 (age, AST, ALT, platelets) the first-line risk test in primary care and endocrinology, with periodic rechecks for patients with 2 or more metabolic risk factors, prediabetes or type 2 diabetes. Below 1.3, advanced fibrosis is unlikely and the patient stays in primary care. At 1.3 or above, a second test follows, preferably vibration-controlled transient elastography or the ELF blood test, or the patient is referred. Refer directly when FIB-4 is above 2.67 or when aminotransferases stay above normal for more than 6 months. Over age 65, the low-risk cutoff is 2.0 instead of 1.3, and patients with 2 or more metabolic risk factors are rechecked every 1 to 2 years. FIB-4 is not used in acutely ill patients. Workup at diagnosis includes alcohol history, a hepatic panel, CBC with platelets, A1c, lipids, kidney function and urine albumin, and hepatitis C if not already screened.

Lifestyle is the base for everyone: 3–5% weight loss improves steatosis, while more than 10% is generally needed to improve steatohepatitis and fibrosis; exercise helps even without weight loss. Statins are safe across MASLD, including advanced liver disease (data are limited in decompensated cirrhosis), and are used for cardiovascular risk as in any patient. AASLD describes vitamin E 800 IU daily (in patients without diabetes) and pioglitazone as improving liver histology. The FDA has since granted accelerated approval to resmetirom (2024) and to semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly (2025) for noncirrhotic MASH with moderate to advanced (F2–F3) fibrosis, used with diet and exercise; these are usually started with hepatology involved.

Reading liver tests (ACG 2017): ALT is more liver-specific than AST, and a true normal ALT is roughly 29–33 IU/L in men and 19–25 IU/L in women. The R ratio, (ALT ÷ ALT upper limit) ÷ (alkaline phosphatase ÷ its upper limit), sorts injury as hepatocellular above 5, cholestatic below 2 and mixed between. A raised GGT confirms that a high alkaline phosphatase comes from the liver. AST at least twice ALT, with values rarely above 300 IU/L, suggests alcohol. Mild elevations call for a history, stopping hepatotoxic drugs and alcohol, and checks for fatty liver, viral hepatitis and hemochromatosis. With ALT above 1,000 IU/L, take a careful history of acetaminophen use, including combination products.

The acute abdomen in the office

Acute abdominal pain is pain of under 7 days. The common causes are gastroenteritis and nonspecific pain, then gallstones, kidney stones, diverticulitis and appendicitis; urinary and respiratory causes are frequent, especially in older adults. Identify instability, peritonitis (rigidity, rebound, pain on heel tap or cough) and pain out of proportion to the examination first, because those patients need resuscitation or surgery. Older, immunocompromised and obese patients often have muted findings. Analgesia does not hide the diagnosis and should be given. A pregnancy test belongs in the workup of anyone who could be pregnant, with ectopic pregnancy, pelvic inflammatory disease and adnexal torsion on the list (AFP 2023).

Imaging by location (AFP 2023): ultrasound for right upper quadrant pain and for pelvic pain in people with female reproductive organs; CT with intravenous contrast for right lower, left lower, left upper and generalized pain. In pregnancy, ultrasound first and MRI if it is inconclusive. Cholecystitis, appendicitis and mesenteric ischemia cannot be confirmed clinically and need imaging; typical diverticulitis and kidney stones can be diagnosed clinically.

Appendicitis (AFP 2018): lifetime risk is 8.6% in males and 6.7% in females, and it is the most common nonobstetric surgical emergency in pregnancy. In adults, right lower quadrant pain (positive likelihood ratio 7.3–8.5), rigidity (3.8) and periumbilical pain migrating to the right lower quadrant (3.2) best rule it in; in children, decreased bowel sounds and the psoas, obturator and Rovsing signs. No WBC or CRP value confirms or excludes it. Scores (Alvarado, Pediatric Appendicitis Score) with ultrasound reduce CT use. Perforation occurs in 17–32% and rises with delay, so moderate- and high-risk patients need prompt surgical consultation. Antibiotics alone are an option in selected adults, but about 40% need appendectomy within a year.

Cholecystitis: right upper quadrant tenderness and a Murphy sign (inspiration stops on palpation) support it, but no single finding excludes it, so right upper quadrant ultrasound is the first test. Diverticulitis (ACP 2022): CT when the diagnosis is uncertain; most uncomplicated left-sided cases can be managed at home, and select patients without antibiotics. Pancreatitis (ACG 2024): 2 of 3 criteria (typical epigastric pain often radiating to the back, lipase or amylase over 3 times the upper limit, characteristic imaging). Gallstones (40–70%) and alcohol (25–35%) cause most cases, so every patient gets an abdominal ultrasound; without either, check triglycerides, which count as the cause especially above 1,000 mg/dL, and over 40 consider a pancreatic tumor. Early CT is not needed for diagnosis. Inpatient care uses moderate fluid resuscitation with lactated Ringer's, early oral feeding within 24–48 hours in mild disease, and no prophylactic antibiotics; mild gallstone pancreatitis is followed by cholecystectomy during the same admission. Older adults with sudden severe pain and few abdominal findings need urgent CT angiography for mesenteric ischemia, and a ruptured aortic aneurysm belongs on the same list (AFP 2023).

Celiac disease

Celiac disease is a permanent immune reaction to gluten in wheat, barley and rye, with small bowel injury and specific antibodies, and it can present far from the gut. ACG 2023 recommends case finding, not population screening: test people with chronic diarrhea, weight loss, steatorrhea, pain or bloating, people with symptoms, signs or lab findings for which celiac disease is a treatable cause, patients with IBS and diarrhea, and first-degree relatives of a confirmed case, with or without symptoms.

Test while the patient is still eating gluten: TTG-IgA plus total IgA if IgA deficiency has not been excluded, because IgA deficiency gives a false-negative TTG-IgA; in that case use IgG-based tests (DGP-IgG or TTG-IgG). A positive TTG-IgA leads to endoscopy with 1–2 biopsies from the duodenal bulb and at least 4 from the distal duodenum. In children whose families agree, a TTG-IgA over 10 times the upper limit with a positive endomysial antibody on a second sample can stand in for biopsy. The standard testing pathway assumes a gluten-containing diet; patients already gluten-free follow a separate pathway in the guideline.

Treatment is a strict lifelong gluten-free diet with lifelong follow-up; most patients respond. Gluten-free oats are recommended, and pneumococcal vaccination is suggested. Persistent or recurrent symptoms on the diet (nonresponsive celiac disease) are usually from hidden gluten and need a systematic workup; true refractory disease is rare and serious.

Infant vomiting and intussusception

Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (Merck 2025) affects 2 to 5 per 1,000 infants, is about 4 to 5 times more common in boys (firstborn males especially), and appears most often between 3 and 6 weeks of age. Macrolide exposure in the first weeks of life (erythromycin especially) and maternal smoking raise the risk. Forceful, nonbilious vomiting follows feeds, and the infant feeds eagerly and looks well until dehydration sets in. Peristaltic waves may cross the upper abdomen, and a firm, mobile, olive-sized mass may be felt right of the midline. Losing gastric acid and volume produces a hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis. Ultrasound confirms a pyloric muscle 3 mm or thicker and elongated. Fluids and electrolytes are corrected first, then a pyloromyotomy, after which most infants feed within a day. AFP 2015 notes that progressively worsening projectile vomiting needs immediate imaging and surgical referral.

Intussusception (Merck 2025) is telescoping of bowel, usually ileocolic, between 6 months and 3 years of age, with 70% before age 2. Most cases are idiopathic, often after a viral illness; a lead point (polyp, lymphoma, Meckel diverticulum, IgA vasculitis) is found in about a quarter, more often in the youngest and oldest children. Sudden, severe colicky pain recurs every 15 to 20 minutes, often with vomiting, and the child looks fairly well between bouts; about 15% present only with lethargy. Heme-positive stool and currant-jelly stool come late as the bowel becomes ischemic, so do not wait for them; the full triad of pain, vomiting and bloody stool is present in fewer than half (AFP 2016). A sausage-shaped mass may be palpable. Ultrasound shows a target sign. A stable child has an air enema, which reduces it in over 80%, followed by overnight observation for perforation; peritonitis, perforation or a failed enema means surgery. Recurrence after enema reduction is about 13% or less. Current rotavirus vaccines carry a very small increase in risk (1 to 2 cases per 100,000) in the week after the first dose.

Bilious vomiting at any age, and especially in the first months of life, signals intestinal obstruction, such as malrotation with volvulus, and is an emergency (AFP 2015). In children younger than 12 months, incarcerated inguinal hernia and intussusception are the most common reasons for surgical referral; after age 1, appendicitis is. Ultrasound, including point-of-care ultrasound, is the preferred first imaging test for acute abdominal pain in children (AFP 2024).
