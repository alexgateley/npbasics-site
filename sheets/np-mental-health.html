Major depression: diagnose and start

  • Five or more of nine symptoms in the same 2 weeks, at least one being depressed mood or loss of interest: mood, interest, weight or appetite, sleep, psychomotor change, fatigue, worthlessness or guilt, poor concentration, thoughts of death or suicide.
  • Distress or impairment; not due to a substance or a medical condition; never a manic or hypomanic episode.
  • Rule out mimics: hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, syphilis, pain, chronic disease, substances. Ask about suicide at the same visit.

Try one

Mental Health in Primary Care practice question

A 16-year-old girl being treated for depression screens positive on the Ask Suicide-Screening Questions because she thought about taking her mother's pills 1 week ago. On a brief suicide safety assessment she has no thoughts of suicide today, no plan or intent, and no past attempts. She says she would tell her mother if the thoughts came back, and her mother is present and supportive. There is a handgun in the home. Which action should the nurse practitioner take NEXT?

  1. Arrange transfer to the emergency department for a psychiatric evaluation
  2. Have her sign a written agreement not to harm herself
  3. Start fluoxetine 10 mg daily with a follow-up visit in 4 weeks
  4. Make a safety plan with her and her mother, including locking up the gun and medications

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Emergency evaluation is for an acute positive screen (current suicidal thoughts) or imminent risk; this patient has neither, and a brief assessment supports outpatient management.
  • B. No-harm contracts have no evidence of preventing suicide and may give false reassurance; they are not a substitute for a safety plan.
  • C. Fluoxetine may be part of her depression treatment, but it does not address the immediate risk, and an antidepressant started in an adolescent calls for closer early follow-up because of the boxed warning on suicidality.
  • D. Correct. A collaborative safety plan with lethal means counseling, a mental health referral and follow-up within days is the recommended response to a non-acute positive screen.

With the ASQ, a yes to any question is a positive screen; a yes to the acuity question about current thoughts is an acute positive needing immediate evaluation. A non-acute positive is followed by a brief suicide safety assessment, and when risk is not imminent the patient can go home with a safety plan. The Stanley-Brown plan lists warning signs, coping strategies, people and places for distraction, people to ask for help, crisis contacts such as 988, and steps to make the environment safe. Removing or locking firearms and medications is the single most protective step because access to lethal means raises the risk of death.

Sources: NIMH: Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ) Toolkit; Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention; NIMH: Warning Signs of Suicide (2025)

Screen for bipolar disorder first

Before any antidepressant, ask about personal or family history of bipolar disorder, mania or hypomania (MDQ if unsure). An antidepressant alone can tip bipolar depression into mania or a mixed episode. New mania on an antidepressant or a stimulant: stop it and refer.

  • Get rating scales from parents and teachers; check vision and hearing; look for anxiety, depression, learning disorders, autism, tics and sleep disorders.

Suicide risk and safety planning

  • Ask directly, and use a validated tool (PHQ-9 item 9, C-SSRS screener, ASQ).
  • Warning signs: talk of wanting to die, preparation, seeking lethal means, rising substance use, hopelessness, feeling trapped, withdrawal, rage, recklessness, severe agitation or insomnia.
  • Acute risk (plan, intent, recent attempt, acute ASQ positive): do not leave the patient alone; arrange emergency evaluation.
Safety plan stepContent
1Personal warning signs of a crisis
2Coping strategies the patient can use alone
3People and places that distract
4People to ask for help
5Professionals and crisis lines, including 988
6Making the environment safe: reduce access to firearms and stockpiled medication; naloxone if at overdose risk

Insomnia

Chronic insomnia: trouble falling or staying asleep at least 3 nights a week for 3 months despite the chance to sleep. Screen for sleep apnea, depression, pain and substances first.

  • First line: CBT-I (sleep restriction, stimulus control, relaxation, cognitive work). Sleep hygiene alone is not a treatment.
  • If a drug is needed, short term: low-dose doxepin, an orexin antagonist (suvorexant, lemborexant, daridorexant) or a Z-drug (zolpidem, eszopiclone, zaleplon).
  • Not suggested: benzodiazepines, trazodone, diphenhydramine, antipsychotics, melatonin.
Safety Z-drugs carry a boxed warning for complex sleep behaviors (sleep-driving, sleepwalking); stop after any episode. Zolpidem IR: 5 mg for women and older adults, with 7–8 hours left for sleep.

In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

Using the scores well

The PHQ-2 asks only about low mood and loss of interest over the past 2 weeks. In its validation study a score of 3 or more found major depression with a sensitivity of 83% and specificity of 92%, and VA/DoD uses 3 as its trigger for the full PHQ-9. USPSTF also reports a cut point of 2 or more, which misses fewer cases at the cost of many more false positives. The PHQ-9 scores all nine DSM symptoms from 0 to 3 (total 0 to 27); 10 or more had 88% sensitivity and specificity in the original study.

VA/DoD asks clinicians to repeat a severity measure during treatment (measurement-based care). Response is a drop of at least 50% from baseline; remission is a PHQ score of 4 or less held for a month; recovery is remission held for 6 months. Treatment-resistant depression means no full response after at least two adequate trials.

For alcohol, USPSTF lists AUDIT-C cut points of 3 or more in women and 4 or more in men, while the VA and DoD call a screen positive at 5 or more. A single question also works: any day in the past year with 5 or more drinks (men) or 4 or more (women). CAGE asks about lifetime problems, so it detects dependence better than current risky drinking; a meta-analysis found it of limited value for screening at its usual cut point of 2. Risky drinking (NIAAA, as quoted by USPSTF) is more than 4 drinks a day or 14 a week for men 21–64, and more than 3 a day or 7 a week for women and for men 65 and older.

Choosing and changing an antidepressant

VA/DoD found no first-line antidepressant more effective than another, so choice rests on side effects, interactions, past response and cost. Bupropion has no sexual side effects but lowers the seizure threshold; mirtazapine is sedating and raises appetite and weight; SSRIs can cause sexual dysfunction (delayed ejaculation, low libido, erectile and arousal problems); venlafaxine raises blood pressure. Citalopram is capped at 40 mg (20 mg over 60, in liver disease or in poor CYP2C19 metabolizers) because higher doses prolong QT.

Before calling a drug a failure, push it to the maximum tolerated dose for at least 4–6 weeks within a 6–12 week trial, and recheck the diagnosis, adherence and coexisting problems such as substance use. Then switch to another antidepressant, switch to or add psychotherapy, or add a second-generation antipsychotic. After two adequate drug trials, rTMS, ECT, ketamine or esketamine are specialist options. Psychotherapies with equal evidence include CBT, behavioral activation, interpersonal therapy, problem-solving therapy, ACT, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy and short-term psychodynamic therapy.

Stopping an antidepressant abruptly can cause discontinuation symptoms such as dizziness, irritability, nausea, sensory disturbances and insomnia, so taper. Relapse within 6 months is about 41% when the drug is stopped after response, which is why VA/DoD recommends continuing the effective dose for at least 6 months after remission and offering CBT, IPT or MBCT to those at high risk of relapse.

The suicidality warning and the bipolar check

In pooled placebo-controlled trials, antidepressants increased suicidal thoughts and behavior in patients under 18 and from 18 to 24, but not at 25 and older (risk was lower in older adults). The warning does not mean withholding treatment; untreated depression is itself the major risk. It means close follow-up early and after dose changes, and teaching patients and families to report agitation, worsening mood or new suicidal thoughts. Atomoxetine carries a similar boxed warning for children with ADHD.

Every antidepressant label asks for a bipolar screen before starting, because antidepressant monotherapy can provoke mania or a mixed episode. Clues: prior episodes of decreased need for sleep with high energy, racing thoughts, risky spending or sex, a family history of bipolar disorder, early-onset or recurrent depression, or a past switch on an antidepressant. A positive MDQ needs a diagnostic interview, usually with psychiatry.

ADHD medications and controlled-substance rules

Methylphenidate and amphetamine products are Schedule II. In 2023 FDA made their boxed warning uniform: high potential for abuse and misuse that can lead to addiction, overdose and death. Before prescribing, assess each patient's risk; during treatment, watch for early refill requests and diversion; teach locked storage, safe disposal and never sharing. Labels also say to avoid stimulants in serious structural heart disease, cardiomyopathy or serious arrhythmia, to monitor blood pressure and pulse, to watch growth in children, and to look for tics, Raynaud's phenomenon and priapism.

Federal law bars refills of Schedule II drugs. A prescriber may instead write several prescriptions dated the day they are written, each with the earliest date it may be filled, for a total of up to a 90-day supply. Whether an NP may prescribe Schedule II drugs, for how many days, and whether the state prescription monitoring program must be checked depends on state law, so follow your state's rules.

For preschool children (4–6), parent training in behavior management comes first because it works about as well as medication with fewer side effects; methylphenidate is added only if behavior treatment is not enough. From age 6, medication plus behavioral treatment is the usual plan.

Buprenorphine in the office

Buprenorphine is a partial opioid agonist that binds tightly, so given to someone with full agonists on board it displaces them and causes precipitated withdrawal. The label says to give the first dose when objective signs of moderate withdrawal appear, and not less than 6 hours after the last opioid use, in divided doses: up to 8 mg/2 mg on day 1 and up to 16 mg/4 mg on day 2, then adjust in 2–4 mg steps to a dose that controls withdrawal and keeps the patient in treatment, generally 4–24 mg a day. Patients on methadone or other long-acting opioids start on buprenorphine alone for the first 2 days.

Section 1262 of the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 removed the DATA (X) waiver and patient limits. Section 1263 added a training requirement for new or renewing DEA registrants who prescribe any Schedule II–V drug, from June 27, 2023: 8 hours of opioid or other substance use disorder training, board certification in addiction medicine or addiction psychiatry, or graduation within 5 years from a program with 8 hours of that content. State laws still apply.

Naltrexone is the opposite: a full antagonist. Patients must be off short-acting opioids for 7–10 days, and those coming off buprenorphine or methadone can have precipitated withdrawal for up to 2 weeks. VA/DoD recommends buprenorphine-naloxone or methadone for OUD and suggests extended-release naltrexone; anyone with OUD or at high overdose risk should leave with naloxone.

Lithium, antipsychotics and serotonin syndrome in more depth

Lithium's boxed warning is about toxicity, which tracks serum levels and can occur close to the therapeutic range. Levels of 1.5 mEq/L or more are toxic, and above 3 mEq/L patients may progress to seizures, coma and permanent brain damage. Neurologic signs run from fine tremor, poor coordination and weakness, through drowsiness, hyperreflexia, twitching, ataxia, tinnitus and slurred speech, to clonus, confusion, seizures and coma; GI signs include nausea, vomiting and diarrhea. Recheck the level after any dose change, a new diuretic, NSAID, ACE inhibitor, ARB or metronidazole, a large change in exercise, or an illness with vomiting, diarrhea or fever. Lithium can cause hypothyroidism, hyperparathyroidism with high calcium, and a urine-concentrating defect (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus) that may be only partly reversible after long use. In pregnancy, early reports linked it with Ebstein anomaly; later studies suggest the cardiac risk is small, and the label suggests fetal echocardiography at 16–20 weeks after first-trimester exposure.

Second-generation antipsychotics cause weight gain, high glucose and high lipids, most with clozapine and olanzapine. The monitoring table on the page comes from the 2004 ADA/APA consensus as reprinted in AFP; it is a minimum. All antipsychotics can prolong QT and cause extrapyramidal symptoms; tardive dyskinesia may not reverse after the drug is stopped. Risperidone and first-generation drugs raise prolactin most.

The Hunter criteria diagnose serotonin toxicity in a patient on a serotonergic drug when any one of these is present: spontaneous clonus; inducible or ocular clonus with agitation or sweating; tremor with hyperreflexia; or hypertonia with temperature above 38 °C and ocular or inducible clonus. Mild cases settle in 24–72 hours after the drugs are stopped. Cyproheptadine (12 mg, then 2 mg every 2 hours while symptoms persist) is the usual antidote, though evidence is limited. Anticholinergic toxicity can look similar but brings flushed skin, dry mouth, dilated pupils, delirium and quiet bowel sounds, usually with temperatures of 39 °C or lower.

Insomnia and eating disorders in more depth

CBT-I works by sleep restriction (limiting time in bed to actual sleep time, then extending it), stimulus control (bed only for sleep), arousal reduction and challenging unhelpful beliefs about sleep. VA/DoD delays it for unstable medical illness, active alcohol or drug use disorder, uncontrolled seizures, current exposure-based PTSD therapy and acute mental health symptoms, and adapts it (or delays it) for excessive daytime sleepiness, nighttime fall risk and bipolar disorder. When a hypnotic is used, plan the stop date at the start. For adults 65 and older, the AGS Beers criteria flag sedative-hypnotics and strongly anticholinergic drugs as usually best avoided.

The SCOFF questionnaire (5 yes/no questions on vomiting, loss of control, weight loss, feeling fat, food dominating life) suggests anorexia or bulimia at 2 or more yes answers. Medical evaluation includes orthostatic vital signs, electrolytes with magnesium and phosphate, and an ECG when indicated. Medications are adjuncts, not stand-alone treatment. Weight gain of about 1–2 kg a week is associated with cardiovascular stabilization. Fluoxetine at 60 mg a day is FDA approved for bulimia in adults; lisdexamfetamine is approved for moderate to severe binge eating disorder in adults.
