In depth

The summary above covers the essentials. These sections go further.

ACE inhibitors and ARBs

Mechanism: ACE inhibitors block conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II and the breakdown of bradykinin; ARBs block the angiotensin II receptor. Both lower blood pressure, protect the kidney in albuminuric disease and are core heart failure drugs; choosing them by condition belongs on the cardiovascular and kidney pages.

  • Fetal toxicity is a boxed warning on both classes: drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system can injure and kill the fetus, so stop them as soon as pregnancy is detected. The Zestril label also advises against breastfeeding.
  • Angioedema: a history of angioedema with an ACE inhibitor, or hereditary or idiopathic angioedema, contraindicates lisinopril. The label notes angioedema is more frequent in Black than non-Black patients, and that monotherapy lowers blood pressure less in Black patients.
  • Sacubitril/valsartan: an ACE inhibitor must not be given within 36 hours of switching to or from it. Aliskiren is contraindicated with lisinopril in diabetes.
  • Hyperkalemia: monitor potassium periodically. Risk rises with kidney disease, diabetes, potassium-sparing diuretics, potassium supplements and potassium-containing salt substitutes.
  • Cough is one of the common adverse reactions in hypertension trials; it is the usual reason to change from an ACE inhibitor to an ARB.
  • NSAIDs: in older, volume-depleted or kidney-impaired patients, an NSAID with an ACE inhibitor can worsen kidney function, and NSAIDs blunt the antihypertensive effect. Lithium: toxicity has been reported, so monitor lithium levels.

Thiazides, calcium channel blockers and beta-blockers

Thiazides block sodium chloride reabsorption in the distal tubule. The hydrochlorothiazide label lists the electrolyte pattern a board question expects: hyponatremia, hypochloremic alkalosis and hypokalemia (which also sensitizes the heart to digoxin), with hyperuricemia that can precipitate gout and a small rise in serum calcium because urinary calcium excretion falls. Marked hypercalcemia on a thiazide suggests hidden hyperparathyroidism. Diabetes medicines may need adjustment. Contraindications are anuria and sulfonamide hypersensitivity; lithium generally should not be combined with diuretics. Counsel sun protection and skin cancer screening (nonmelanoma skin cancer) and note photosensitivity and possible lupus flares.

Dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (amlodipine) relax arterial smooth muscle. Edema is the most common adverse reaction and is dose-related: in the Norvasc trials it occurred in 10.8% at 10 mg against 0.6% on placebo. Amlodipine raises simvastatin exposure, so simvastatin is capped at 20 mg. Non-dihydropyridines (verapamil, diltiazem) also slow the SA and AV nodes. Verapamil is contraindicated in severe left ventricular dysfunction and in second- or third-degree AV block without a pacemaker, should be avoided with any ventricular dysfunction in a patient on a beta-blocker, and causes constipation (7.3% in trials). Both are moderate CYP3A inhibitors (FDA table), so simvastatin is capped at 10 mg with either.

Beta-blockers (metoprolol succinate shown) are contraindicated in severe bradycardia, second- or third-degree heart block, cardiogenic shock, decompensated heart failure and sick sinus syndrome without a pacemaker. Stopping abruptly can worsen angina or cause MI, and in thyrotoxicosis can precipitate thyroid storm, so taper. They can raise the risk of hypoglycemia and mask its warning signs. In bronchospastic disease the label says beta-blockers should generally not be used, but a beta1-selective agent may be used at the lowest dose when other drugs fail or are not tolerated, since selectivity is not absolute.

Statins

Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase and lower LDL cholesterol. The adverse effect that matters is muscle: myopathy (pain, tenderness or weakness with a raised CK) and, rarely, rhabdomyolysis with kidney injury. The Lipitor label names the risk factors: age 65 or older, uncontrolled hypothyroidism, renal impairment, interacting drugs and higher doses. The label says to consider liver enzyme testing before starting and then as clinically indicated, not on a fixed schedule. Statins are stopped when pregnancy is recognized, and breastfeeding is not recommended during treatment.

Interactions follow CYP3A4. Simvastatin and lovastatin are sensitive CYP3A substrates (FDA table), so simvastatin is contraindicated with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (select azole antifungals and macrolides, some antivirals, nefazodone), with gemfibrozil, cyclosporine or danazol, and in acute liver failure or decompensated cirrhosis; limited to 10 mg with verapamil, diltiazem or dronedarone, and to 20 mg with amiodarone, amlodipine or ranolazine. The 80 mg dose is restricted to people who have taken it for 12 months or more without muscle toxicity (the Zocor 80 mg tablet is no longer marketed). Atorvastatin is also affected: no more than 20 mg with clarithromycin or itraconazole.

Anticoagulants

Warfarin blocks vitamin K-dependent clotting factor synthesis. The Jantoven label targets an INR of 2.5 (range 2.0–3.0) for VTE and nonvalvular AF, checks the INR daily at initiation until stable and then every 1–4 weeks, and notes that genetics (CYP2C9 and VKORC1), diet and drugs all move the dose. CYP2C9 inhibitors that raise the INR include amiodarone, TMP-SMX, fluconazole and metronidazole; inducers such as rifampin, carbamazepine and phenobarbital lower it. Warfarin is contraindicated in pregnancy except in women with mechanical heart valves, and can cause skin necrosis (under 0.1%).

Apixaban is a direct factor Xa inhibitor. For nonvalvular AF the dose is 5 mg twice daily, reduced to 2.5 mg twice daily when at least two of these apply: age 80 or older, weight 60 kg or less, serum creatinine 1.5 mg/dL or more. It is not recommended with prosthetic heart valves or triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome. Boxed warnings: stopping early raises the risk of thrombosis (bridge with another anticoagulant if it is stopped for a reason other than bleeding), and spinal or epidural hematoma with neuraxial procedures. A specific reversal agent exists.

Diabetes drugs and insulin

ADA 2026 no longer makes metformin automatic first-line therapy for everyone. In adults with type 2 diabetes who have, or are at high risk for, ASCVD, the plan should include a GLP-1 RA or SGLT2 inhibitor with proven cardiovascular benefit regardless of A1C; with heart failure an SGLT2 inhibitor is recommended; with CKD (eGFR 20–60 or albuminuria) an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 RA with proven kidney benefit; and with eGFR under 30 a GLP-1 RA is preferred. Metformin remains effective, safe and cheap, and is the usual choice when glucose lowering is the only goal or cost is a barrier.

  • Metformin lowers hepatic glucose output. Diarrhea (53% against 12% on placebo in one trial) and nausea are the commonest effects; extended-release is taken once daily. Lactic acidosis is the boxed warning. Do not use at eGFR under 30, do not start at 30–45 (ADA: not under 45), and reassess or reduce the dose if eGFR falls below 45. Stop at or before iodinated contrast when eGFR is 30–60, with liver disease, alcoholism or heart failure, or for intra-arterial contrast, and restart after eGFR is rechecked at 48 hours and stable. Check B12 every 2–3 years.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors make the kidney excrete glucose. Their glucose-lowering effect is small below eGFR 45, but they are continued or started for heart and kidney benefit above eGFR 20 (ADA Table 9.2). Empagliflozin is not for type 1 diabetes or for glycemic control at eGFR under 30. Correct volume depletion first; warn about genital yeast infections, Fournier's gangrene and ketoacidosis; withhold at least 3 days before major surgery or prolonged fasting.
  • GLP-1 RAs raise glucose-dependent insulin release, slow gastric emptying and reduce appetite. Semaglutide starts at 0.25 mg weekly. Boxed: thyroid C-cell tumors in rodents, so not with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or with MEN 2. Also pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, worsening diabetic retinopathy in people who already have it, and aspiration risk under anesthesia or deep sedation. Never share a pen.
  • Insulin: ADA 2026 considers insulin when there are symptoms of hyperglycemia, A1C is over 10% or glucose is 300 mg/dL or more, and otherwise prefers GLP-1-based therapy to insulin. Basal insulin alone is the simplest start, at 0.1–0.2 units/kg/day (ADA) or 0.2 units/kg or up to 10 units once daily (Lantus label), titrated to fasting glucose. Keep other glucose-lowering drugs, pair insulin with a GLP-1 RA when possible, watch for overbasalization, and prescribe glucagon for everyone on insulin.
  • Hypoglycemia drugs: when any new glucose-lowering drug is started, reassess sulfonylureas, meglitinides and insulin (ADA 9.17). A DPP-4 inhibitor adds nothing to a GLP-1 RA (ADA 9.18).

Levothyroxine

Take levothyroxine once daily on an empty stomach, 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast, and at least 4 hours apart from drugs that bind it, such as calcium carbonate, ferrous sulfate, sevelamer and lanthanum (the label groups these as phosphate binders) and bile acid sequestrants; soy formula also reduces absorption. Full replacement in a healthy adult is about 1.6 mcg/kg/day; start lower in older adults and in those with heart disease or atrial fibrillation risk. Titrate by 12.5–25 mcg every 4–6 weeks (every 6–8 weeks in cardiac patients) and check TSH 6–8 weeks after any change; doses over 200 mcg/day are seldom needed. Biotin falsifies thyroid tests, so stop it at least 2 days before the blood draw. In pregnancy with pre-existing hypothyroidism, check TSH and free T4 as soon as pregnancy is confirmed and at least once each trimester, keep TSH in the trimester-specific range, and raise the dose by 12.5–25 mcg/day as needed. The boxed warning: thyroid hormone is not for obesity or weight loss.

Antidepressants, bupropion, benzodiazepines and stimulants

  • SSRIs block serotonin reuptake. Boxed warning: antidepressants increase suicidal thoughts and behaviors in children and young adults (the medication guide says 24 and younger), most in the first months and after dose changes, so monitor closely. Contraindicated with an MAOI or within 14 days of one (including linezolid and IV methylene blue). Serotonin syndrome risk rises with other serotonergic drugs. Hyponatremia from SIADH, bleeding (especially with aspirin, NSAIDs or anticoagulants), angle-closure glaucoma in narrow angles, sexual dysfunction and discontinuation symptoms are the label warnings to know.
  • SNRIs share these warnings. Venlafaxine raises blood pressure: control hypertension before starting and monitor. Duloxetine can injure the liver: avoid it with substantial alcohol use or chronic liver disease; it also causes orthostatic hypotension and falls.
  • Bupropion inhibits dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake. Its risk is seizures, so it is contraindicated with a seizure disorder, current or past bulimia or anorexia, abrupt withdrawal from alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates or antiepileptics, and an MAOI now or within 14 days (hypertensive reactions); limit the dose to 450 mg/day and titrate slowly. It can raise blood pressure, and neuropsychiatric events have been reported during smoking cessation. It is a strong CYP2D6 inhibitor (FDA table).
  • Benzodiazepines enhance GABA-A signalling. Since 2020 the boxed warning covers abuse, misuse and addiction, physical dependence and withdrawal, and the danger of combining them with opioids (profound sedation, respiratory depression, death). Reduce or stop with a gradual, individualized taper; abrupt stopping can cause seizures. Alprazolam is contraindicated with strong CYP3A inhibitors such as ketoconazole and itraconazole. The AGS Beers Criteria list drugs that older adults should usually avoid, and benzodiazepines are a familiar example on boards.
  • Stimulants (amphetamine salts, methylphenidate) are Schedule II with a boxed warning for abuse, misuse and addiction. Before starting, take a cardiac history and family history of sudden death or ventricular arrhythmia and examine the patient; avoid them with structural heart disease, cardiomyopathy, serious arrhythmia or coronary disease. Monitor blood pressure and pulse, growth in children, Raynaud's phenomenon and other vasculopathy, tics, and mania risk. No MAOI within 14 days (hypertensive crisis).

Antibiotics in more depth

  • Penicillins and other beta-lactams kill bacteria by blocking cell wall synthesis. Amoxicillin: stop if a rash progresses, and evaluate diarrhea for C. difficile. Its label found no fetal harm in animal studies; it is the usual pregnancy choice when the organism allows.
  • Cephalosporins are beta-lactams too. The cephalexin label warns that cross-hypersensitivity can occur in up to 10% of patients with a penicillin allergy, so ask about the reaction before prescribing, and that C. difficile diarrhea can follow. Decades of pregnancy data have not shown a drug-related risk of birth defects.
  • Clindamycin carries a boxed warning for C. difficile colitis, which can be fatal; its label reserves it for serious infections where less toxic drugs are inappropriate, and it is a common choice when a penicillin cannot be used.
  • Metronidazole: avoid alcohol and propylene glycol products during treatment and for at least 3 days after (disulfiram-like reaction); do not give within 2 weeks of disulfiram (psychotic reactions); it potentiates warfarin, so check the INR. The Flagyl 375 label contraindicates it for trichomoniasis in the first trimester and carries a boxed warning that it is carcinogenic in rodents, so avoid unnecessary use.
  • Macrolides block the 50S ribosome. Azithromycin can prolong the QT interval (torsades has been reported), can cause serious hepatotoxicity and passes into breast milk; its label finds no drug-related birth-defect risk after decades of use. Clarithromycin is a strong and erythromycin a moderate CYP3A inhibitor, which is why they collide with simvastatin, alprazolam and others.
  • Fluoroquinolones block DNA gyrase. Boxed: tendinitis and tendon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, CNS effects and worsening of myasthenia gravis, which can be disabling and occur together; stop at the first sign and do not use the class again. Aortic aneurysm and dissection are more frequent within 2 months of use, especially in older adults. Ciprofloxacin is contraindicated with tizanidine, raises theophylline levels, and must be taken 2 hours before or 6 hours after magnesium or aluminum antacids. Because of these risks the label reserves it, for uncomplicated cystitis, acute sinusitis and acute flares of chronic bronchitis, for patients with no other option. Tendon risk is highest over age 60, with corticosteroids and after a kidney, heart or lung transplant. Fluoroquinolones also cause hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, mostly in people on insulin or oral diabetes drugs. In children ciprofloxacin is labeled only for a few indications (complicated UTI and pyelonephritis, anthrax, plague) because joint and tendon adverse events were more frequent. The 2026 label reports no drug-related birth-defect risk, but boards still expect a beta-lactam or other option first in pregnancy.
  • Tetracyclines block the 30S ribosome. Given during tooth development (last half of pregnancy, infancy and childhood to age 8) they can permanently stain teeth, so doxycycline is used at 8 or younger only for severe illness such as Rocky Mountain spotted fever or anthrax.
  • TMP-SMX blocks two steps of folate synthesis. It is contraindicated in sulfonamide or trimethoprim allergy, after drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia from either component, in infants under 2 months, in folate-deficiency megaloblastic anemia, with marked liver damage, in severe kidney impairment when kidney function cannot be monitored, and with dofetilide. It causes hyperkalemia even at usual doses in people with kidney disease or other potassium-raising drugs, can trigger SJS, TEN and DRESS, and raises the INR on warfarin. Some studies link pregnancy exposure with neural tube, heart and urinary defects and clefts, and the label urges caution while nursing a jaundiced, ill or premature infant (bilirubin displacement, kernicterus).
  • Nitrofurantoin is concentrated in urine. Its label contraindicates it with creatinine clearance under 60 mL/min, at term (38–42 weeks), in labor or when labor is imminent, and in infants under 1 month (all for hemolytic anemia), and after cholestatic jaundice or liver dysfunction from a previous course. Acute and chronic pulmonary reactions, sometimes fatal, are its main warning.

NSAIDs and opioids

NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase. NSAID labels (Celebrex shown) carry a boxed warning for cardiovascular thrombotic events and for GI bleeding, ulceration and perforation, and contraindicates use around CABG surgery; use the lowest dose for the shortest time. Older adults and people with a prior ulcer or GI bleed carry the most GI risk. Kidney injury is most likely with kidney disease, dehydration, heart failure, liver disease, diuretics, ACE inhibitors or ARBs, and NSAIDs blunt those drugs. In pregnancy, limit NSAIDs between about 20 and 30 weeks (oligohydramnios and fetal kidney dysfunction) and avoid them from about 30 weeks (premature closure of the ductus arteriosus). Celecoxib is contraindicated in sulfonamide allergy and aspirin-sensitive asthma.

CDC 2022 opioid guideline, 12 recommendations in brief. Acute pain is under 1 month, subacute 1–3 months, chronic over 3 months. Nonopioid and nonpharmacologic therapy come first for all three; opioids are not first-line for subacute or chronic pain. Start with immediate-release, not extended-release or long-acting, opioids, at the lowest effective dose (often 20–30 MME/day in an opioid-naive adult). Pause and reassess before going to 50 MME/day or higher: benefit rarely grows and risk does, and increases beyond 50 MME/day are progressively more likely to give diminishing returns. These are guideposts, not hard caps. For acute pain, a few days or less is often enough; reassess at least every 2 weeks if it continues. Reevaluate within 1–4 weeks of starting or raising a dose for subacute or chronic pain. Offer naloxone to patients with a history of overdose or substance use disorder, sleep-disordered breathing, 50 MME/day or more, or a benzodiazepine. Check the PDMP before the first prescription and at least every 3 months during long-term therapy. Consider toxicology testing before starting and at least yearly, without punishing the patient for results. Use particular caution with benzodiazepines and other CNS depressants. Offer or arrange medication treatment (buprenorphine or methadone) for opioid use disorder.

Tapering: do not stop abruptly or cut high doses quickly unless there is a life-threatening problem. After a year or more of use, about 10% per month or slower is usually tolerated; after shorter use, about 10% per week until about 30% of the starting dose remains. Follow up at least monthly during a taper. Buprenorphine: the CDC text still mentions the DATA waiver, but Congress removed it in December 2022, so any practitioner whose DEA registration includes Schedule III authority may prescribe buprenorphine for OUD where state law allows, with no patient cap. Since June 27, 2023 new or renewing DEA registrants who prescribe Schedule II–V drugs need 8 hours of substance use disorder training (or a qualifying board certification or recent qualifying school curriculum).

  • Opioid labels (Roxicodone shown): boxed warnings for addiction, abuse and misuse; life-threatening respiratory depression (greatest at the start and after a dose increase); accidental ingestion, which can kill a child with one dose; combination with benzodiazepines or other CNS depressants (reserve for patients with no adequate alternative); neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome with prolonged use in pregnancy; and CYP3A4 inhibitors or inducer withdrawal raising oxycodone levels.
  • Tramadol: contraindicated in children under 12 and under 18 after tonsillectomy or adenoidectomy (ultra-rapid CYP2D6 metabolizers have died). Seizure risk rises with SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics and other drugs that lower the seizure threshold, and serotonin syndrome can occur. Breastfeeding is not recommended during treatment.

PPIs, inhalers and bisphosphonates

PPIs irreversibly block the gastric proton pump. Label warnings for prolonged use: vitamin B12 deficiency (daily use beyond about 3 years), hypomagnesemia, osteoporosis-related fractures of the hip, wrist and spine, C. difficile diarrhea, acute interstitial nephritis at any point, cutaneous or systemic lupus, and fundic gland polyps beyond a year; omeprazole should not be combined with clopidogrel. The AGA 2022 de-prescribing update asks primary care to document every patient's indication and review it regularly, to try stopping when there is no definite indication, and to step twice-daily users down to once daily. Do not stop a PPI in complicated GERD (severe erosive esophagitis, ulcer, stricture), Barrett's esophagus, eosinophilic esophagitis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or high upper-GI bleeding risk. Warn about rebound symptoms; tapering and stopping outright are both acceptable. The reason to stop is the missing indication, not fear of the adverse effects.

Asthma (GINA 2026): asthma should not be treated with a SABA alone, because SABA-only treatment raises the risk of exacerbations and death; dispensing 3 or more canisters a year raises exacerbation risk and 12 or more is linked with asthma death. The preferred track for adults and adolescents uses as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol as the reliever at every step, alone at steps 1–2 and as maintenance-and-reliever therapy (MART) from step 3. The alternative track uses a SABA (or ICS-SABA) reliever with maintenance ICS. A LABA should never be used without an ICS in asthma. A patient who needs more than 12 inhalations of ICS-formoterol in 24 hours needs medical review. ICS adverse effects: thrush and hoarseness (rinse and spit; use a spacer with an MDI), and with long-term high doses osteoporosis, cataract and glaucoma.

COPD (GOLD 2026): rescue short-acting bronchodilators for everyone. For group B and group E, start a LABA + LAMA combination; group A gets a bronchodilator. LABA + ICS is not encouraged in COPD; when an ICS is indicated, triple therapy (LABA + LAMA + ICS) is preferred. Consider triple therapy from the start in group E with eosinophils 300 cells/µL or more, and step up to it when exacerbations continue on LABA + LAMA with eosinophils 100 or more. ICS in COPD raises the rates of thrush, hoarse voice, skin bruising and pneumonia; pneumonia risk is highest in current smokers, age 55 or older, prior exacerbations or pneumonia, BMI under 25 and severe obstruction. LAMAs mainly cause dry mouth, occasionally urinary symptoms, and nebulized solution through a face mask has precipitated acute glaucoma. COPD with asthma is treated as asthma, so an ICS is required.

Bisphosphonates inhibit osteoclast bone resorption and are first-line for high fracture risk (AFP 2023). Oral alendronate (70 mg weekly or 10 mg daily) is swallowed whole with 6–8 oz of plain water at least 30 minutes before the first food, drink or medicine of the day, and the patient stays upright for at least 30 minutes and until after eating. Contraindications: esophageal disorders that delay emptying (stricture, achalasia), inability to sit or stand upright for 30 minutes, and uncorrected hypocalcemia; avoid with creatinine clearance below 30–35 mL/min. Rare harms rise with duration: osteonecrosis of the jaw (usually after extraction or local infection) and atypical femur fractures. The alendronate label suggests considering stopping after 3 to 5 years in patients at low fracture risk; AFP 2023 advises reassessing for a drug holiday after 5 years of oral or 3 years of IV zoledronic acid therapy; high-risk patients may continue up to 10 years oral or 6 years IV. Give calcium (about 1,200 mg/day for women over 50) and vitamin D (800–1,000 IU/day).

CYP450 interactions that matter

FDA defines a strong inhibitor as one that raises the exposure (AUC) of a sensitive substrate 5-fold or more, a moderate inhibitor 2- to 5-fold, and a strong inducer as one that lowers it by 80% or more. Inhibitors raise substrate levels and toxicity; inducers lower levels and cause treatment failure, and stopping an inducer raises levels again.

Pregnancy and lactation principles

Medicine use in pregnancy is the rule, not the exception: CDC reports about 9 in 10 women take some medicine during pregnancy and about 7 in 10 take a prescription drug, while fewer than 10% of drugs approved since 1980 have enough data to judge pregnancy safety. Stopping a needed medicine can be more harmful than continuing it, so the plan starts before conception with a review of every drug, supplement and herb.

The FDA Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (effective June 30, 2015) removed the A, B, C, D and X letter categories from prescription labels. Section 8.1 (Pregnancy) gives registry information, a risk summary, clinical considerations and the data; 8.2 (Lactation) replaces 'nursing mothers' and covers drug in milk and effects on the infant and on milk; 8.3 covers pregnancy testing, contraception and fertility for females and males of reproductive potential. Older labels, such as amoxicillin's, still show a letter.

  • Stop or avoid in pregnancy (from the labels on this page): ACE inhibitors and ARBs (boxed fetal toxicity); warfarin (except mechanical valves); statins (stop when pregnancy is recognized); NSAIDs from about 20 weeks (limit) and 30 weeks (avoid); tetracyclines in the last half of pregnancy; nitrofurantoin at 38–42 weeks and in labor; TMP-SMX when an alternative exists; opioids for prolonged use (neonatal withdrawal).
  • Dose changes in pregnancy: levothyroxine requirements rise, so check TSH as soon as pregnancy is confirmed and each trimester.
  • Lactation: CDC notes that most prescription medicines are safe for breastfeeding mothers and their infants. Exceptions on this page: tramadol (not recommended, ultra-rapid metabolism), statins and lisinopril (labels advise against breastfeeding), and TMP-SMX with caution in jaundiced, ill or premature infants.

Controlled substances

The schedules come from 21 USC 812. Schedule I: high abuse potential, no accepted medical use. Schedule II: high abuse potential with accepted medical use; abuse may lead to severe dependence. Schedules III, IV and V: progressively lower abuse potential and dependence, all with accepted medical use.
