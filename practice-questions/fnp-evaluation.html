Question 1

A 52-year-old woman who does not smoke was diagnosed with acute bronchitis 10 days ago and returns because her cough, now 3 weeks old, has not fully gone away. It is slowly improving and brings up green sputum in the morning. She has no fever, coughing fits, post-cough vomiting, night sweats or weight loss. Data are: temperature 37.0 °C (98.6 °F), heart rate 78/min, RR 16/min, SpO2 98%, clear lungs. She asks for an antibiotic. Which response is MOST appropriate?

  1. Order a chest radiograph to rule out pneumonia
  2. Prescribe a 5-day course of azithromycin for the colored sputum
  3. Explain that the cough is expected to fade on its own
  4. Prescribe an albuterol inhaler to use as needed for cough

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Imaging is for abnormal vital signs or focal lung findings, or a cough that persists beyond the expected course with red flags; her vital signs and exam are normal.
  • B. Sputum color reflects inflammatory cells, not bacteria; antibiotics do not shorten acute bronchitis and add adverse effects and resistance.
  • C. Correct. A bronchitic cough commonly lasts about 3 weeks; with normal vital signs, a clear exam and steady improvement, reassurance and symptom care are appropriate, with return if new fever or dyspnea develops or the cough lasts past 8 weeks.
  • D. Beta-agonists help only patients who are wheezing or have underlying airway disease; she has neither.

Acute bronchitis is almost always viral, and its cough lasts a mean of about 18 days, so a cough at 3 weeks that is improving is still within the expected course. Pneumonia is unlikely when heart rate is under 100/min, respiratory rate under 24/min, temperature under 38 °C and the chest exam is normal. Pertussis testing is considered for a cough of 2 weeks or more with paroxysms, whoop or post-tussive vomiting, which she does not have. A cough beyond 8 weeks is chronic and needs its own evaluation.

Sources: AAFP: Acute Bronchitis (Am Fam Physician 2016); CDC, Clinical Overview of Pertussis

Question 2

A 16-year-old boy with moderate major depressive disorder started escitalopram 10 mg daily 5 weeks ago along with weekly cognitive behavioral therapy, and at his 2-week check his mood had begun to lift. Today his mother says that for the past 5 days he has slept 3 to 4 hours a night without seeming tired, talks rapidly, has started three new business plans and has spent $400 of his savings online. He denies suicidal thoughts on direct questioning and denies drug use, and a urine drug screen is negative. His father takes lithium for a mood disorder. Which is the MOST appropriate action?

  1. Switch escitalopram to fluoxetine 10 mg daily
  2. Add trazodone 50 mg at bedtime for insomnia
  3. Stop escitalopram and arrange urgent psychiatric evaluation
  4. Continue escitalopram and review a mood chart at the next weekly therapy visit

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Fluoxetine is the other SSRI approved for adolescent depression, but any SSRI can drive hypomania; switching within the class does not address a possible bipolar disorder.
  • B. He sleeps little without feeling tired, which is decreased need for sleep, a mood symptom rather than insomnia; a sedative treats the wrong problem and leaves the SSRI in place.
  • C. Correct. Decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, increased goal-directed activity and spending for 5 days during SSRI treatment, with a family history of bipolar disorder, point to antidepressant-emergent hypomania; the drug should be stopped and a psychiatrist should assess him promptly.
  • D. His improvement in mood may look like the response everyone hoped for, but these features go beyond recovery; continuing the SSRI risks progression to mania or a mixed state with higher suicide risk.

Antidepressant labels warn that they can activate mania or hypomania and advise screening for personal or family history of bipolar disorder before starting. Under DSM-5-TR, a full hypomanic episode that emerges during antidepressant treatment and persists beyond the drug's physiological effect counts toward a bipolar diagnosis. GLAD-PC advises primary care clinicians to monitor youth on antidepressants for side effects, including activation, agitation and suicidality, and to consult or refer to mental health specialists when bipolar disorder is suspected. His father's lithium treatment raises the likelihood. Stopping the SSRI, keeping a safety plan in place and arranging prompt psychiatric evaluation are the evaluation steps.

Sources: LEXAPRO (escitalopram) label (DailyMed); GLAD-PC Part II: Treatment and Ongoing Management of Adolescent Depression in Primary Care (2018); DSM-5-TR (APA, 2022)

Question 3

A 10-year-old boy was started 3 months ago on a low-dose inhaled corticosteroid with albuterol as needed for a dry cough at night and with running. The diagnosis of asthma was made from his symptoms; no lung function testing was done. His cough is unchanged. His parent supervises every dose, the pharmacy refill record is complete and his spacer technique is correct when observed. His lungs are clear and he has no rhinitis. Which step is MOST appropriate NEXT?

  1. Switch to a low-dose inhaled corticosteroid with a long-acting beta-agonist
  2. Add montelukast 5 mg chewable tablet at bedtime
  3. Increase to a medium-dose inhaled corticosteroid
  4. Spirometry before and after a bronchodilator

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. ICS-LABA is a valid step up for confirmed asthma in this age group, but escalating before confirming the diagnosis exposes him to more drug without a clear target.
  • B. Montelukast is an add-on option, but it carries an FDA boxed warning for neuropsychiatric effects, and adding it still skips the needed confirmation of the diagnosis.
  • C. Stepping up treats a disease that has not been confirmed; with good adherence and technique, a lack of any response to ICS is a reason to question the diagnosis first.
  • D. Correct. When a child has no response to a correctly taken controller, GINA advises confirming variable airflow limitation with spirometry and bronchodilator testing before escalating therapy.

GINA advises that before any step-up for uncontrolled symptoms, the clinician checks inhaler technique, adherence, the diagnosis, modifiable risk factors and comorbidities. Here adherence and technique are confirmed, so the diagnosis is the next item to check. Children of about 6 years and older can perform spirometry; in children an FEV1 rise of more than 12% of predicted after a bronchodilator supports asthma. Isolated cough has a wide differential, including protracted bacterial bronchitis, upper airway cough and habit cough, which will not improve with inhaled steroids.

Sources: GINA Asthma Strategy Report (2026); ATS/ERS Spirometry Standardization (2019)

Question 4

A 73-year-old woman with polymyalgia rheumatica responded well to prednisone 15 mg daily and has tapered to 7.5 mg daily over 4 months. She now reports 1 week of new right temporal headache, scalp tenderness when brushing her hair and jaw pain when chewing. Her vision is normal and fundoscopy shows no abnormality. ESR is 68 mm/h, up from 18 mm/h a month ago. Which is the MOST appropriate next step?

  1. Increase prednisone back to 15 mg daily
  2. Start prednisone 60 mg daily today and arrange urgent temporal artery biopsy
  3. Order a temporal artery biopsy and start treatment if it is positive
  4. Continue the taper and refer to ophthalmology

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Returning to the last effective dose is right for a PMR flare of girdle pain and stiffness, but cranial symptoms point to giant cell arteritis, which needs much higher doses.
  • B. Correct. New temporal headache, scalp tenderness and jaw claudication with a rising ESR suggest giant cell arteritis; high-dose glucocorticoids should start the same day to prevent vision loss, and biopsy or vascular ultrasound remains accurate for 1 to 2 weeks after starting.
  • C. Biopsy is important, but waiting for the result before raising the steroid dose risks sudden, permanent vision loss.
  • D. Normal vision now does not exclude imminent ischemic optic neuropathy; the taper must be reversed, and ophthalmology alone does not address the vasculitis.

About 15 to 20 percent of patients with polymyalgia rheumatica develop giant cell arteritis, often during the steroid taper. Monitoring at each taper visit should include asking about new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication and visual symptoms, and checking inflammatory markers. A PMR flare, with returning shoulder and hip girdle stiffness, is managed by going back to the previous effective dose. Suspected GCA without vision loss needs prednisone about 40 to 60 mg daily started at once, plus urgent rheumatology referral and temporal artery biopsy or ultrasound. Tocilizumab may be added to spare steroids.

Sources: ACR/VF Giant Cell Arteritis Guideline (2021); EULAR/ACR Recommendations for the Management of Polymyalgia Rheumatica (2015)

Question 5

A 67-year-old woman with major depressive disorder had a partial response to sertraline 150 mg daily, which she has taken for 6 months. Three weeks ago her psychiatrist added aripiprazole 5 mg daily. She now describes an inner restlessness, cannot sit through a television program and paces the kitchen, and she says she feels jittery. Her mood is somewhat better, and she denies suicidal thoughts. Temperature is 37.0 C, heart rate 84 beats/min, and she has no tremor, clonus, hyperreflexia, rigidity, sweating or diarrhea. In coordination with her psychiatrist, which is the MOST appropriate action?

  1. Stop sertraline for presumed serotonin syndrome
  2. Lower the aripiprazole dose
  3. Add benztropine 1 mg twice daily
  4. Increase aripiprazole to 10 mg daily for agitation

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Serotonin syndrome shows autonomic instability and neuromuscular signs such as clonus, hyperreflexia and tremor; she has none, and her sertraline dose has been stable for months.
  • B. Correct. Her subjective restlessness and pacing after starting aripiprazole are akathisia, and the first step is to lower the dose of the drug causing it, or stop it if needed.
  • C. Anticholinergics help acute dystonia and drug-induced parkinsonism but work poorly for akathisia, and the Beers Criteria advise avoiding strongly anticholinergic drugs in older adults because of confusion, constipation and falls.
  • D. Reading akathisia as anxiety or agitation of depression and raising the dose makes it worse; akathisia is dose related.

Akathisia, an inner restlessness with an urge to move, is among the most common adverse reactions when aripiprazole is added for depression, usually starting in the first weeks. It is distressing, can be mistaken for worsening anxiety and has been linked to suicidal thinking, so it should be asked about at each follow-up. Management starts with reducing the dose or stopping the drug; if it persists, low-dose propranolol or mirtazapine may be tried by the prescriber. Serotonin syndrome is ruled out here by the absence of clonus, hyperreflexia, tremor, fever or diarrhea. Monitoring for second-generation antipsychotics also includes weight, glucose and lipids.

Sources: ABILIFY (aripiprazole) label (DailyMed); AGS Beers Criteria (2023); Merck Manual Professional: Serotonin Syndrome (2025)

Question 6

A 74-year-old man with COPD (post-bronchodilator FEV1 55% predicted) was started 2 years ago on a LAMA/LABA/ICS inhaler by another clinician for frequent winter colds. He has had no COPD exacerbations needing oral steroids in that time, but he was treated twice in the past year for pneumonia confirmed on chest radiograph. His blood eosinophil count is 70 cells/µL on two checks. His dyspnea is stable. Which change is MOST appropriate?

  1. Switch to a LAMA/LABA inhaler
  2. Add azithromycin 250 mg daily to prevent infections
  3. Switch to an ICS/LABA inhaler and stop the LAMA
  4. Continue the triple inhaler because he has had no exacerbations

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Repeated pneumonia, a weak original indication and eosinophils under 100 cells/µL are the GOLD reasons to withdraw the inhaled corticosteroid and continue dual long-acting bronchodilators.
  • B. Long-term azithromycin is for exacerbations despite optimal inhaled therapy, mainly in former smokers, and it adds QT and hearing risks; it does not address the cause of his pneumonias.
  • C. This keeps the drug linked to his pneumonias and removes the bronchodilator class with the best evidence for preventing exacerbations; GOLD does not favor ICS/LABA in COPD.
  • D. Tempting because he is stable, but GOLD advises reviewing the ICS when pneumonia occurs or the original reason was weak; his stability does not depend on the ICS when eosinophils are this low.

GOLD's follow-up cycle is review, assess, adjust. Inhaled corticosteroids raise pneumonia risk, especially in older patients, those with lower BMI and those with more severe airflow limitation. GOLD advises considering ICS de-escalation when pneumonia occurs, when the original indication was inappropriate, or when there is no response, and notes that withdrawal is least likely to cause exacerbations when eosinophils are under 300 and especially under 100 cells/µL. After the switch, the NP reviews him for exacerbations and symptoms at the next visit.

Sources: GOLD COPD Report (2026)

Question 7

A 44-year-old woman has used a combined oral contraceptive (ethinyl estradiol 30 mcg with levonorgestrel) for 6 years. She does not smoke and has no migraine. At her annual visit her blood pressure is 148/94 mm Hg, and at two nurse visits over the next 3 weeks, with a correctly sized cuff, it is 146/92 and 150/96 mm Hg; it was 124/78 mm Hg two years ago. She wants reliable contraception and plans no more children. Which is the MOST appropriate next step?

  1. Change to a levonorgestrel intrauterine device
  2. Change to the etonogestrel and ethinyl estradiol vaginal ring
  3. Change to a pill with 20 mcg of ethinyl estradiol
  4. Continue the pill and start amlodipine 5 mg daily

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The levonorgestrel IUD is category 1 at this blood pressure level, is highly effective, and removes the estrogen that may be raising her pressure; her blood pressure should be rechecked after the change.
  • B. The ring is a combined hormonal method with the same category 3 rating as the pill at this blood pressure; changing the route does not remove the estrogen risk.
  • C. A lower-dose combined pill still contains estrogen, and the US MEC category for combined methods with blood pressure of 140-159/90-99 mm Hg is 3 regardless of the estrogen dose.
  • D. Treating the blood pressure while keeping the pill is tempting, but even adequately controlled hypertension makes combined hormonal contraception category 3, and the pill may be the cause of the rise.

Under the US MEC 2024, elevated blood pressure of 140-159 systolic or 90-99 diastolic mm Hg, confirmed on repeat measurements, makes all combined hormonal contraception (pill, patch, ring) category 3, and 160/100 mm Hg or higher makes it category 4; adequately controlled hypertension is also category 3. Estrogen can raise blood pressure, and the rise often resolves within months of stopping it. Estrogen-free methods are safer choices: the levonorgestrel IUD, the etonogestrel implant and the copper IUD are category 1 at her level. The US SPR advises a blood pressure check before starting combined methods and at follow-up; recheck after switching and treat if hypertension persists.

Sources: CDC U.S. MEC for Contraceptive Use (2024); CDC U.S. Selected Practice Recommendations (2024)

Question 8

A 10-year-old boy was seen 3 days ago for 1 day of left scrotal pain. He had a tender nodule at the upper pole of the left testis with a blue dot visible through the skin and a normal cremasteric reflex, and scrotal Doppler ultrasound showed normal, symmetric testicular blood flow with a small avascular nodule beside the upper pole. He was sent home on ibuprofen and rest. At follow-up today, the pain is milder but still bothers him when he walks. He has no fever, vomiting or urinary symptoms. The testis lies normally and is nontender, and the upper-pole nodule is less tender. Which is the MOST appropriate plan?

  1. Continue ibuprofen and limited activity, with return precautions
  2. Repeat the scrotal Doppler ultrasound today
  3. Refer to pediatric urology for excision of the appendix testis
  4. Start cephalexin for presumed epididymitis and send a urine culture

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Torsion of the appendix testis is self-limited; pain usually settles within about a week with analgesia and rest, and the family should return at once for sudden severe pain, swelling, a high-riding testis or vomiting.
  • B. A repeat study is warranted if the exam changes, for example new testicular tenderness or a high-riding testis. His testis is nontender and the nodule is improving, so the original normal-flow study still stands.
  • C. Excision is reserved for pain that persists well beyond the usual course or cannot be controlled. Three days in, with steady improvement, surgery is premature.
  • D. Epididymitis can mimic this picture, but in prepubertal boys it is usually postinfectious or due to urinary reflux, and antibiotics are given only with pyuria or a positive culture; he has no urinary symptoms and the imaging showed a torsed appendage.

The appendix testis is a small Mullerian remnant at the upper pole; when it twists, children aged about 7 to 14 years develop localized upper-pole tenderness, sometimes with a visible blue dot, while the cremasteric reflex and testicular blood flow stay normal. Once Doppler has excluded testicular torsion, care is supportive: nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, rest and scrotal support, with most pain gone in 5 to 10 days. Follow-up should confirm steady improvement and teach return precautions, because a separate testicular torsion remains a surgical emergency. Persistent or refractory pain beyond the usual course is the indication for urology referral.

Sources: Merck Manual: Testicular Torsion (2025); Fujita et al., Torsion of the Appendix Testis vs Testicular Torsion (2017)

Question 9

A 68-year-old man is on day 9 of a planned 4-week course of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole DS twice daily for acute bacterial prostatitis. His fever and dysuria have resolved, but he noticed tiny red spots on both shins 2 days ago. He has no gum, nose, urine or stool bleeding. Platelet count is 62,000/mm3 (240,000/mm3 one month ago); hemoglobin, white blood cell count and creatinine are normal, and the smear shows large platelets with no schistocytes. Which action is MOST appropriate?

  1. Start prednisone 1 mg/kg daily and continue the antibiotic
  2. Refer him for a bone marrow biopsy
  3. Switch to levofloxacin and recheck a CBC within a week
  4. Continue the antibiotic and recheck the platelet count when the course ends

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Corticosteroids are not first-line for drug-induced thrombocytopenia and do not replace removing the drug; with no mucosal bleeding and a count above 50,000/mm3 they add risk without benefit.
  • B. Marrow examination is for unexplained thrombocytopenia with other cytopenias, abnormal cells on the smear or failure to recover; an isolated fall with a clear drug trigger does not need it first.
  • C. Correct. A new fall in platelets about a week into a sulfonamide, without schistocytes or other cell line changes, is most consistent with drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia; stopping the drug usually brings recovery within 1 to 2 weeks, and the prostatitis still needs a full course of an alternative agent.
  • D. Continuing a likely offending drug lets the count keep falling and risks serious bleeding; sulfonamides are a common cause of drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia.

Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia typically appears 5 to 10 days after starting a new drug, or sooner on re-exposure, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is one of the most frequently implicated agents. The key evaluation step is stopping the suspected drug and following the count, which usually rises within days and normalizes within about 1 to 2 weeks. A smear without schistocytes and a normal creatinine and hemoglobin argue against thrombotic microangiopathy. Severe thrombocytopenia below 20,000/mm3, mucosal or internal bleeding, or failure to recover warrants urgent hematology evaluation. Acute bacterial prostatitis still requires 2 to 4 weeks of therapy, so a fluoroquinolone completes the course.

Sources: AFP: Thrombocytopenia, Evaluation and Management (2022); AFP: Acute Bacterial Prostatitis, Diagnosis and Management (2016); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 10

A 52-year-old man has posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) after a workplace explosion 2 years ago. At diagnosis he declined psychotherapy and started sertraline; he has now taken 200 mg daily for 10 weeks, takes it every day and has no side effects. His PCL-5 score has fallen from 58 to 41, but he still has nightmares, avoids driving past the site and is irritable at home. He denies suicidal ideation and drinks about one beer a week. He now says he is ready to talk to someone. Which is the BEST next step in his treatment?

  1. Add quetiapine 25 mg at bedtime for sleep and nightmares
  2. Add clonazepam 0.5 mg at bedtime for hyperarousal
  3. Refer for trauma-focused psychotherapy
  4. Switch from sertraline to venlafaxine extended release

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Antipsychotic add-ons lack good evidence for PTSD, and quetiapine brings weight gain, sedation and metabolic risk; a low bedtime dose is often used for sleep but is not a guideline-supported step.
  • B. Benzodiazepines are recommended against in PTSD: they do not treat the core symptoms, carry dependence risk and may interfere with exposure-based therapy.
  • C. Correct. Individual manualized trauma-focused psychotherapy, such as cognitive processing therapy or prolonged exposure, is recommended over pharmacotherapy, and he is now willing; sertraline can continue while he starts it.
  • D. Venlafaxine is a recommended drug for PTSD, and a switch is reasonable after an inadequate response; but he has partly responded, and the more effective step he now accepts is trauma-focused psychotherapy.

The VA/DoD 2023 PTSD guideline recommends individual, manualized trauma-focused psychotherapy (prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy or eye movement desensitization and reprocessing) over other psychotherapies and over medication. When drugs are used, sertraline, paroxetine and venlafaxine are recommended, and benzodiazepines are recommended against. A PCL-5 drop of 17 points is a meaningful partial response, but his score remains above the usual cutoff of about 31 to 33, so treatment should be escalated. Evaluation includes asking about suicidal thoughts, alcohol and safety at each visit and repeating the PCL-5 to track progress.

Sources: VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline: Management of PTSD and Acute Stress Disorder (2023); VA National Center for PTSD: Clinician’s Guide to Medications for PTSD (2025)
