Question 1

A 40-year-old woman whose last menstrual period was 6 weeks ago has light spotting and mild pelvic cramping. Two days ago her quantitative serum hCG was 1,900 mIU/mL and transvaginal ultrasound showed no intrauterine pregnancy, no adnexal mass and no free fluid. Today her hCG is 2,050 mIU/mL. She is afebrile, HR 82/min, BP 122/76 mm Hg, and her abdomen is soft and nontender. Which interpretation is MOST accurate?

  1. A ruptured ectopic pregnancy with active intra-abdominal hemorrhage
  2. A completed miscarriage with hCG still clearing from the circulation
  3. An abnormal pregnancy, ectopic or failing intrauterine, of unknown location
  4. A normal early intrauterine pregnancy that is still too small to see

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Rupture would be suggested by hemodynamic instability, peritoneal signs or free fluid on ultrasound; she has none, so this overstates what the data show.
  • B. After a completed miscarriage the hCG falls, usually by at least 20% to 35% in 48 hours; a plateau or slight rise does not fit.
  • C. Correct. An 8% rise in 48 hours is far below the minimum expected for a viable intrauterine pregnancy, so the pregnancy is very likely nonviable; its location is not yet established.
  • D. Tempting because the hCG is below the 3,500 mIU/mL discriminatory level, but a viable pregnancy starting near 1,900 should rise by roughly 40% or more in 48 hours, not 8%.

In a pregnancy of unknown location, serial hCG values 48 hours apart separate viable from nonviable pregnancies. Data cited by ACOG show that a viable intrauterine pregnancy rises by at least about 49% in 48 hours when the starting level is below 1,500 mIU/mL, about 40% from 1,500 to 3,000, and about 33% above 3,000. A rise of 1,900 to 2,050 is about 8%, which strongly suggests a nonviable pregnancy, either an ectopic pregnancy or a failing intrauterine one, and the two cannot yet be told apart. She is stable, so she needs same-day gynecology referral for repeat ultrasound and possible uterine aspiration or methotrexate, with strict return precautions.

Sources: ACOG PB 193: Tubal Ectopic Pregnancy (2018); AFP: Ectopic Pregnancy (2020)

Question 2

A 52-year-old woman reports 3 months of constant worry about her job and family, restlessness, irritability and trouble sleeping. Her GAD-7 score is 14. She has lost 4 kg despite a good appetite and notes frequent loose stools. Heart rate is 104/min and regular, blood pressure 138/72 mm Hg, and she has a fine tremor of the outstretched hands. She drinks one cup of coffee daily and takes no medications. Before confirming generalized anxiety disorder, which test is MOST appropriate?

  1. Urine drug screen for stimulants
  2. Thyroid-stimulating hormone
  3. Plasma free metanephrines
  4. 24-hour ambulatory ECG monitoring

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Stimulant use is a reasonable consideration in new anxiety, but she reports no use, and the physical findings are better explained by thyrotoxicosis, so TSH comes first.
  • B. Correct. Weight loss with increased appetite, loose stools, tachycardia and fine tremor suggest hyperthyroidism, which must be excluded before attributing her symptoms to anxiety.
  • C. Pheochromocytoma can mimic anxiety, but it is rare and suspected with paroxysmal spells of headache, sweating and palpitations with hypertension; her steady weight loss and tremor point elsewhere first.
  • D. Ambulatory monitoring helps with intermittent palpitations, but her sinus tachycardia is persistent and the overall picture suggests a systemic cause that a blood test identifies faster.

DSM-5-TR criteria for generalized anxiety disorder require that the symptoms not be attributable to a substance or another medical condition, so the work-up looks for medical mimics. Hyperthyroidism is the most common one in a middle-aged woman: worry, insomnia and irritability accompanied by weight loss despite good appetite, hyperdefecation, resting tachycardia, heat intolerance and fine tremor. A TSH (with free T4 if low) is the best initial test. Other mimics to weigh by history include caffeine and stimulant use, alcohol withdrawal, arrhythmia, hypoglycemia and, rarely, pheochromocytoma. A GAD-7 of 10 or more supports, but does not by itself make, the diagnosis.

Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA, 2022); ATA Hyperthyroidism Guideline (2016); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 3

A 48-year-old woman has hypertension treated with amlodipine 10 mg, lisinopril 40 mg and chlorthalidone 25 mg daily. Pharmacy refill records and a pill count confirm adherence, and her validated home readings average 150/94 mm Hg. Her potassium is 3.2 mEq/L, sodium 143 mEq/L and creatinine 0.8 mg/dL. She has no episodic headaches, palpitations or sweating and no cushingoid features. Which is the MOST appropriate next diagnostic test?

  1. Plasma aldosterone and renin for an aldosterone-to-renin ratio
  2. Plasma free metanephrines
  3. Overnight 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test
  4. Renal artery duplex ultrasound

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. Resistant hypertension, especially with hypokalemia, calls for screening for primary aldosteronism with a morning plasma aldosterone and renin (activity or concentration), after correcting the potassium.
  • B. Pheochromocytoma screening fits paroxysmal headaches, palpitations, sweating or labile pressures, which she does not have.
  • C. Screening for Cushing syndrome is warranted with features such as proximal weakness, wide violaceous striae or easy bruising, which are absent.
  • D. Renovascular disease is a cause of resistant hypertension, but clues such as an abdominal bruit, a creatinine rise on ACE inhibitor therapy, flash pulmonary edema or diffuse atherosclerosis are absent here.

Primary aldosteronism is the most common curable cause of hypertension and is present in a large share of patients with resistant hypertension (uncontrolled on three drugs including a diuretic). Hypokalemia on a thiazide is common, but in resistant hypertension it strengthens the case for screening, and the Endocrine Society 2025 guideline now advises screening everyone with hypertension. The aldosterone-to-renin ratio can often be drawn without stopping most current drugs (mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists interfere most); ACE inhibitors and diuretics raise renin, so a suppressed renin despite them is telling. A positive screen goes to endocrinology for confirmation and subtype testing.

Sources: Endocrine Society 2025 Primary Aldosteronism Guideline; ESC Elevated Blood Pressure and Hypertension (2024)

Question 4

A 36-year-old carpenter has had 6 weeks of aching pain on the outside of his right elbow that is worse when he grips a hammer or lifts a full coffee mug. He has no neck pain. Exam shows point tenderness about 1 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle, pain at that site with resisted wrist extension while the elbow is straight, full elbow range of motion, normal sensation and strength in the hand, and a negative Spurling test. Which is the MOST likely diagnosis?

  1. Distal biceps tendinopathy
  2. Radial tunnel syndrome from compression of the posterior interosseous nerve at the supinator
  3. Lateral epicondylitis (extensor carpi radialis brevis tendinopathy)
  4. C6 cervical radiculopathy

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Distal biceps problems cause anterior elbow pain with resisted elbow flexion and forearm supination, not lateral pain with wrist extension.
  • B. Radial tunnel syndrome also causes lateral forearm pain, but the tender point is 3 to 5 cm distal to the epicondyle over the supinator, not just below the bony origin.
  • C. Correct. Tenderness at the common extensor origin and pain with resisted wrist extension are the defining findings of this overuse tendinopathy of the extensor carpi radialis brevis.
  • D. C6 radiculopathy can refer pain to the lateral arm, but it usually brings neck pain, a positive Spurling test and thumb numbness or biceps weakness, all absent here.

Lateral epicondylitis (lateral epicondylalgia, tennis elbow) is a degenerative tendinopathy of the wrist extensor origin, most common between ages 35 and 54 and in work or sport with repetitive gripping. The diagnosis is clinical: tenderness just distal and anterior to the lateral epicondyle with pain on resisted wrist or middle finger extension and on gripping. Imaging is not needed for a typical case. Normal sensation, strength and Spurling test steer away from nerve causes, and full range of motion argues against intra-articular disease. Most cases resolve within a year with activity change and a counterforce brace.

Sources: American Family Physician (AAFP); AAOS OrthoInfo

Question 5

A 27-year-old woman reports occasional palpitations and brief, sharp left chest twinges at rest. She is slender, and her blood pressure is 108/68 mm Hg. At the apex the nurse practitioner hears a midsystolic click followed by a late systolic murmur. When she stands from a squat, the click moves earlier toward S1 and the murmur lengthens; when she squats again, the click moves later. Which is the MOST likely diagnosis?

  1. Mitral valve prolapse
  2. Bicuspid aortic valve with mild stenosis
  3. Innocent flow murmur of a hyperdynamic state
  4. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with outflow obstruction

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A mobile midsystolic click with a late systolic murmur at the apex that moves earlier with standing (smaller left ventricle) and later with squatting is the signature of mitral valve prolapse.
  • B. A bicuspid valve can produce an ejection click, but it is early systolic, heard best at the base, and does not shift with standing and squatting the way a prolapse click does.
  • C. Innocent flow murmurs are soft midsystolic murmurs at the left sternal border without clicks; a click that moves with preload changes points to a structural valve finding.
  • D. Tempting because its murmur also grows louder on standing, but obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy gives a harsh crescendo-decrescendo murmur at the left lower sternal border without a midsystolic click.

Mitral valve prolapse is the most common valvular finding in young, slender women. The leaflets billow into the atrium when the ventricle shrinks to a critical size, producing the click; anything that reduces left ventricular volume (standing, Valsalva) makes this happen sooner in systole, and anything that increases volume (squatting, leg raise) delays it. Transthoracic echocardiography confirms the diagnosis and grades any mitral regurgitation, which sets the follow-up interval. Most patients need reassurance and periodic review only.

Sources: ESC/EACTS Valvular Heart Disease Guidelines (2025); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 6

A 29-year-old woman who takes a combined oral contraceptive comes to clinic with 2 days of right pleuritic chest pain and dyspnea on exertion. She flew home from overseas 5 days ago. Her left calf is swollen 3 cm larger than the right and tender along the deep veins. Data are: heart rate 112/min, respiratory rate 22/min, blood pressure 124/76 mm Hg, SpO2 94% on room air. She has no hemoptysis, cancer or prior clots. Which is the MOST appropriate next step?

  1. Apply the PERC rule to decide whether any testing is needed
  2. Outpatient D-dimer, reviewing the result the next morning
  3. Same-day ED referral for CT pulmonary angiography
  4. Office ECG and chest radiograph, then reassess in 48 hours

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. PERC is used when clinical probability is already low, and she fails several criteria (heart rate, SpO2, hormone use, unilateral leg swelling).
  • B. Tempting because D-dimer is the usual first test, but it is used to rule out PE when the Wells score is 4 or less; with PE likely, a negative D-dimer cannot safely exclude it, and an overnight delay is unsafe.
  • C. Correct. Signs of deep vein thrombosis (3 points) plus heart rate above 100/min (1.5 points) already give a Wells score of 4.5, so PE is likely and imaging, not D-dimer, is the next test.
  • D. An ECG and radiograph help exclude other causes but cannot rule out PE; sending her home for 48 hours with a likely PE risks clot progression and death.

The two-level Wells score assigns 3 points for clinical signs of DVT, 3 if PE is the most likely diagnosis, 1.5 each for heart rate above 100/min, recent immobilization or surgery and prior VTE, and 1 each for hemoptysis and cancer. A score above 4 makes PE likely, and the patient goes straight to CT pulmonary angiography without D-dimer; anticoagulation is started while imaging is awaited if it cannot be done promptly. A score of 4 or less calls for a D-dimer, and PERC can end the workup in low-probability patients. Estrogen and long travel raise risk even though Wells does not score them.

Sources: ESC/ERS Acute PE Guidelines (2019); CHEST Antithrombotic Therapy for VTE (2021); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 7

A 3-week-old boy born at term is brought for a weight check. His mother says he tires and sweats during feeds, and he has gained about 10 g/day since discharge. His respiratory rate is 62/min, and SpO2 is 98% in the right hand and the foot. A grade 3/6 harsh holosystolic murmur is heard at the left lower sternal border; no murmur was noted at the newborn discharge exam. Femoral and brachial pulses are equal and strong. Which is the MOST likely diagnosis?

  1. Patent ductus arteriosus with a left-to-right shunt
  2. Tetralogy of Fallot
  3. Peripheral pulmonary artery stenosis
  4. Ventricular septal defect

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. A significant ductus also causes failure to thrive, but its murmur is continuous (machinery-like) at the left infraclavicular area, often with bounding pulses, not holosystolic at the lower sternal border.
  • B. Tetralogy produces a systolic ejection murmur at the upper left sternal border and cyanosis from right-to-left shunting; normal saturations in hand and foot argue against it.
  • C. This common benign newborn murmur is soft, ejection in quality, heard at the upper sternal border and radiating to the axillae and back, and it does not cause feeding fatigue or poor growth.
  • D. Correct. A harsh holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border that appears as pulmonary vascular resistance falls over the first weeks, with tachypnea, sweating during feeds and poor weight gain, fits a moderate-to-large ventricular septal defect.

Ventricular septal defect is the most common congenital heart lesion. At birth, high pulmonary vascular resistance limits shunting, so the murmur may be absent; as resistance falls over 2 to 8 weeks, left-to-right flow rises, the holosystolic murmur appears, and large defects cause heart failure signs: tachypnea, diaphoresis and fatigue with feeds, and poor weight gain. A newborn with these findings needs echocardiography and prompt pediatric cardiology referral. Normal preductal and postductal saturations and equal pulses make cyanotic disease and coarctation less likely.

Sources: American Family Physician (AAFP); AAP Bright Futures Guidelines (4th ed.) and Periodicity Schedule

Question 8

A 44-year-old woman has 4 months of widespread pain with unrefreshing sleep, fatigue and poor concentration. She has Hashimoto thyroiditis treated with levothyroxine, and her TSH last month was 1.8 mIU/L. CBC, ESR, CRP, CK and a metabolic panel are normal. Her widespread pain index is 8, her symptom severity scale score is 6, and she has pain in 4 of the 5 body regions. On examination 7 of the 18 traditional tender points are tender. Which conclusion is MOST accurate?

  1. She meets criteria for fibromyalgia now
  2. She falls short of fibromyalgia criteria, because at least 11 of 18 tender points must be tender on examination
  3. Fibromyalgia cannot be diagnosed while she has autoimmune thyroid disease
  4. She falls short of fibromyalgia criteria, because symptoms must be present for at least 6 months

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. A widespread pain index of 7 or more with a symptom severity score of 5 or more, generalized pain in at least 4 of 5 regions and symptoms for at least 3 months meet the 2016 criteria, and a coexisting diagnosis does not exclude fibromyalgia.
  • B. The 11 of 18 tender point rule came from the 1990 classification criteria; the 2010 and 2016 criteria replaced the tender point examination with the pain index and symptom severity scores.
  • C. The 2016 revision states that a fibromyalgia diagnosis is valid regardless of other diagnoses; her thyroid disease is treated and her TSH is normal, so it does not explain her symptoms.
  • D. The required duration is symptoms at a similar level for at least 3 months, which her 4-month history meets.

The 2016 revised fibromyalgia criteria require a widespread pain index of 7 or more with a symptom severity score of 5 or more (or an index of 4 to 6 with a severity score of 9 or more), generalized pain in at least 4 of 5 regions, and symptoms at a similar level for at least 3 months. The revision also made clear that fibromyalgia is not a diagnosis of exclusion: it can coexist with other conditions such as autoimmune thyroid disease or rheumatoid arthritis. A small set of tests (CBC, inflammatory markers, TSH, metabolic panel) is reasonable to look for mimics, but tender point counts are no longer part of diagnosis.

Sources: 2016 Revisions to the Fibromyalgia Diagnostic Criteria; American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 9

A 70-year-old woman was bitten on the back of the left hand by her own cat while giving it medicine 14 hours ago. She washed the wound at home. Since this morning the hand has become red, swollen and very painful, with serous drainage from two small puncture wounds. She takes lisinopril and has an intact spleen. Temperature is 37.6 C (99.7 F). Erythema extends 4 cm from the punctures, finger range of motion is full but painful over the wound, and there is no axillary or epitrochlear adenopathy. Which organism is MOST likely causing this infection?

  1. Bartonella henselae
  2. Capnocytophaga canimorsus
  3. Staphylococcus aureus
  4. Pasteurella multocida

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Bartonella causes cat-scratch disease, with a papule at the inoculation site followed 1 to 3 weeks later by tender regional lymphadenopathy, not rapid cellulitis within a day.
  • B. Capnocytophaga is associated mainly with dog bites and causes severe sepsis in people with asplenia or alcohol use disorder; she has neither, and the infection is local.
  • C. S. aureus and streptococci are common in bite infections, but they usually cause infections that appear more than 24 hours after the bite, rather than within hours.
  • D. Correct. Pasteurella is carried in the mouths of most cats and causes rapidly progressive cellulitis with pain and swelling, often within 12 to 24 hours of a bite.

Cat bites become infected more often than dog bites because the sharp teeth inject bacteria deep into tissue, often over the joints and tendon sheaths of the hand. Pasteurella multocida is the leading pathogen, and infection develops rapidly, within 24 hours. Bite infections are usually polymicrobial, with streptococci, staphylococci and anaerobes, so IDSA recommends amoxicillin-clavulanate. Cat bites on the hand need close follow-up or hand surgery referral for signs of tenosynovitis, septic arthritis or osteomyelitis. Tetanus status should be checked.

Sources: IDSA Skin and Soft Tissue Infections (2014); Merck Manual Professional: Human and Mammal Bites (2025)

Question 10

A 57-year-old man receiving chemotherapy for metastatic colon cancer reports 3 days of swelling and aching in the right calf. The right calf is 4 cm larger than the left, there is pitting edema in the right leg only, and he is tender along the deep veins of the calf. He has had no recent surgery or trauma, and no other diagnosis seems as likely. Which is the MOST appropriate next diagnostic step?

  1. CT venography of the pelvis and legs
  2. Venous compression ultrasound of the right leg
  3. High-sensitivity D-dimer first, then ultrasound if it is positive
  4. Serial calf measurements at a visit in 1 week

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. CT venography adds radiation and contrast and is reserved for selected cases, such as suspected pelvic or iliac thrombus when ultrasound is inconclusive; compression ultrasound is the first imaging test.
  • B. Correct. Active cancer, calf swelling over 3 cm, unilateral pitting edema and deep-vein tenderness give a Wells score of 4, so DVT is likely and imaging comes first.
  • C. D-dimer first is the strategy for a low or unlikely pretest probability; with a likely score a negative result would not safely exclude DVT, and cancer itself raises D-dimer, making a positive result uninformative.
  • D. Watchful waiting leaves a likely proximal-extending clot untested in a patient at high thrombotic risk; DVT is excluded or confirmed with imaging, not by following calf size.

Diagnosis of suspected DVT starts with pretest probability. The Wells score gives 1 point each for active cancer, paralysis or recent casting, bed rest or major surgery, deep-vein tenderness, entire-leg swelling, calf swelling over 3 cm, unilateral pitting edema, collateral superficial veins and prior DVT, and subtracts 2 when another diagnosis is as likely. A score of 2 or more (DVT likely) goes straight to compression ultrasound, proximal or whole-leg; an unlikely score starts with a D-dimer. In cancer, D-dimer is often elevated, so it rarely helps.

Sources: ASH 2018 VTE Guidelines: Diagnosis of VTE; American Family Physician (AAFP)
