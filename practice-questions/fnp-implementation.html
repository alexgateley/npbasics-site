Question 1

A nurse practitioner uses an ambient artificial intelligence scribe, with the patient's agreement, during a visit with a 61-year-old man who has type 2 diabetes. The draft note states "monofilament examination normal bilaterally" and "counseled on statin therapy," but the NP did neither during this visit. What should the NP do BEFORE signing the note?

  1. Leave the wording and complete the foot exam and statin talk at the next visit
  2. Add a statement that the note was generated by AI and not fully reviewed
  3. Sign the note and report the error to the vendor so the model can improve
  4. Delete the two statements so the note shows only what was done

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Doing the care later does not make today's note true; documenting care not performed is a falsified record and a billing risk.
  • B. A disclaimer does not remove the NP's accountability; the signer attests that the content is accurate, so unreviewed content cannot be signed.
  • C. Reporting the error to the vendor is reasonable, but signing first puts false findings into the legal record under the NP's name.
  • D. Correct. The signing clinician is responsible for every entry, so AI-drafted content must be edited to match the care actually given before authentication.

Ambient AI scribes and other generative tools can fabricate plausible findings (hallucinations) or carry forward template text. A signature authenticates the entry as the author's own account of the encounter, so the NP must review every AI-drafted note, remove anything not done, and correct errors before signing. The NP should then make sure the missing care happens: an annual comprehensive foot exam with monofilament testing and a statin discussion are standard for adults with diabetes. Responsible use also includes disclosure and consent for recording as the organization and state require, and reporting recurrent tool errors.

Sources: CMS Medicare Program Integrity Manual, Ch. 3 (signatures, amendments and corrections); AANP Scope and Standards of Practice for NPs; ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2026)

Question 2

An 84-year-old woman with moderate Alzheimer dementia is brought in by her son, who is her live-in caregiver and health care agent. The nurse practitioner finds a stage 3 sacral pressure injury, a 5-kg weight loss over 2 months, and bruises in different stages of healing on both upper arms. The son says she bruises easily and asks the NP not to involve any agencies. The NP practices in a state that requires health care providers to report suspected abuse or neglect of vulnerable adults. Which action is MOST appropriate?

  1. Respect the son's request, because as her health care agent he directs her care
  2. Arrange home health nursing and reassess the bruising at a visit in 2 weeks
  3. Report the suspected abuse and neglect to adult protective services today and document the findings
  4. Order a platelet count and coagulation studies and report if they are normal

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. A health care agent makes treatment decisions when the patient lacks capacity, but that authority does not let a suspected abuser block a legally required report.
  • B. Home health may help, but delaying a required report leaves a vulnerable adult at risk and does not meet the legal duty.
  • C. Correct. In a mandatory-reporting state, reasonable suspicion is the threshold; the pattern of untreated pressure injury, weight loss and bruises of different ages meets it, and HIPAA permits disclosures required by law.
  • D. Ruling out a bleeding disorder is a reasonable part of the workup, but the duty to report rests on suspicion rather than proof, and the neglect findings stand on their own; the report should not wait.

Elder mistreatment includes physical abuse and caregiver neglect; red flags are injuries of different ages, untreated pressure injuries, unexplained weight loss and a caregiver who resists outside involvement. Where state law requires health care providers to report suspected abuse of vulnerable adults, the NP reports on reasonable suspicion to adult protective services without needing proof, the patient's agreement or the caregiver's permission. HIPAA (45 CFR 164.512(a) and (c)) permits disclosures required by law and to agencies authorized to receive abuse reports. The NP also treats the wound, evaluates nutrition, documents findings objectively, and assesses immediate safety.

Sources: AFP: Elder Abuse and Neglect (2014); HIPAA Privacy Rule, 45 CFR 164.512

Question 3

A 16-year-old girl is seen alone in a family practice clinic for vaginal discharge, consents to her own testing, and is treated for chlamydia. In this state minors may consent to their own STI care, and state law neither requires nor permits notifying parents. Two days later her mother, who is listed as her emergency contact and carries the family insurance, calls the nurse practitioner and asks what the visit found. Which response is MOST appropriate?

  1. Share the results, because the mother is the legal guardian of a minor patient
  2. Tell the mother the testing was routine and nothing serious was found
  3. Explain that the visit information can be shared only with the teen's permission
  4. Share the results because the mother's insurance will be billed for the visit

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Parents are usually the personal representative of a minor, which makes this tempting, but HIPAA makes an exception for care the minor consented to under state law, as here.
  • B. A reassuring summary still discloses (and here misstates) protected information about the visit; confidentiality covers the fact and outcome of care, not just the lab values.
  • C. Correct. When a minor lawfully consents to care under state law, HIPAA treats her as the individual for that care, so the parent is not her personal representative for it and the clinic needs the teen's agreement to disclose.
  • D. Paying for or holding the insurance does not make a parent the personal representative for confidential minor-consented care; the explanation of benefits risk is handled by discussing billing options with the teen, not by disclosing.

Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule (45 CFR 164.502(g)), a parent is generally the personal representative of an unemancipated minor, but not for care the minor may lawfully consent to herself, such as STI care in this state. For that care the adolescent controls disclosure, and because state law here neither requires nor permits parental notification, the NP declines and offers to help the teen talk with her mother. Good practice also anticipates confidentiality leaks through insurance explanations of benefits, patient portals and pharmacy pickups, and encourages, without forcing, parental involvement. Expedited partner therapy and retesting in about 3 months follow the CDC STI guideline.

Sources: HIPAA Privacy and Breach Rules, 45 CFR 164; AAFP Policy: Adolescent Health Care, Confidentiality (2025); CDC STI Treatment Guidelines (2021)

Question 4

A 16-year-old rolled his ankle at basketball yesterday. He walks 4 steps in clinic and has lateral swelling but no bony tenderness at the malleoli, navicular or fifth metatarsal. Which plan is BEST?

  1. Crutches without weight bearing for 2 weeks
  2. Ankle radiographs before deciding on treatment
  3. Lace-up brace, weight bearing as tolerated, early exercises
  4. Short leg cast for 2 weeks

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Prolonged non-weight bearing slows healing and return to sport; early protected loading is preferred once a fracture is excluded clinically.
  • B. He can bear weight and has no bony tenderness at the Ottawa points, so a fracture is unlikely and radiographs are not indicated.
  • C. Correct. Functional support with early weight bearing and exercise therapy gives faster return to activity and fewer recurrences than rigid immobilization for a lateral ankle sprain.
  • D. Rigid immobilization is tempting for a painful sprain, but it delays recovery and causes stiffness; it is reserved for severe sprains, and then for no more than about 10 days.

The Ottawa ankle rules call for radiographs only with inability to bear weight for 4 steps or bony tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus, the navicular or the base of the fifth metatarsal. With none of these, treat as a lateral sprain: a lace-up brace or tape, weight bearing as tolerated, NSAIDs or acetaminophen for pain, and early balance and strengthening exercises. Early loading is preferred because it restores function faster than a cast, and supervised exercise lowers the risk of a repeat sprain.

Sources: Ankle Sprains: Evidence-Based Clinical Guideline Update (BJSM 2018); Ottawa Ankle Rules: Implementation Study (JAMA 1994); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 5

A 41-year-old woman, G2P1, is seen at 10 weeks of gestation. She has chronic hypertension controlled on labetalol 200 mg twice daily, with blood pressure 128/80 mm Hg today. Her first pregnancy was uncomplicated. She has no bleeding disorder and no aspirin allergy. Which aspirin plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Aspirin 325 mg daily starting now
  2. Aspirin 81 mg daily starting after 20 weeks if proteinuria appears
  3. Aspirin 81 mg daily from 12 weeks until 28 weeks
  4. Aspirin 81 mg daily from 12 weeks until delivery

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The evidence and US recommendations support low-dose aspirin, 81 mg; a 325 mg dose adds bleeding risk without proven extra benefit.
  • B. Aspirin is preventive and works best started before 16 weeks; giving it once preeclampsia is suspected does not treat the disease.
  • C. The start is right, but stopping at 28 weeks gives up protection in the third trimester, when most preeclampsia appears; aspirin continues until delivery.
  • D. Correct. Chronic hypertension is a high-risk factor for preeclampsia, so low-dose aspirin is advised from 12 weeks, ideally before 16, and continued until delivery.

The USPSTF (2021) recommends low-dose aspirin, 81 mg daily, after 12 weeks of gestation for people at high risk for preeclampsia, and ACOG advises starting between 12 and 28 weeks, optimally before 16 weeks, and continuing until delivery. High-risk factors include prior preeclampsia, multifetal gestation, chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes, kidney disease and autoimmune disease such as lupus or antiphospholipid syndrome; one is enough. Two or more moderate factors, such as nulliparity, obesity, age 35 or older, or Black race (as a marker of structural racism), also qualify. Her age is a moderate factor, but the chronic hypertension alone qualifies her. She also needs a baseline urine protein, creatinine, liver enzymes and platelets.

Sources: USPSTF 2021: Aspirin Use to Prevent Preeclampsia; ACOG PB 222: Preeclampsia (2020); ACOG PB 203: Chronic HTN in Pregnancy (2019)

Question 6

A 74-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation was seen yesterday, and the nurse practitioner dictated "metoprolol succinate 50 mg daily" using voice recognition software. Today the NP notices the signed note reads "15 mg daily." The electronic prescription sent to the pharmacy was correct, but the note was faxed with a referral to her cardiologist yesterday afternoon. Which action is BEST?

  1. Call the cardiology office with the correct dose and document the phone call
  2. Edit the signed note to read 50 mg, since the prescription itself was correct
  3. Add a dated addendum correcting the dose, and send the correction to the cardiologist
  4. Leave the note unchanged, since the pharmacy received the correct dose

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Calling the cardiologist is useful, but it leaves the wrong dose standing in the NP's own signed note, where later readers will find it.
  • B. Overwriting a signed entry without a visible, dated correction alters the legal record and can look like tampering.
  • C. Correct. A dated, signed amendment keeps the original visible and fixes the record, and the cardiologist must get the corrected dose before acting on the wrong one.
  • D. The pharmacy record is right, but the chart is wrong, and the error already reached a clinician who may adjust therapy from it.

Voice recognition errors with numbers that sound alike (fifteen and fifty) are a known documentation hazard, so the NP should proofread dictated notes before signing. Once a note is signed, CMS and general medical-record standards require corrections as a clearly identified amendment that is dated, timed and authenticated by the author, with the original entry still readable; backdating or deleting is improper. Because the incorrect note was already sent, the NP also sends the corrected information to the cardiologist and confirms that the medication list in the chart shows the right dose.

Sources: CMS Medicare Program Integrity Manual, Ch. 3 (signatures, amendments and corrections); AANP Scope and Standards of Practice for NPs

Question 7

A 58-year-old man had a positive fecal immunochemical test (FIT) 10 weeks ago, and the nurse practitioner placed a referral for colonoscopy. Reviewing a tracking list, the NP finds no colonoscopy report in the chart and no note that the patient was contacted. A portal message from him 4 weeks ago said he feels well and has seen no blood in his stool. His next scheduled visit is in 3 months. Which action is MOST appropriate?

  1. Order CT colonography, since it does not need a gastroenterology appointment
  2. Repeat the FIT now to confirm the first result before pursuing the referral
  3. Contact him and the GI office to find out whether the colonoscopy was done
  4. Review the referral status with him at the visit in 3 months

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. CT colonography is a screening option, not the follow-up for a positive stool test, and a finding would still require colonoscopy, adding cost and delay.
  • B. A positive FIT is not confirmed or cancelled by a repeat stool test; a negative repeat would falsely reassure and delay the needed colonoscopy.
  • C. Correct. The ordering clinician is responsible for closing the referral loop, finding the barrier and getting the colonoscopy completed.
  • D. Tempting because he reports no symptoms, but most colorectal cancers found after a positive FIT are asymptomatic, and waiting lets a positive screening test go without follow-up for months; delays beyond several months are linked to worse outcomes.

Any positive stool-based screening test requires follow-up colonoscopy; the USPSTF notes that screening benefit depends on completing it in a timely way. Missed or incomplete referrals are a leading cause of diagnostic delay in primary care. The ordering clinician tracks referrals until the result returns: contact the patient and the specialist's office, find the barrier (cost, transport, bowel preparation fears, a lost referral), arrange help such as patient navigation, and document each step. Repeating the stool test or substituting imaging wastes resources and delays diagnosis.

Sources: USPSTF: Colorectal Cancer Screening (2021); AHRQ: Closed Loop Diagnostics (Patient Safety Learning Laboratory)

Question 8

A 33-year-old woman, G2P1, is seen at 14 weeks of gestation. She had postpartum depression after her first delivery 3 years ago that resolved with sertraline, which she stopped 2 years ago. She currently feels well, sleeps normally and has a PHQ-9 score of 4 with no thoughts of self-harm. She asks what she can do to keep it from happening again. Which plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Refer for cognitive behavioral or interpersonal therapy
  2. Rescreen with the Edinburgh scale at the postpartum visit
  3. Restart sertraline 50 mg daily now
  4. Plan zuranolone 50 mg nightly for 14 days after delivery

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. The USPSTF recommends counseling interventions such as cognitive behavioral or interpersonal therapy for pregnant and postpartum people at increased risk of perinatal depression.
  • B. Screening continues, but waiting until after delivery misses the chance to prevent depression in a patient known to be at increased risk.
  • C. Antidepressants treat depression, but the USPSTF found evidence for counseling, not medication, as prevention in a patient who is currently well; sertraline remains an option if symptoms return.
  • D. Zuranolone is approved to treat postpartum depression once it is diagnosed, not to prevent it in a patient without symptoms.

The USPSTF (2019, grade B) recommends that clinicians refer pregnant and postpartum people at increased risk of perinatal depression to counseling interventions; cognitive behavioral therapy and interpersonal therapy reduced the risk of depression by about 39 percent in trials. A history of depression, especially prior perinatal depression, is one of the strongest risk factors; others include current depressive symptoms, low income, adolescent or single parenthood, intimate partner violence and a history of significant stressors. She should still be screened during pregnancy and postpartum, and she should know that sertraline is compatible with breastfeeding if treatment becomes necessary.

Sources: USPSTF 2019: Interventions to Prevent Perinatal Depression; USPSTF Recommendation Statements

Question 9

A 72-year-old woman has had fever, cough and myalgia for 2 days and tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 at home. Her oxygen saturation is 96% on room air, and she does not need hospital care. She has hypertension and chronic kidney disease with an eGFR of 38 mL/min/1.73 m2 and takes amlodipine 5 mg and simvastatin 40 mg daily. Which plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Molnupiravir 800 mg twice daily for 5 days; continue simvastatin
  2. Nirmatrelvir 300 mg with ritonavir 100 mg twice daily for 5 days; continue simvastatin
  3. Nirmatrelvir 150 mg with ritonavir 100 mg twice daily for 5 days; hold simvastatin
  4. Symptomatic care, since she does not need oxygen

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Molnupiravir avoids the interaction but is less effective and is reserved for when ritonavir-boosted nirmatrelvir and remdesivir are not options; the statin can be held for a few days instead.
  • B. The 300-mg dose is for an eGFR of 60 or higher, and continuing simvastatin with ritonavir is contraindicated because of the risk of myopathy.
  • C. Correct. Her age and kidney disease make her high risk, an eGFR of 30 to under 60 calls for the reduced 150-mg nirmatrelvir dose, and simvastatin must be stopped because ritonavir raises its levels enough to cause rhabdomyolysis.
  • D. Antivirals are aimed at outpatients who do not yet need oxygen; her age over 65 and chronic kidney disease put her at high risk of progression, so she should be treated within 5 days of symptom onset.

Ritonavir-boosted nirmatrelvir started within 5 days of symptoms is first-line for outpatients at high risk of severe COVID-19, which includes adults 65 and older and those with chronic kidney disease. The label sets the dose by kidney function: 300 mg nirmatrelvir with 100 mg ritonavir twice daily for an eGFR of 60 or more, 150 mg with 100 mg twice daily for 30 to under 60, and a once-daily schedule after a day-1 loading dose for under 30. Ritonavir strongly inhibits CYP3A, so lovastatin and simvastatin are stopped at least 12 hours before, during and for 5 days after treatment. Amlodipine levels also rise, so she should watch for dizziness and edema.

Sources: PAXLOVID (nirmatrelvir; ritonavir) prescribing information (2026)

Question 10

A 44-year-old man has had 2 weeks of low back pain radiating down the back of the left leg after lifting furniture. He has used ibuprofen and stayed active. Today he says the leg pain is a little better, but for 2 days he has had trouble starting his urine stream and the skin between his buttocks feels numb when he wipes. Ankle dorsiflexion and plantar flexion strength are 5/5. Which plan is MOST appropriate?

  1. Outpatient lumbar MRI within 2 weeks and physical therapy
  2. Gabapentin 300 mg at bedtime and continued activity
  3. Emergency MRI today via the emergency department
  4. Prednisone 50 mg daily for 5 days with recheck in 1 week

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. MRI is indicated, but not on a routine schedule. Suspected cauda equina syndrome needs imaging and surgical evaluation within hours, not weeks.
  • B. Gabapentin is a common choice for radicular pain, but it treats symptoms only and would delay the urgent work-up these new neurologic symptoms require.
  • C. Correct. Urinary hesitancy with saddle numbness is a red flag for cauda equina syndrome; emergency MRI and decompression give the best chance of preserving bladder, bowel and sexual function.
  • D. An oral steroid burst is sometimes offered for radicular pain, but the new bladder and perineal symptoms suggest cauda equina compression, which a week of observation could make permanent.

Most acute low back pain, even with sciatica, improves with activity, heat and NSAIDs, and imaging is not needed in the first 6 weeks. Red flags change that plan. New urinary retention or hesitancy, saddle anesthesia, fecal incontinence or progressive leg weakness suggest cauda equina syndrome, usually from a large central disc herniation. The ACR Appropriateness Criteria rate MRI as appropriate immediately in suspected cauda equina, and outcomes depend on how quickly decompression occurs. Improving leg pain and normal ankle strength do not exclude it, which is why the bladder and perineal history is decisive.

Sources: ACR Appropriateness Criteria: Low Back Pain (2021 Update); AFP: Acute Low Back Pain (2025); ACP Guideline: Noninvasive Treatments for Low Back Pain (2017)
