Question 1

A 68-year-old man has office blood pressures of 152/94 and 148/92 mm Hg, measured correctly at two visits a month apart. He brings a log from a wrist cuff he bought online, a model that is not on any validated-device list, showing home readings that average 128/80 mm Hg. He has no diabetes or kidney disease, and his examination is otherwise normal. Which step is BEST to confirm or exclude hypertension?

  1. Diagnose white-coat hypertension based on his home wrist-cuff log
  2. Echocardiogram to look for left ventricular hypertrophy before deciding
  3. Recheck his office blood pressure at his annual visit next year
  4. 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring before any treatment decision

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because the home average is normal, but an unvalidated wrist device is prone to positioning error, so its readings cannot exclude sustained hypertension.
  • B. Hypertrophy supports long-standing hypertension, but an echocardiogram does not confirm the diagnosis and is not a screening or confirmatory test for it.
  • C. Two elevated office readings in the stage 2 range call for out-of-office confirmation now, not a year of possible untreated hypertension.
  • D. Correct. Ambulatory monitoring is the reference standard for out-of-office confirmation and settles whether this is sustained hypertension or white-coat hypertension.

USPSTF recommends confirming an elevated office blood pressure with out-of-office measurement before diagnosis and treatment, because 15 to 30 percent of people with high office readings have white-coat hypertension. Ambulatory monitoring is the reference standard; validated upper-arm home monitoring is an acceptable alternative. Wrist devices are often unvalidated and read low or high with arm position, so this log neither confirms nor excludes the diagnosis. A daytime ambulatory average of 135/85 mm Hg or higher confirms hypertension.

Sources: USPSTF: Hypertension Screening in Adults (2021); VA/DoD Hypertension Guideline (2020); ESC Elevated Blood Pressure and Hypertension (2024)

Question 2

A 27-year-old woman with a positive home pregnancy test has light vaginal spotting and mild left lower abdominal cramping. Her last menstrual period was 6 weeks ago. Data are: HR 78/min, BP 116/72 mm Hg. The abdomen is soft without guarding. Transvaginal ultrasound shows no intrauterine pregnancy and no adnexal mass or free fluid. Quantitative serum hCG is 1,150 mIU/mL. What is the MOST appropriate next step?

  1. Repeat quantitative hCG in 48 hours
  2. Send her to the emergency department for surgical evaluation today
  3. Diagnose a completed miscarriage and plan routine follow-up
  4. Prescribe methotrexate for presumed ectopic pregnancy

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: A

  • A. Correct. This is a pregnancy of unknown location in a stable patient with an hCG below the level at which an intrauterine pregnancy should be seen, so the trend of hCG over 48 hours guides the next decision.
  • B. Urgent surgical evaluation is for hemodynamic instability, peritoneal signs or ultrasound signs of rupture such as a large amount of free fluid. She is stable, with a soft abdomen and no free fluid.
  • C. An empty uterus with a positive hCG is not proof of completed miscarriage unless an intrauterine pregnancy was seen before. Assuming so can miss an ectopic pregnancy.
  • D. Methotrexate is used once ectopic pregnancy is diagnosed or a nonviable pregnancy of unknown location is confirmed by serial values. Giving it now could end a desired early intrauterine pregnancy.

When transvaginal ultrasound shows neither an intrauterine nor an ectopic pregnancy, the patient has a pregnancy of unknown location. If she is stable and the hCG is below the discriminatory level (about 3,500 mIU/mL in ACOG's conservative approach), a normal early intrauterine pregnancy may still be too small to see, so the NP repeats the hCG in 48 hours. A rise of at least about 35% in that time suggests a viable intrauterine pregnancy and repeat ultrasound; a fall suggests a failing pregnancy; a plateau or slow rise raises concern for ectopic pregnancy. She needs clear return precautions for heavier bleeding, worsening pain, dizziness or shoulder pain, which suggest rupture.

Sources: AFP: Ectopic Pregnancy (2020)

Question 3

A 76-year-old man with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (EF 30%) takes sacubitril-valsartan, carvedilol, spironolactone and dapagliflozin. Amlodipine was added 6 weeks ago for persistent hypertension, and he now has bilateral ankle swelling. Which examination finding MOST supports volume overload rather than drug-induced edema?

  1. Pitting edema of both ankles extending to mid-shin
  2. Fine bibasilar crackles that clear after several deep coughs
  3. Positive abdominojugular reflux
  4. A weight gain of 0.4 kg since his visit one month ago

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Both fluid overload and dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers cause dependent pitting edema, so this finding does not separate them.
  • B. Crackles that clear with coughing suggest atelectasis; chronic heart failure often has clear lungs even when congested.
  • C. Correct. A sustained rise in jugular venous pressure with abdominal pressure reflects elevated right-sided filling pressure, which amlodipine edema does not produce.
  • D. A gain this small over a month is within daily variation; fluid retention usually shows as about 1 kg in a day or 2 kg in a week.

Calcium channel blocker edema comes from precapillary arteriolar dilation, which raises capillary pressure without raising central venous pressure, so the neck veins stay normal. Congestion from heart failure raises filling pressures, which shows as an elevated jugular venous pressure (more than 3 cm above the sternal angle with the patient at 30 to 45 degrees) and a positive abdominojugular reflux. These are the most useful bedside signs of congestion, because chronic heart failure patients often have clear lungs.

Sources: ESC Heart Failure Guideline (2021); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 4

A 58-year-old man who smokes a pack of cigarettes a day has a routine urine dipstick that is 3+ for blood. Two days earlier he ran his first half-marathon, and he has had aching thigh muscles since. He takes atorvastatin. He has no dysuria or visible blood in his urine. Microscopy of a fresh specimen shows 0 to 2 red blood cells per high-power field and no casts. Which test is MOST appropriate NEXT?

  1. Referral to urology for cystoscopy
  2. Urine cytology on three morning specimens
  3. Serum creatine kinase
  4. CT urography of the abdomen and pelvis

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Cystoscopy evaluates the bladder for cancer in confirmed hematuria, and his smoking history would make that important, but the finding here is a pigment-positive dipstick without red cells.
  • B. Urine cytology has low sensitivity and is not a first-line test; at most it is an adjunct to cystoscopy in selected high-risk patients with confirmed, persistent microhematuria, and his hematuria has not been confirmed.
  • C. Correct. A dipstick positive for blood with few or no red cells on microscopy suggests myoglobin or hemoglobin in the urine; after strenuous exercise in a patient taking a statin, muscle breakdown should be assessed with a creatine kinase.
  • D. CT urography is part of the workup of confirmed microscopic hematuria in a high-risk patient such as a smoker, but microscopy here does not confirm red cells, so hematuria has not been established.

The dipstick detects the peroxidase activity of heme, so it is positive with intact red cells, free hemoglobin from hemolysis, or myoglobin from muscle injury. Microscopic hematuria is confirmed only by microscopy showing at least 3 red cells per high-power field, and a positive dipstick should not trigger an imaging or cystoscopy workup on its own. A strongly positive dipstick with few red cells after intense exercise, especially in a statin user with myalgias, points to myoglobinuria from rhabdomyolysis, assessed with creatine kinase, creatinine and potassium. Repeat urinalysis with microscopy after he recovers is reasonable, since his smoking still makes confirmed hematuria significant.

Sources: AFP: Urinalysis Review (2005); American Family Physician (AAFP)

Question 5

A 44-year-old man has a screening lipid panel: total cholesterol 292 mg/dL, LDL cholesterol 212 mg/dL, HDL 46 mg/dL, triglycerides 140 mg/dL. He does not smoke, his blood pressure is 124/78 mm Hg and his glucose is normal. His calculated 10-year ASCVD risk is 4.8%. Which assessment is MOST appropriate NEXT?

  1. Coronary artery calcium score to refine his risk
  2. Family history of early heart disease and a tendon exam
  3. Repeat the lipid panel and risk estimate in five years
  4. Exercise treadmill test to screen for silent ischemia

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: B

  • A. Calcium scoring helps when risk is borderline or intermediate and the decision is uncertain; at this LDL level, statin therapy is indicated whatever the score.
  • B. Correct. An LDL of 190 mg/dL or higher suggests familial hypercholesterolemia, so premature ASCVD in relatives and tendon xanthomas are the key findings to look for.
  • C. The low calculated risk is reassuring only on the surface; risk equations do not apply when LDL is 190 mg/dL or higher.
  • D. Stress testing an asymptomatic patient is not recommended and does not change the lipid assessment.

Severe primary hypercholesterolemia (LDL 190 mg/dL or higher) is a statin benefit group on its own, so the pooled cohort equations underestimate his lifetime risk and USPSTF excludes these patients from its risk-based statement. The next step is to assess for familial hypercholesterolemia: premature ASCVD in first-degree relatives, tendon xanthomas, corneal arcus before age 45, and secondary causes such as hypothyroidism or nephrotic syndrome. Confirming familial disease matters because it prompts cascade screening of relatives, including children, and early high-intensity lipid lowering.

Sources: USPSTF: Statins for Primary Prevention (2022); VA/DoD Dyslipidemia Guideline (2020); ESC/EAS Dyslipidaemia Guideline (2019)

Question 6

A 52-year-old man with moderate alcohol use disorder asks for a medication to help him cut down. He drinks 5 to 6 beers most evenings and had 6 last night. He takes lisinopril for hypertension and tramadol 50 mg three times daily, prescribed by his orthopedist for knee osteoarthritis. He has no history of seizures or withdrawal. Data are: eGFR 55 mL/min/1.73 m², ALT 48 U/L (reference up to 40), AST 52 U/L. Which finding MOST affects whether naltrexone can be started today?

  1. His drinking last night, so he is not yet abstinent
  2. His eGFR of 55 mL/min/1.73 m²
  3. His ALT and AST just above the reference range
  4. His tramadol use

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Naltrexone can be started while a patient is still drinking and helps reduce heavy drinking days; it is disulfiram that requires abstinence before the first dose.
  • B. Reduced kidney function matters for acamprosate, which is renally cleared and needs a lower dose at a creatinine clearance of 30 to 50 mL/min; naltrexone is metabolized by the liver and needs no adjustment at this eGFR.
  • C. Mild aminotransferase elevation is common in heavy drinkers and is not a contraindication; naltrexone is avoided in acute hepatitis or liver failure, and liver tests are monitored during treatment.
  • D. Correct. Tramadol is an opioid agonist. Naltrexone is contraindicated in patients receiving opioid analgesics: it blocks the analgesia and can precipitate opioid withdrawal, so this must be resolved before naltrexone is prescribed.

Before prescribing medication for alcohol use disorder, the NP reviews every drug the patient takes. Naltrexone, an opioid antagonist, is a first-line option, but it cannot be given to a patient who uses or needs opioids: it blocks opioid analgesia and precipitates withdrawal, so a urine screen and a pain-management plan come first, and acamprosate or topiramate become alternatives if he must stay on an opioid. Acamprosate is renally cleared and avoided at a creatinine clearance of 30 mL/min or less. Mild liver test elevations are monitored, not disqualifying. Neither naltrexone nor acamprosate requires abstinence before starting, unlike disulfiram.

Sources: APA Practice Guideline: Pharmacological Treatment of Alcohol Use Disorder (2018); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed); NIAAA Core Resource: Defining How Much Alcohol Is Too Much

Question 7

A 15-month-old boy who weighs 12 kg is brought in for his scheduled MMR and varicella vaccines. For the past 3 weeks he has taken prednisone 24 mg daily for newly diagnosed nephrotic syndrome. At 11 months he had hives around his mouth after eating scrambled egg. His mother is 20 weeks pregnant. Today he has clear rhinorrhea and a temperature of 37.9°C (100.2°F) but is playful and eating well. Which finding is a contraindication to giving the MMR vaccine today?

  1. Today's mild illness with fever
  2. His mother's pregnancy
  3. His corticosteroid course for the kidney disease
  4. His reaction to egg as an infant

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Mild acute illness, with or without low-grade fever, is not a contraindication or a reason to delay vaccination. Moderate or severe acute illness is a precaution.
  • B. A pregnant household contact is not a reason to withhold MMR or varicella from a child. MMR vaccine virus is not transmitted to contacts, varicella vaccine virus spreads only rarely from a recipient who develops a rash, and vaccinating the child protects the mother.
  • C. Correct. For a 12-kg child, 24 mg daily is 2 mg/kg/day; prednisone at 2 mg/kg/day or more (or 20 mg/day or more in a child over 10 kg) for 14 days or longer is immunosuppressive, so live vaccines are deferred until at least 1 month after the steroid stops.
  • D. Egg allergy, even severe, is not a contraindication to MMR. The measles and mumps components are grown in chick embryo fibroblast culture and contain negligible egg protein.

Screening for contraindications is part of every vaccine visit. Live vaccines such as MMR and varicella are contraindicated in severe immunosuppression, and CDC counts prednisone or its equivalent at 2 mg/kg/day or more (or 20 mg/day or more in a child over 10 kg) given for 14 days or longer as high-dose therapy. This child takes 24 mg daily, which is 2 mg/kg/day, for 3 weeks. Live vaccines should wait until at least 1 month after the steroid course ends, while inactivated vaccines can still be given today. Egg allergy, a pregnant household member and mild illness are common false contraindications that lead to missed doses.

Sources: CDC Child and Adolescent Immunization Schedule Notes (2025); CDC Pink Book, Chapter 6: Vaccine Administration (2024)

Question 8

A 69-year-old man asks for "a sleeping pill." For 6 months he has woken 3 to 4 times a night and feels unrefreshed in the morning. He dozes while watching television and once nodded off at a red light. His wife reports loud snoring and pauses in his breathing. Data are: BMI 32 kg/m², neck circumference 44 cm, BP 148/88 mm Hg on amlodipine and lisinopril. Which action is MOST appropriate NEXT?

  1. Prescribe zolpidem 5 mg at bedtime for 2 weeks
  2. Begin cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia with sleep restriction
  3. Have him keep a 2-week sleep diary and return to review it
  4. Arrange testing for obstructive sleep apnea

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. The Beers Criteria advise avoiding Z-drugs in older adults because of falls, fractures and delirium, and sedative-hypnotics can worsen untreated sleep apnea.
  • B. CBT-I is first-line for chronic insomnia, but sleep restriction worsens daytime sleepiness, which is dangerous in a man already falling asleep while driving, and it leaves a likely sleep-related breathing disorder untreated.
  • C. A sleep diary is useful for insomnia, but it delays testing for a condition that has strong clinical markers and already threatens his safety on the road.
  • D. Correct. Loud snoring, witnessed apneas, daytime sleepiness, obesity, a large neck and uncontrolled hypertension give a high pretest probability of moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea, which needs polysomnography or a home sleep apnea test.

Sleep-maintenance insomnia in an older adult has many causes, and the history must screen for sleep apnea, restless legs, nocturia, pain, depression and medications before insomnia disorder is treated. This man has the classic picture of obstructive sleep apnea: snoring, witnessed apneas, excessive daytime sleepiness, obesity, a neck over 43 cm (17 inches) in a man and hypertension that stays above goal on two drugs. The AASM recommends polysomnography or home sleep apnea testing for adults with these findings. The NP should also counsel him not to drive while drowsy. If insomnia persists after the apnea is treated, CBT-I is the first-line therapy.

Sources: AASM Clinical Practice Guideline: Diagnostic Testing for Adult OSA (2017); AASM Behavioral Treatments for Insomnia (2021); AGS Beers Criteria (2023)

Question 9

A 30-year-old woman comes in after a positive home pregnancy test; her last menstrual period began 6 weeks ago. Her medication history includes methimazole 10 mg daily for Graves disease (euthyroid at her last check 2 months ago), sertraline 100 mg daily for depression that has been stable for 2 years, sumatriptan 50 mg as needed for migraine about twice a month, and clindamycin gel for acne. Which medication in her history requires the MOST prompt action?

  1. Sertraline for stable depression
  2. Sumatriptan as needed for migraine
  3. Clindamycin gel for acne
  4. Methimazole for Graves disease

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: D

  • A. Tempting because SSRIs worry many patients, but sertraline is not a major teratogen and stopping it abruptly risks relapse and discontinuation symptoms; it is usually continued.
  • B. Pregnancy registry data have not shown a rise in major malformations with sumatriptan; acetaminophen is preferred first, but occasional use does not need urgent action.
  • C. Topical clindamycin has minimal systemic absorption and is considered compatible with pregnancy; it is the opposite of an urgent concern, unlike oral isotretinoin or tetracyclines.
  • D. Correct. Methimazole exposure in gestational weeks 6 to 10 is linked to an embryopathy, so the NP acts today: same-day thyroid tests and endocrinology input on stopping it or switching to propylthiouracil for the first trimester.

A medication review at the first pregnancy visit looks for drugs whose risk depends on timing. Methimazole taken during weeks 6 to 10 of gestation is associated with aplasia cutis, choanal or esophageal atresia and abdominal wall defects. The ATA advises that a woman on methimazole who becomes pregnant have thyroid function checked promptly; if she is euthyroid on a low dose, stopping it with close monitoring can be considered, and if antithyroid treatment is still needed, propylthiouracil is preferred through the first trimester because its birth defects are less frequent and less severe. Sertraline, occasional sumatriptan and topical clindamycin are generally continued.

Sources: ATA Hyperthyroidism Guideline (2016); ATA Thyroid Disease in Pregnancy and Postpartum (2017); FDA Prescribing Information (DailyMed)

Question 10

A 76-year-old retired farmer has many rough, gritty, pink scaly papules on the scalp and the backs of both hands that he has had for years, and several brown, waxy stuck-on plaques on his back. One lesion on the back of the right hand has changed over the past 2 months. Which finding in that lesion MOST suggests progression to squamous cell carcinoma?

  1. Its location among many similar lesions on sun-exposed skin
  2. A brown stuck-on surface with small white keratin cysts
  3. Rapid growth with a firm base and tenderness
  4. A rough, sandpaper-like surface that is easier to feel than see

Show the answer and rationale

Answer: C

  • A. Many actinic keratoses in a sun-damaged field raise overall risk of squamous cell carcinoma, but this describes the field, not progression of this lesion.
  • B. A stuck-on appearance with keratin pseudocysts describes a seborrheic keratosis, which has no malignant potential.
  • C. Correct. Induration, tenderness, rapid enlargement, bleeding or ulceration in an actinic keratosis suggest invasive squamous cell carcinoma and call for biopsy.
  • D. A gritty, sandpaper texture that is felt more easily than seen is the classic feature of an actinic keratosis, not a sign of progression.

Actinic keratoses are rough, scaly papules on chronically sun-exposed skin and are precursors of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma; the risk for any single lesion is low, but patients with many lesions have a higher lifetime risk. A lesion that becomes thick, indurated, tender, larger than about 1 cm, rapidly growing, bleeding or ulcerated should be biopsied rather than treated with cryotherapy or field therapy. Seborrheic keratoses are benign, stuck-on, waxy plaques with keratin pseudocysts. The NP should also perform a full skin examination and counsel on sun protection in a patient with this degree of field damage.

Sources: AAD Guidelines of Care for the Management of Actinic Keratosis (2021); AAD Guidelines of Care for the Management of Cutaneous Squamous Cell Carcinoma (2018)
