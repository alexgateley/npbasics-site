Beta blockers (-olol)

Hypertension, angina, heart failure, rate control

PrototypesMetoprolol, atenolol (β₁-selective); propranolol (nonselective); carvedilol, labetalol (β plus α₁)
UseHypertension, angina, heart failure with reduced EF (metoprolol succinate, carvedilol), rate control
How it worksBlock β₁ receptors: slower heart rate, less contractility, lower BP
Key adverse effectsBradycardia, heart block, hypotension, fatigue, dizziness, depression; bronchospasm; heart failure can worsen while the dose is raised
Assess before givingApical pulse and BP. Hold and report a pulse under 60 or a low BP unless the order says otherwise
TeachingCheck pulse before each dose; never stop suddenly (angina, MI); carvedilol with food to limit orthostasis; report weight gain or swelling
Antidote and interactionsBlunt albuterol and can trigger severe bronchospasm in asthma; IV diltiazem and IV beta blockers are not given together
NCLEX trapsMasks hypoglycemia warning signs such as tachycardia; avoid in bronchospastic disease (nonselective agents especially); taper over 1–2 weeks

ACE inhibitors (-pril) and ARBs (-sartan)

Hypertension, heart failure, after MI

PrototypesLisinopril, enalapril, captopril (ACE inhibitors); losartan, valsartan (ARBs)
UseHypertension; heart failure and acute MI (lisinopril)
How it worksACE inhibitors stop angiotensin II forming; ARBs block its receptor: vasodilation and less aldosterone
Key adverse effectsDry cough (ACE inhibitors), angioedema, hypotension in the first days, hyperkalemia, rising creatinine
Assess before givingBP, K⁺ and creatinine; any history of angioedema (contraindicated)
TeachingReport swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat at once; report lightheadedness early on; no potassium supplements or salt substitutes unless prescribed
Antidote and interactionsRaise lithium levels; NSAIDs blunt the effect and harm the kidneys; K⁺ supplements and spironolactone add hyperkalemia; no aliskiren in diabetes
NCLEX trapsBoxed: stop as soon as pregnancy is found (fetal injury). Angioedema is more common in Black patients on ACE inhibitors. Losartan caused far less cough than lisinopril in its label trials. 36 h gap to or from sacubitril/valsartan

Calcium channel blockers (-dipine, diltiazem, verapamil)

Hypertension, angina, AF rate control, PSVT

PrototypesAmlodipine, nifedipine (dihydropyridines, act on vessels); diltiazem, verapamil (also slow the AV node)
UseHypertension and stable angina (amlodipine); AF or flutter rate control and PSVT (IV diltiazem)
How it worksBlock calcium entry into vascular and cardiac muscle: vasodilation; diltiazem and verapamil slow AV conduction
Key adverse effectsAnkle edema (amlodipine 10 mg: 10.8% vs 0.6% placebo), flushing, dizziness, palpitations; diltiazem: hypotension, bradycardia, AV block
Assess before givingBP; apical pulse and rhythm before diltiazem or verapamil
TeachingRise slowly; report ankle swelling, dizziness or a slow pulse; angina can briefly worsen when starting or raising amlodipine
Antidote and interactionsAmlodipine caps simvastatin at 20 mg; CYP3A4 inhibitors raise amlodipine; grapefruit juice does not change amlodipine exposure (label)
NCLEX trapsIV diltiazem is contraindicated in AF or flutter with WPW, 2nd or 3rd degree block or sick sinus without a pacemaker, severe hypotension, shock and VT

Digoxin (cardiac glycoside)

Heart failure and AF rate control

PrototypesDigoxin (Lanoxin)
UseMild to moderate heart failure; ventricular rate control in chronic AF
How it worksInhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase: stronger contraction, slower AV conduction
Key adverse effectsToxicity: anorexia, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, color-vision changes, any new arrhythmia
Assess before givingApical pulse for 1 full minute: hold and report under 60. K⁺ and digoxin level
TeachingTake and record the pulse daily; report nausea, vision changes or a slow or irregular pulse
Antidote and interactionsDigoxin immune fab (DigiFab) for toxicity. Amiodarone (about 70%) and verapamil (50–75%) raise levels: cut digoxin 30–50%
NCLEX trapsLow K⁺, low Mg²⁺ and high Ca²⁺ invite toxicity (watch loop and thiazide diuretics). Levels over 2 ng/mL add toxicity without benefit; under 0.5 lose effect

Nitrates (nitroglycerin, isosorbide)

Angina relief and prevention

PrototypesNitroglycerin (SL tablet, spray, patch, IV); isosorbide mononitrate
UseAcute angina and prevention before exertion
How it worksDilate veins more than arteries: less preload and myocardial oxygen demand
Key adverse effectsHeadache (dose-related, early in therapy), hypotension especially standing, dizziness; tolerance with excessive use
Assess before givingBP before each dose; chest pain rating
Teaching1 SL tablet at the first sign of pain, sitting down; repeat every 5 min, no more than 3 in 15 min; get emergency care if pain persists or differs from usual. Keep in the original glass bottle, capped tightly
Antidote and interactionsContraindicated with PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) and riociguat: severe hypotension
NCLEX trapsContraindicated in raised ICP, severe anemia, acute circulatory failure and shock. Headache is expected, not a reason to stop

Antiarrhythmics: amiodarone and adenosine

Life-threatening ventricular rhythms and PSVT

PrototypesAmiodarone (class III); adenosine
UseAmiodarone: recurrent life-threatening VF and hemodynamically unstable VT. Adenosine: converts PSVT to sinus rhythm
How it worksAmiodarone prolongs repolarization; adenosine briefly blocks the AV node (half-life under 10 s)
Key adverse effectsAmiodarone: lung toxicity (fatal in about 10% of cases), liver injury, hypo- or hyperthyroidism, bradycardia, optic neuropathy, photosensitivity, blue-gray skin. Adenosine: flushing, dyspnea, chest pressure, brief asystole
Assess before givingContinuous ECG with IV doses. Amiodarone: baseline chest X-ray, PFTs, LFTs, thyroid tests, eye exam
TeachingReport cough or breathlessness, vision changes, a slow pulse; sunscreen; no grapefruit juice or St John’s wort with amiodarone
Antidote and interactionsAmiodarone: cut warfarin by ⅓ to ½ and halve digoxin. Adenosine: dipyridamole potentiates it; caffeine and theophylline blunt it
NCLEX trapsAdenosine is 6 mg rapid IV push over 1–2 s with a fast saline flush, then 12 mg; avoid in asthma (bronchospasm)

Statins (-statin)

Lower LDL and cardiovascular risk

PrototypesAtorvastatin, simvastatin, rosuvastatin
UseHigh LDL; lower the risk of MI and stroke
How it worksBlock HMG-CoA reductase, the liver’s cholesterol-making enzyme
Key adverse effectsMuscle pain, myopathy, rarely rhabdomyolysis; raised liver enzymes; small rise in A1C and glucose
Assess before givingLipid panel; liver enzymes before starting; ask about muscle pain or weakness
TeachingReport unexplained muscle pain, tenderness or weakness promptly; simvastatin in the evening, atorvastatin any time; diet and exercise still matter
Antidote and interactionsSimvastatin: strong CYP3A4 inhibitors, gemfibrozil, cyclosporine contraindicated; max 10 mg with diltiazem or verapamil, 20 mg with amlodipine or amiodarone. Atorvastatin: no more than 1.2 L grapefruit juice a day
NCLEX trapsStop when pregnancy is recognized; no breastfeeding; contraindicated in acute liver failure or decompensated cirrhosis; avoid grapefruit juice with simvastatin

Heparin and low-molecular-weight heparin (-parin)

DVT and PE prevention and treatment, ACS

PrototypesHeparin (unfractionated); enoxaparin (LMWH)
UsePrevent and treat DVT and PE; unstable angina and MI; heparin also for AF clots, DIC, dialysis and bypass circuits
How it worksBoost antithrombin to block thrombin and factor Xa (enoxaparin acts mostly on Xa)
Key adverse effectsBleeding; HIT (falling platelets with new clots); injection-site bruising; spinal or epidural hematoma with neuraxial anesthesia (enoxaparin, boxed)
Assess before givingHeparin: aPTT (therapeutic 1.5–2 times normal), platelets, hematocrit, stool occult blood. Enoxaparin: CBC with platelets, CrCl (dose change under 30)
TeachingEnoxaparin deep SC into the abdominal wall, alternating sides; keep the air bubble in a prefilled syringe; do not rub the site; report bleeding or black stools
Antidote and interactionsProtamine: 1 mg per about 100 units of heparin (no more than 50 mg in 10 min); 1 mg per 1 mg enoxaparin given within 8 h, which neutralizes at most about 60%
NCLEX trapsCheck vial strength (fatal mix-ups); never IM; stop heparin for platelets under 100,000 or a new clot; enoxaparin is not interchangeable unit for unit, and aPTT does not monitor it

Warfarin (vitamin K antagonist)

Long-term anticoagulation monitored by INR

PrototypesWarfarin (Jantoven)
UsePrevent and treat DVT and PE; AF and valve clots; after MI
How it worksBlocks vitamin K–dependent clotting factors; no effect on a clot already formed
Key adverse effectsBleeding (boxed), skin necrosis, purple toes syndrome; fetal harm
Assess before givingINR: 2–3 for most uses, 2.5–3.5 for mechanical mitral valves; signs of bleeding
TeachingKeep vitamin K intake steady (leafy greens) rather than avoiding it; regular INR checks; report bleeding; tell dentists; ask before any new drug, herb or supplement
Antidote and interactionsVitamin K₁ (phytonadione) takes at least 1–2 h to improve the PT; PCC or plasma for urgent bleeding. NSAIDs, aspirin, SSRIs, garlic and ginkgo add bleeding; St John’s wort weakens it; antibiotics and amiodarone change INR
NCLEX trapsPeak effect takes 72–96 h, so heparin overlaps for 4–5 days until the INR is therapeutic; INR over 4 adds bleeding without benefit; contraindicated in pregnancy except some mechanical valves

Direct oral anticoagulants (-xaban, dabigatran)

AF stroke prevention, DVT and PE

PrototypesApixaban, rivaroxaban (factor Xa inhibitors); dabigatran (direct thrombin inhibitor)
UseStroke prevention in nonvalvular AF; treat and prevent DVT and PE
How it worksDirectly block factor Xa or thrombin; no INR monitoring
Key adverse effectsBleeding; spinal or epidural hematoma with neuraxial procedures (boxed); dabigatran: dyspepsia and GI upset
Assess before givingKidney function and bleeding. Apixaban drops to 2.5 mg twice daily with 2 of: age 80 or more, weight 60 kg or less, creatinine 1.5 or more
TeachingDo not stop early (boxed: clots); missed apixaban: take it the same day, never double; rivaroxaban 15 and 20 mg with the evening meal for AF
Antidote and interactionsDabigatran: idarucizumab. Apixaban and rivaroxaban: US andexanet sales ended December 2025, so follow the hospital bleeding protocol. Strong CYP3A4 and P-gp inhibitors raise levels
NCLEX trapsNot studied in prosthetic valves (apixaban); not recommended in triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome; avoid rivaroxaban for DVT or PE at CrCl under 15

Antiplatelets: aspirin and P2Y₁₂ inhibitors (-grel)

ACS, stents, recent MI or stroke, PAD

PrototypesAspirin; clopidogrel
UseACS (clopidogrel with aspirin), recent MI or stroke, peripheral artery disease
How it worksClopidogrel irreversibly blocks the platelet P2Y₁₂ ADP receptor for the platelet’s 7–10 day life; aspirin irreversibly blocks platelet COX
Key adverse effectsBleeding (most common), GI bleeding; clopidogrel: TTP (rare, can follow under 2 weeks); aspirin: ringing in the ears
Assess before givingBleeding signs, platelets, any planned procedure
TeachingDo not stop without the prescriber; tell dentists and surgeons; report unusual bleeding; stop aspirin and call if the ears ring
Antidote and interactionsAvoid omeprazole and esomeprazole with clopidogrel (less effect); NSAIDs, warfarin, SSRIs and SNRIs add bleeding; ibuprofen can blunt aspirin’s antiplatelet effect
NCLEX trapsClopidogrel is held about 5 days before surgery when possible; poor CYP2C19 metabolizers get less effect (boxed); no aspirin for children or teens with chickenpox or flu-like illness (Reye)

Thrombolytics (-teplase)

Acute ischemic stroke, MI, massive PE

PrototypesAlteplase (tPA)
UseAcute ischemic stroke; also MI and massive PE; Cathflo restores blocked central lines
How it worksConverts plasminogen to plasmin, which dissolves fibrin
Key adverse effectsBleeding, including intracranial; angioedema during and up to 2 h after the infusion
Assess before givingNo intracranial or subarachnoid hemorrhage. BP under 185/110 before and under 180/105 for 24 h after (AHA)
TeachingStroke dose 0.9 mg/kg (max 90 mg): 10% as a 1-min bolus, the rest over 60 min; no IM injections or new noncompressible punctures; compress any arterial stick at least 30 min
Antidote and interactionsAngioedema is more common with ACE inhibitors; anticoagulants and antiplatelets add bleeding
NCLEX trapsLabel stroke window is 3 h from onset; AHA guidance extends it to 4.5 h. Contraindicated after intracranial surgery or serious head trauma within 3 months, with bleeding diathesis or severe uncontrolled hypertension

Loop (-semide, -anide) and thiazide (-thiazide) diuretics

Edema and hypertension

PrototypesFurosemide, bumetanide (loop); hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone (thiazide)
UseLoop: edema in heart failure, cirrhosis and kidney disease; hypertension. Thiazide: hypertension, milder edema
How it worksLoop: block Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻ reabsorption in the loop of Henle (strong). Thiazide: block sodium reabsorption in the distal tubule
Key adverse effectsHypokalemia, hyponatremia, dehydration, orthostatic hypotension, high glucose, high uric acid and gout. Loop: ototoxicity. Thiazide: high calcium, lipids, angle-closure glaucoma
Assess before givingK⁺, Na⁺, BUN, creatinine, BP, daily weight, intake and output
TeachingStand up slowly; weigh daily; report weakness, dizziness or eye pain; sunscreen and skin checks (HCTZ)
Antidote and interactionsRaise lithium levels; low K⁺ worsens digoxin toxicity; furosemide adds aminoglycoside ototoxicity; NSAIDs blunt the effect
NCLEX trapsIV furosemide slowly over 1–2 min (high doses no faster than 4 mg/min); onset within 5 min, lasts about 2 h. Sulfonamide allergy may cross-react. Thiazides are contraindicated in anuria

Potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone)

Heart failure, hyperaldosteronism, cirrhosis edema

PrototypesSpironolactone, eplerenone; triamterene, amiloride
UseHeart failure with reduced EF (survival), add-on for hypertension, cirrhosis edema, primary hyperaldosteronism
How it worksSpironolactone blocks aldosterone in the distal tubule: loses sodium, keeps potassium
Key adverse effectsHyperkalemia; gynecomastia (most common)
Assess before givingK⁺ within 1 week of starting or a dose change, then regularly; kidney function
TeachingNo potassium supplements or salt substitutes (KCl) unless prescribed; report muscle weakness or palpitations
Antidote and interactionsACE inhibitors, ARBs, K⁺ supplements and trimethoprim add hyperkalemia; spironolactone with eplerenone is contraindicated
NCLEX trapsContraindicated in hyperkalemia and Addison disease; oral KCl with triamterene or amiloride is contraindicated

Bronchodilators: β₂ agonists (-terol) and anticholinergics (-tropium)

Rescue and maintenance in asthma and COPD

PrototypesAlbuterol (SABA, rescue); salmeterol (LABA); ipratropium (short-acting), tiotropium (long-acting anticholinergic)
UseAlbuterol: acute bronchospasm and before exercise. Salmeterol and tiotropium: maintenance (salmeterol in asthma only with an ICS; tiotropium for COPD)
How it worksβ₂ agonists relax airway smooth muscle; anticholinergics block muscarinic bronchoconstriction
Key adverse effectsAlbuterol: tachycardia, tremor, nervousness, low K⁺, paradoxical bronchospasm. Tiotropium: dry mouth, urinary retention, worse narrow-angle glaucoma
Assess before givingBreath sounds, work of breathing, SpO₂, heart rate
TeachingAlbuterol 2 puffs every 4–6 h; needing it more often means asthma is worsening. Prime a new inhaler with 4 sprays; wash the actuator weekly. Spiriva capsules are inhaled, never swallowed; keep powder out of the eyes
Antidote and interactionsBeta blockers block albuterol and can cause severe bronchospasm; no second LABA alongside salmeterol
NCLEX trapsLABA alone in asthma is contraindicated (boxed: asthma deaths); salmeterol and tiotropium are never rescue drugs

Asthma controllers: inhaled steroids (-sone, -nide) and montelukast (-lukast)

Daily maintenance, not rescue

PrototypesFluticasone, budesonide (ICS); montelukast (leukotriene receptor antagonist)
UseDaily asthma maintenance; montelukast also allergic rhinitis
How it worksICS calm airway inflammation; montelukast blocks the CysLT₁ leukotriene receptor
Key adverse effectsICS: oral thrush, throat irritation, slower growth in children, adrenal insufficiency when switched from oral steroids. Montelukast: neuropsychiatric events including suicidal thoughts (boxed)
Assess before givingAsthma control and how often the rescue inhaler is used; growth in children; mood and behavior on montelukast
TeachingRinse the mouth with water and spit after each ICS dose; full benefit can take 1–2 weeks or longer; take daily even when well; montelukast once in the evening
Antidote and interactionsSwitching from oral steroids to an ICS: adrenal insufficiency deaths have occurred, so taper slowly under supervision
NCLEX trapsNeither treats an acute attack. Montelukast for allergic rhinitis is reserved for patients who cannot use other treatments (boxed)

Insulins (by onset, peak and duration)

Rapid, short, intermediate and long acting

PrototypesRapid: lispro, aspart. Short: regular. Intermediate: NPH. Long: glargine, degludec
UseType 1 diabetes (basal plus mealtime), type 2 when needed; IV regular insulin only under close supervision
How it worksOnset / peak / duration (FDA 2026): rapid within 15 min / 1–2 h / 3–5 h; regular 30–60 min / 2–4 h / 5–8 h; NPH 1–3 h / 4–12 h / 12–18 h; long-acting no pronounced peak, about 20–24 h
Key adverse effectsHypoglycemia (most common), hypokalemia, lipodystrophy and reactions at the injection site
Assess before givingBlood glucose and the meal: is food coming? K⁺ with IV insulin
TeachingLispro within 15 min before or right after a meal; regular about 30 min before; peaks are the hypoglycemia danger times; rotate sites within one region; never share pens
Antidote and interactionsGlucose under 70 mg/dL and awake: 15 g glucose, recheck in 15 min, repeat if still low; glucagon if unable to swallow (ADA 2026). Beta blockers mask warning signs
NCLEX trapsDraw regular or lispro before NPH (clear before cloudy); only NPH mixes with them; roll and invert NPH to mix; glargine is never mixed, diluted or given IV

Non-insulin diabetes drugs (metformin, -gliflozin, -glutide, -gliptin, sulfonylureas)

Type 2 diabetes

PrototypesMetformin; glipizide (sulfonylurea); empagliflozin (SGLT2); semaglutide (GLP-1 RA); sitagliptin (DPP-4)
UseType 2 diabetes. ADA 2026: an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 RA with proven benefit for ASCVD, heart failure or CKD regardless of A1C
How it worksMetformin lowers liver glucose output; sulfonylureas release insulin; SGLT2 inhibitors spill glucose into urine; GLP-1 RAs and DPP-4 inhibitors boost glucose-dependent insulin
Key adverse effectsMetformin: diarrhea, nausea, B₁₂ deficiency, rare lactic acidosis (boxed). Sulfonylureas: hypoglycemia. SGLT2: genital yeast infection, UTI, volume loss, ketoacidosis even with glucose under 250, Fournier gangrene. GLP-1: nausea, vomiting, pancreatitis, gallbladder disease
Assess before givingGlucose and A1C; eGFR (metformin contraindicated under 30, not started at 30–45)
TeachingMetformin with meals; glipizide XL with breakfast; semaglutide shot once weekly on the same day (missed: give within 5 days); report severe abdominal pain
Antidote and interactionsHold metformin at or before iodinated contrast when eGFR is 30–60 and recheck eGFR at 48 h; stop an SGLT2 inhibitor at least 3 days before surgery; insulin or sulfonylureas with any of these raise hypoglycemia; tell anesthesia about GLP-1 RAs (aspiration)
NCLEX trapsGLP-1 RAs are contraindicated with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or MEN 2 (boxed); metformin’s hypoglycemia risk comes with insulin or a secretagogue; avoid heavy alcohol on metformin

Thyroid drugs: levothyroxine and antithyroid drugs (-azole, PTU)

Hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism

PrototypesLevothyroxine (replacement); methimazole, propylthiouracil (antithyroid)
UseLevothyroxine: hypothyroidism, usually for life. Methimazole, PTU: hyperthyroidism
How it worksLevothyroxine is synthetic T₄; methimazole and PTU block thyroid hormone synthesis
Key adverse effectsToo much levothyroxine looks like hyperthyroidism: tachycardia, arrhythmias, angina, bone loss. Antithyroid drugs: agranulocytosis, liver injury (worse with PTU), vasculitis, hypothyroidism
Assess before givingHeart rate; TSH (recheck 6–8 weeks after a levothyroxine change); CBC if fever or sore throat on antithyroid drugs
TeachingLevothyroxine on an empty stomach 30–60 min before breakfast, 4 h apart from calcium, iron and antacids; peak effect in 4–6 weeks. Antithyroid drugs: report sore throat or fever at once
Antidote and interactionsLevothyroxine strengthens warfarin and can weaken digoxin; methimazole can strengthen warfarin
NCLEX trapsLevothyroxine is not for weight loss (boxed); start low in older or cardiac patients; needs often rise in pregnancy. PTU is preferred just before and during the first trimester, methimazole after (liver risk)

Systemic corticosteroids (-sone, -solone)

Inflammation, autoimmune flares, adrenal replacement

PrototypesPrednisone, methylprednisolone, hydrocortisone, dexamethasone
UseInflammatory and autoimmune flares, asthma and COPD exacerbations, allergic reactions, adrenal insufficiency
How it worksSuppress inflammation and the immune response
Key adverse effectsHigh glucose, infection (signs masked), sodium and fluid retention, hypertension, low K⁺, osteoporosis, peptic ulcer, mood changes or psychosis, Cushingoid features, slowed growth in children
Assess before givingGlucose, BP, K⁺, weight, signs of infection
TeachingNever stop suddenly after long use: taper; report infection early; extra doses may be needed for stress such as surgery
Antidote and interactionsAvoid live vaccines on immunosuppressive doses; diabetics need more insulin or oral agents
NCLEX trapsA patient on long-term steroids who stops abruptly or faces major stress risks adrenal insufficiency; steroids hide infection

Acid suppressants: PPIs (-prazole) and H₂ blockers (-tidine)

GERD, ulcers, GI bleed prevention

PrototypesPantoprazole, omeprazole (PPIs); famotidine (H₂ blocker)
UseGERD, erosive esophagitis, peptic ulcer
How it worksPPIs block the parietal cell proton pump (H⁺/K⁺-ATPase); H₂ blockers block histamine-2 receptors
Key adverse effectsPPIs long term: C. difficile diarrhea, hip, wrist and spine fractures, low Mg²⁺, B₁₂ deficiency, interstitial nephritis, lupus. Famotidine: confusion or delirium and QT prolongation in older or kidney patients
Assess before givingEpigastric pain, stool for blood; Mg²⁺ on long-term PPIs; kidney function (famotidine dose cut at CrCl under 60)
TeachingSwallow pantoprazole tablets whole, no splitting, crushing or chewing; report diarrhea that does not stop
Antidote and interactionsOmeprazole and esomeprazole weaken clopidogrel; PPIs reduce absorption of iron and some azoles and HIV drugs; rilpivirine contraindicated; may raise methotrexate and INR
NCLEX trapsPersistent diarrhea on a PPI: think C. difficile. Long-term PPI: low Mg²⁺ can need replacement, so check it

Antiemetics: ondansetron (-setron), metoclopramide, promethazine

Nausea and vomiting, gastroparesis

PrototypesOndansetron (5-HT₃ blocker); metoclopramide (dopamine blocker, prokinetic); promethazine (phenothiazine)
UseChemotherapy and postoperative nausea (ondansetron); diabetic gastroparesis and reflux (metoclopramide)
How it worksBlock serotonin (5-HT₃) or dopamine (D₂) signals; metoclopramide also speeds gastric emptying
Key adverse effectsOndansetron: headache, constipation or diarrhea, QT prolongation, serotonin syndrome. Metoclopramide: tardive dyskinesia (boxed), EPS, akathisia, NMS, depression. Promethazine: sedation, severe tissue injury
Assess before givingQT and ECG when K⁺ or Mg²⁺ is low (ondansetron); bowel sounds (ondansetron can mask ileus); abnormal movements on metoclopramide
TeachingMetoclopramide 30 min before meals and at bedtime, no longer than 12 weeks; report restlessness or involuntary movements
Antidote and interactionsAvoid ondansetron in congenital long QT; no metoclopramide with antipsychotics (EPS); serotonergic drugs add serotonin syndrome
NCLEX trapsPromethazine: never under age 2 (fatal respiratory depression), never intra-arterial or SC; deep IM preferred; IV only at 1 mg/mL or less in a large vein. Metoclopramide is contraindicated in GI obstruction, bleeding or perforation, pheochromocytoma and seizures

Bowel drugs: lactulose, laxatives and loperamide

Hepatic encephalopathy, constipation, diarrhea

PrototypesLactulose; senna, docusate; loperamide
UseLactulose: portal-systemic (hepatic) encephalopathy. Senna, docusate: opioid constipation. Loperamide: diarrhea
How it worksLactulose acidifies the colon and traps ammonia as ammonium; loperamide slows gut motility
Key adverse effectsLactulose: gas, cramps, diarrhea; overdosing causes fluid loss, low K⁺ and high Na⁺. Loperamide above the label dose: serious heart problems or death
Assess before givingLactulose: mental status, stool count, K⁺ and Na⁺. Loperamide: blood in stool, fever
TeachingLactulose goal: 2–3 soft stools a day; in coma it can be a retention enema. Loperamide: stop and call if diarrhea lasts over 2 days
Antidote and interactionsPatients on opioids usually need a laxative such as senna or docusate along with fluids and fiber

Antidote and interactionsLive vaccines (MMR, varicella) are generally delayed 3 months after it, but postpartum rubella or MMR vaccine is not delayed
NCLEX trapsIM only for RhoGAM; never given to the newborn or to an Rh-positive mother; one 300 mcg dose covers up to 15 mL of Rh-positive red cells, larger bleeds need more

Childhood vaccines: the basics

Live versus inactivated, contraindications, sites

PrototypesLive: MMR, varicella, nasal influenza (FluMist). Most others (DTaP, hepatitis B, polio, Hib, pneumococcal, injected influenza) are inactivated
UseMMR at 12–15 months and 4–6 years (CDC 2026). Hepatitis B: AAP 2026 keeps a dose within 24 h of birth for all newborns; CDC (December 2025) made it an individual decision when the mother is HBsAg-negative; HBsAg-positive mother: vaccine plus HBIG within 12 h
How it worksTrain the immune system with weakened live organisms or inactivated parts
Key adverse effectsSoreness, redness, low fever, fussiness; rare anaphylaxis
Assess before givingSevere allergic reaction to a prior dose or component (contraindication); moderate or severe illness (delay); pregnancy or severe immunosuppression (no live vaccines)
TeachingMild illness, low fever or antibiotics are not reasons to delay; avoid pregnancy for 1 month after MMR or varicella
Antidote and interactionsLive vaccines (MMR, varicella) the same day or at least 28 days apart; immune globulin and other antibody products delay MMR and varicella
NCLEX trapsIM site: anterolateral thigh in infants (1-inch needle), deltoid from age 3; no aspiration; separate shots in one limb by 1 inch. Non-severe egg allergy is not a contraindication to injected flu vaccine

Potassium chloride (KCl)

Hypokalemia replacement

PrototypesOral KCl (extended-release); IV KCl (premixed, or concentrate diluted before use)
UseTreat and prevent hypokalemia (diuretics, digoxin)
How it worksReplaces potassium for nerve, muscle and cardiac conduction
Key adverse effectsHyperkalemia (paresthesias, weakness, arrhythmias, arrest); oral: GI ulcers and strictures; IV: pain and phlebitis
Assess before givingSerum K⁺, kidney function and urine output; ECG during IV replacement
TeachingOral: with meals and a full glass of water; do not chew; report black stools, vomiting or abdominal pain
Antidote and interactionsPotassium-sparing diuretics (oral KCl with triamterene or amiloride is contraindicated), ACE inhibitors and ARBs raise K⁺
NCLEX trapsNever IV push: undiluted concentrate can be instantly fatal. Usual IV rate no more than 10 mEq/h (up to 40 mEq/h only for severe deficits with continuous cardiac monitoring); usually no more than 200 mEq in 24 h

Iron, vitamin B₁₂, folic acid and vitamin K

Anemia, neural tube defects, newborn bleeding

PrototypesFerrous sulfate; iron dextran (IV or IM); cyanocobalamin injection; folic acid; phytonadione
UseIron deficiency anemia; pernicious anemia (B₁₂); preventing neural tube defects (folic acid); newborn bleeding prevention and warfarin reversal (vitamin K)
How it worksReplace what the body lacks for red cell production or clotting-factor synthesis
Key adverse effectsOral iron: constipation, nausea, black stools. Iron dextran: anaphylaxis (boxed). Early B₁₂ treatment: low K⁺. IV or IM vitamin K: severe, sometimes fatal reactions (boxed)
Assess before givingHemoglobin and hematocrit to track the response to iron; K⁺ in the first 48 h of B₁₂ therapy; INR with vitamin K
TeachingIron 1 h before or 2 h after meals (with food if it upsets the stomach), with vitamin C (orange juice), more fiber for constipation. Folic acid 0.4–0.8 mg daily from at least 1 month before conception. Pernicious anemia: B₁₂ injections monthly for life
Antidote and interactionsAntacids and PPIs reduce iron absorption, and iron blocks doxycycline; folic acid can hide B₁₂ deficiency while nerve damage progresses; vitamin K does not reverse heparin
NCLEX trapsIron dextran: test dose first; IM injections only by Z-track into the upper outer buttock. Oral B₁₂ is unreliable in pernicious anemia. Newborn vitamin K: 0.5–1 mg IM within 1 h of birth
